# Spatial Innovation and Structural Transformation

## Lead

The spatial innovation mechanism describes how the geographic concentration of economic activity endogenously drives technological progress and sectoral reallocation in a growing economy. Firms in each location invest in innovation — buying a probability of drawing a productivity improvement from a known distribution — while technology diffuses spatially with distance decay between periods. The key proposition is that competition for the fixed local factor (land) and the one-period spatial diffusion of technology make the dynamic innovation problem reduce to a sequence of static decisions, rendering an otherwise intractable spatial growth model solvable. As the economy grows, non-homothetic preferences shift consumption from goods to services, while the spatial concentration of manufacturing (and later services) determines which locations innovate. Technology diffusion plays a dual role: it is the channel through which agglomeration feeds back into innovation, and it is the dispersion force through which lagging regions catch up, offsetting the inherent urban bias of structural change. Transport costs enter because lower trade costs generate static gains but can reduce the density of agglomerations and slow the dynamic rate of innovation.

## Theoretical Framework: A Spatial Endogenous Growth Model

[[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]] develop a model with a continuum of locations on the unit interval, two sectors (manufacturing and services), and two factors (labor and land). Firms in each location choose how much to invest in innovation, with the cost of buying a probability $\phi$ of innovating given by $\psi(\phi) = w(\ell,t)\big(\psi_1 + \psi_2 / (1-\phi)\big)$. Conditional on innovating, firms draw a technology multiplier $z_i$ from a Pareto distribution with shape parameter $a$, so the expected technology after innovation is $E[Z_i^+] = (\phi/(a-1) + 1) Z_i^-$. Technology diffuses across space between periods with exponential decay: $Z_i^-(\ell,t) = \max_r e^{-\delta|\ell-r|} Z_i^+(r, t-1)$.

The spatial concentration of production creates a scale effect in innovation: the optimal innovation probability is increasing in local employment, consistent with evidence that a doubling of employment density raises patents per capita by roughly 20 percent (Carlino, Chatterjee, and Hunt, 2007). Land as a fixed local factor provides the congestion force that prevents complete agglomeration, while trade costs and spatial technology diffusion set the strength of the agglomeration forces. The same architecture scales to the whole world: [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] solve the model on a $1^\circ \times 1^\circ$ grid of about 65,000 cells, embedding the innovation decision in a technology-accumulation equation in which the parameter $1-\gamma_2$ governs the degree of spatial diffusion. [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] and [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] extend the framework to two final goods with an agricultural subsistence requirement and, in the latter, an endogenous transport sector whose infrastructure stock determines trade costs.

## The Static-Innovation Proposition

The central analytical result of [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]] is Proposition 1: a firm's optimal dynamic innovation decisions maximize only current-period profits. The reasoning has three parts. First, because labor is perfectly mobile and technology diffuses at the end of each period with exponential decay, the advantage a firm gains from innovating today dissipates by tomorrow — competing firms in neighboring locations have access to the same technology. Second, the land market ensures that expected profits are zero in equilibrium (firms bid for land until they break even), so the only benefit of innovation is the increase in the land rent the firm can offer in the current period. Third, because innovation draws are spatially correlated and the diffusion process is continuous, a firm's own decisions do not affect the technology it wakes up with tomorrow, since neighboring innovations will have diffused to it regardless. The same reduction holds in the global model of [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]], where the location decision is likewise static despite migration frictions, which is what allows the world economy to be simulated on a fine grid.

The contrast with other dynamic spatial approaches is instructive. [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]] model forward-looking capital accumulation by geographically immobile landlords — a genuinely dynamic problem requiring transition-path solutions rather than static choices. [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]] survey this trade-off: the static-innovation simplification avoids the "curse of dimensionality" that plagues models combining forward-looking migration and investment, while the landlord-investor approach preserves richer dynamics at the cost of greater computational complexity. The two are complementary — the static-innovation framework for long-run growth over centuries, the forward-looking framework for medium-run convergence over decades.

## Structural Transformation Through Non-Homothetic Preferences

The transformation from manufacturing to services is driven by the interaction of differential innovation rates across sectors and non-homothetic preferences. The model uses CES preferences with an elasticity of substitution below one (calibrated to 0.4, consistent with Stockman and Tesar, 1995). When manufacturing productivity grows faster than service productivity — as it does initially, because manufacturing firms have higher initial productivity and thus larger scale, making innovation more profitable — the relative price of manufactured goods falls. With an elasticity of substitution below one, this price decline shifts employment toward the sector whose relative price is rising, namely services.

This mechanism is the spatial analog of the structural transformation literature, with two crucial differences. First, the reallocation toward services is not exogenous but endogenously accelerates service-sector innovation, because the growing scale of service-producing locations makes it profitable for service firms to invest in innovation. Second, the Baumol effect — the fear that low productivity growth in services leads to long-run stagnation — fails to materialize because structural transformation itself makes the service sector concentrated enough for innovation to take off. Other specifications deliver the same income channel: [[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]] adopt the Price-Independent Generalized Linear (PIGL) class of indirect utility functions, in which the agricultural expenditure share declines with real income and asymptotes to a positive floor, while [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] and [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] use Stone–Geary preferences with a subsistence agricultural requirement, so that productivity gains in agriculture release labor into manufacturing and power early industrialization.

## Technology Diffusion as a Spatial Convergence Force

In the baseline framework, diffusion is an assumption: exponential spatial decay transmits innovations to neighbors, which is what makes the innovation decision static. The expansion of this corpus turns diffusion into a first-order determinant of regional convergence by making its strength a structural object. [[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]] model technology adoption explicitly: adoption costs depend on a region's lagged own productivity and a distance-weighted aggregate of other regions' productivity, giving a law of motion for sectoral productivity in which lagging regions catch up at a rate governed by a catch-up parameter per sector — $K_{rt} = \mu_A A_{rt-1}^{1-\lambda_A} \mathcal{F}_{rAt}^{\lambda_A}$ in agriculture and an analogous expression in manufacturing. When the catch-up parameters are positive, less productive regions grow faster — a "benefit of backwardness" producing spatial convergence; when they are zero, productivity growth is spatially uniform and the productivity distribution is stationary.

The estimated parameters are large: $\lambda_A = 0.73$ and $\lambda_M = 0.28$, implying that backward regions experienced faster productivity growth in both sectors. This is the model counterpart of the data's rural wage premium — wages in the initially most agricultural US regions grew 1.5 to 2 percentage points per year faster than in urban areas, and agricultural TFP growth alone would have raised rural wages by nearly 2% annually. A counterfactual that shuts down catch-up entirely reverses the spatial predictions of structural change — wage growth becomes urban-biased and rural industrialization collapses — establishing diffusion as the force that allowed rural America to benefit from, rather than be left behind by, the first structural transformation ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]).

> *Cross-paper pattern:* Diffusion functions as the dispersion/convergence force that offsets agglomeration and the urban bias of structural change across the whole corpus. In [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]], the degree of spatial diffusion $1-\gamma_2$ must be positive for a balanced growth path with a non-degenerate employment distribution to exist, and stronger diffusion lowers the incentive to concentrate, reducing innovation in the long run; in [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]], weaker spatial decay brings forward the services take-off and mitigates cross-location productivity differences; in [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]], the distribution of infrastructure determines how effectively lagging regions adopt technology. In every case, diffusion is the counterweight to agglomeration — a spatial convergence force.

## Spatial Concentration and the Take-Off of Services Innovation

The timing of the productivity acceleration in services is endogenous. In the calibrated model of [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]], service productivity growth remains low from 1950 until the mid-1990s, then accelerates sharply. The acceleration coincides with three related spatial phenomena: the service sector becomes increasingly concentrated, with its spatial standard deviation approaching that of manufacturing by the 2000s; competition for land between the two clusters intensifies, driving up the dispersion of land rents; and the growing scale of service-producing firms makes innovation profitable in locations previously too small to amortize fixed innovation costs.

> *Cross-pattern observation:* The positive relationship between employment density and innovation in services, documented for the late twentieth century, mirrors the pattern found by Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg (2009) for manufacturing in the early twentieth century — the link between spatial concentration and productivity growth is a general feature of sectors undergoing rapid innovation.

The collocation of manufacturing and service clusters is a distinctive prediction. Because goods are costly to transport, service-producing locations near manufacturing clusters have higher demand and thus larger scale, making them more likely to innovate; the service sector therefore clusters around the existing manufacturing belt rather than replacing it, and rising land costs eventually push manufacturing outward. The Chinese city-size evidence of [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]] — an inverted-U relationship between city size and welfare driven by agglomeration externalities versus congestion — is the same density-innovation logic operating on city size.

## Empirical Applications: From the Postwar US to the Industrial Revolution

The calibrated model of [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]] reproduces key features of the postwar US evolution: the declining manufacturing employment share, the timing and magnitude of the service productivity take-off in the mid-1990s, and the rise in the level and dispersion of land rents, with the standard deviation of log land values across US metropolitan areas rising from 0.87 in 1995 to 1.07 in 2005.

The same mechanism, with a different sectoral pair, explains the first US structural transformation. [[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]] apply the framework to 495 US commuting zones from 1880 to 1920, during which the agricultural employment share fell from 50 to 25 percent while the most agricultural regions grew fastest in wages — the empirical counterpart of the catch-up estimates above. The historical takeoff literature pushes the mechanism back to the birth of industrialization: [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] calibrates a two-sector model to England c. 1710 and reproduces the timing and location of the Industrial Revolution, with manufacturing takeoff at location 305 (Lancashire) around period 61, where the scale threshold for innovation is reached only because energy costs are low and manufacturing productivity is concentrated; flattening the productivity distribution makes takeoff immediate and everywhere, while retaining only the London peak produces no takeoff within 150 periods. [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] adds an endogenous transport sector, matching England and Wales over 1710–1881 and showing that the timing and spatial distribution of infrastructure development govern when takeoff occurs.

## Transport Costs, Agglomeration, and the Growth Trade-Off

A counterintuitive result of the framework is that higher transport costs can generate dynamic gains that offset the standard static losses. [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]] establish Proposition 3: for any level of transport costs such that aggregate productivity in an industry is stagnant, an increase in transport costs weakly increases aggregate productivity in that industry. Higher transport costs induce the lagging sector to locate more densely near the leading sector's cluster, increasing firm scale and making innovation profitable. In the model's comparative statics, lowering transport costs from $\kappa = 0.08$ to $\kappa = 0.07$ reduces the present discounted value of utility from 2.64 to 2.36, because weaker agglomeration delays the service take-off.

The historical models strengthen this finding. [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] show that exogenously higher transport costs can bring forward the date of takeoff by increasing the agglomeration forces that help firms overcome fixed innovation costs, while early infrastructure investment accelerates takeoff only if focused on locations with local demand — a spatially uniform improvement has a severely muted effect. [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] finds that inducing an industrial revolution in London would require reducing London energy prices by 56 percent, suggesting that transport infrastructure directed at the dominant population center is not a cost-effective way to induce takeoff.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Whether the static losses or dynamic gains from transport cost reductions dominate in practice remains an open empirical question. The framework implies that the net effect depends on the initial spatial distribution of activity and the strength of scale effects in innovation, and the historical evidence that lower transport costs weaken agglomeration and delay innovation reinforces the second-best logic. Direct empirical tests are scarce because transport costs and innovation outcomes are jointly determined.

## Connections to Dynamic QSE and Chinese Applications

The spatial innovation mechanism grounds several extensions in the dynamic quantitative spatial equilibrium literature surveyed by [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]], including the forward-looking capital framework of [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]] discussed above. For China, the density-innovation link is central to the city-size results of [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]] (an inverted-U between city size and welfare, with most large cities above their locally optimal size), and [[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]] extend the spatial innovation logic to data as a non-rival factor whose wider circulation amplifies the scale effects that drive innovation, providing a framework for understanding how a unified national data market could deepen the spatial division of labor in China's ongoing structural transformation. The US services-innovation concentration observed after 1995 may provide a template for innovation clusters in Chinese service sectors as the economy shifts from manufacturing-driven to service-driven growth.
