# Endogenous Infrastructure and Spatial Development

## Lead

The endogenous infrastructure mechanism describes the bidirectional interaction between transport infrastructure investment and the spatial evolution of economic activity over the course of development. In this mechanism, the location, timing, and quantity of infrastructure investment respond to the spatial concentration of production and the demand for interregional trade; in turn, reduced trade costs reshape the geography of comparative advantage, market access, and agglomeration, feeding back into further infrastructure demand. Crucially, infrastructure is both a cause and a consequence of spatial development: employment in the transport sector and investment in fixed infrastructure absorb labor and capital that could otherwise be used in production, generating a dynamic trade-off between the short-run costs and long-run growth benefits of infrastructure spending. The mechanism now connects to a broader dynamic spatial growth literature in which connectivity improvements interact with technology diffusion and labour mobility, and in which the welfare gains from infrastructure depend on the population reallocation that lower trade costs set in motion. Understanding this two-way interaction is of first-order importance for evaluating infrastructure policy in both historical and modern contexts.

## Theoretical Framework: Spatial Development with Transport Costs

The canonical framework for the interaction between transport costs and spatial development builds on the model of [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]], which features a continuum of locations, two sectors (manufacturing and services), and endogenous innovation with spatial diffusion of technology. [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] applies this framework to the first industrial revolution in England, calibrating the model with Shaw-Taylor parish-level occupational data for c. 1710 and showing that the initial spatial concentration of manufacturing productivity in the North of England was critical to whether and when an industrial takeoff occurred. [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] extends the 2014 model to incorporate a third sector -- transport and distribution -- alongside endogenous infrastructure investment, so that trade costs are no longer an exogenous iceberg parameter but instead emerge from the interaction of transport labor, fixed infrastructure stock, and the volume of goods traded across space.

The general equilibrium structure follows the standard quantitative spatial equilibrium approach surveyed in [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]], in which goods and factor markets clear across a continuum of locations, labor is perfectly mobile, and land is the fixed local factor. What distinguishes the endogenous-infrastructure extension is the explicit modeling of the transport sector as a user of labor and capital whose marginal product varies across space and over time. These dynamic growth foundations -- innovation responding to market size and technology diffusing across space -- are shared with the global model of [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]], which operates at a $1^{\circ} \times 1^{\circ}$ resolution for the whole world.

## The Transport Sector in General Equilibrium

Transport costs at a location are determined by two components: a physical cost that depends on the stock of fixed infrastructure (roads, canals, railway tracks) and a toll charged by landowner-carriers who hire transport labor to facilitate the wholesaling and distribution of goods. [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] models the transport sector's production as a constant-elasticity-of-substitution function of infrastructure stock and transport labor, where these two inputs are substitutes. The physical transport cost declines exponentially in the infrastructure stock, capturing the idea that better roads and railways reduce the "melt" of goods in transit. The toll, in turn, is set by landowners to cover the wages of transport labor, and the equilibrium toll rises with the volume of trade passing through a location but falls with better infrastructure and higher transport productivity.

A key general equilibrium constraint is that transport labor is drawn from the same labor pool as agricultural and manufacturing labor. Faster growth in output and trade increases the demand for transport services, which pulls labor out of production and can delay the point at which manufacturing firms reach the scale required to cover the fixed costs of innovation. In the baseline calibration for England and Wales, the transport sector absorbs about 8 percent of the labor force initially, and the substitutability between transport labor and infrastructure stock determines how much of this labor infrastructure improvements release back into production -- the central trade-off of the mechanism.

## Two Channels of Interaction

The endogenous interaction between infrastructure and spatial development operates through two distinct channels. The first is the *productivity channel*: growth in industrial concentration generates demand for interregional trade, creating local incentives for infrastructure improvement, which lowers trade costs, further reinforcing agglomeration and innovation through scale effects. In England and Wales, the rise of industrial hotspots in the North and the specialization of the South in agriculture meant that a greater quantity of output was traded over greater average distances, generating local demand for turnpikes, canals, and railways ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]). This channel parallels the static findings of [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]], who estimate that the U.S. interstate highway system increased welfare by 1.1 to 1.4 percent through reductions in trade costs.

The second is the *labor reallocation channel*: transport employment growth reduces the labor available for production, potentially delaying innovation take-off, whereas infrastructure that reduces the labor required to provide transport services releases labor back into production and can accelerate innovation. The net effect on the timing of take-off depends on which channel dominates. The same two-way logic operates in the commuting version of the framework: [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] show that local labor markets are integrated through commuting as well as goods trade costs, so transport improvements that reduce commuting costs reallocate workers across residences and workplaces before any innovation response occurs.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The tension between the productivity-driven demand for infrastructure and the labor reallocation cost is a robust feature of the model, but the quantitative magnitude of each channel depends on the substitutability between transport labor and infrastructure stock -- a parameter for which direct empirical estimates remain scarce.

## Historical Application: England and Wales, 1710--1881

The most detailed quantitative application is the calibration of [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]] to the Industrial Revolution in England and Wales. Using occupational data from the Shaw-Taylor and Wrigley project at three dates (1710, 1817, and 1881), mapped into a one-dimensional north-south interval, the model captures the aggregate structural transformation across primary, secondary, and tertiary sectors simultaneously with the spatial evolution of economic activity.

A striking finding is that the growth of tertiary (transport and distribution) employment occurs relatively uniformly across all locations, in contrast to the spatial concentration of agricultural specialization in the South and the manufacturing hotspot in the North. The model explains this as an equilibrium response to the increased output and trade volumes generated by the other two sectors: every location needs more transport services as more goods are produced and traded. This is consistent with the historical conjecture of Shaw-Taylor and Wrigley (2014) that most tertiary employment growth in the nineteenth century was required simply to move increased output longer average distances.

Infrastructure development proceeds endogenously through a Nash equilibrium among "counties" deciding whether to invest in infrastructure improvements, coordinated by a stylized parliamentary mechanism. The model replicates the acceleration of infrastructure supply around 1850 and the spatial pattern of improvements concentrated around the northern industrial hotspot. The location of takeoff is robust across exercises: the initially high manufacturing productivity of the North of England, together with its cheap energy, makes it the cradle of the Industrial Revolution in all counterfactuals, echoing the conclusion of [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]] that the initial spatial concentration of productivity was decisive.

## Counterfactual Policy Experiments

The endogenous infrastructure framework permits counterfactual analysis of alternative infrastructure policies. Two central findings emerge from [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]'s counterfactual exercises. First, exogenously higher transport costs can, paradoxically, *bring forward* the date of industrial take-off by increasing agglomeration forces: activity concentrates more densely, increasing firm scale and making it more likely that firms can amortize the fixed costs of innovation. This mirrors Proposition 3 of [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]], which establishes that higher transport costs can raise aggregate productivity in a stagnant sector.

Second, early infrastructure investment accelerates industrial take-off only when it is spatially targeted. In the baseline with endogenous infrastructure, takeoff occurs in 1782.7; bringing the model's endogenously generated infrastructure improvements forward by 50 years advances takeoff to 1777.6 (roughly 5 years earlier), and bringing them forward by 100 years advances takeoff by over 13 years. However, if the same total infrastructure investment is distributed uniformly across all locations rather than concentrated where demand is greatest, the effect on take-off is reversed: uniform early investment actually *delays* take-off by over 4 years. Spatially targeted improvements release labor from transport where it matters most, while leaving trade costs high elsewhere -- preserving the agglomeration benefits of costly trade -- whereas uniform improvements lower trade costs everywhere, weakening agglomeration forces without a sufficiently compensating release of transport labor.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The finding that uniform infrastructure spending has muted or even negative effects on the timing of industrial take-off is robust within the model's structure, but has not been directly tested empirically. The result highlights the importance of spatial targeting in infrastructure policy, though it also raises the challenge of identifying ex ante which locations will become industrial hotspots.

## Dynamic Growth, Technology Diffusion, and Infrastructure

The historical transport mechanism sits inside a broader dynamic spatial growth literature in which connectivity, technology diffusion, and migration jointly determine long-run development. [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] provide the canonical global version: a world economy at $1^{\circ} \times 1^{\circ}$ resolution in which firms invest in local technology, the return to which depends on the market size reachable given transport costs, and technology diffuses spatially. Transport costs delimit the market that justifies innovation -- lower trade costs expand it and raise the incentive to innovate, but also weaken the spatial concentration that agglomeration rewards. The stakes are large: raising trade costs by 40 percent reduces world real GDP by 30.1 percent and welfare by 34.2 percent, an order of magnitude larger than static one-sector trade models would predict. Connectivity improvements and migration liberalization are also substitutes: the gains from fully liberalizing migration rise from 125.8 percent to 156.9 percent of real income when trade costs are 40 percent higher.

> *Cross-pattern observation:* Across this corpus, the welfare effect of connectivity improvements depends on the spatial pattern of the improvement and on the mobility margin that responds -- labour reallocation in [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] and [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]], transport-labour release in [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]], and the narrowing of the digital divide in [[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]. No single mechanism governs the return to infrastructure; the identity of the responding margin is what determines it.

## Labour Mobility and the Welfare Margin

A second new foundation is the labour-mobility welfare margin emphasized by [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]. In a quantitative spatial model with trade costs, heterogeneous worker preferences, and a rich geography of locations, a change in transport costs -- exactly what an infrastructure improvement is -- triggers population reallocation toward locations whose market access improves most. The welfare gain from the improvement is a sufficient-statistic function of the changes in domestic trade shares *and* the changes in population, with the population term absent from the conventional immobile-labor calculation. In the model's transport-improvement exercise, the road/railroad raises common expected utility by around 9 percent, but a policy analyst who falsely assumed labor immobility and used only domestic trade shares would measure the treated locations' gain as a welfare increase of about 22 percent -- a substantial overstatement, because true expected utility is equalized across locations once population reallocation and land-price adjustment are accounted for. The reallocation effects are large relative to the welfare effect, echoing the Fogel (1964) observation that the huge population movements induced by new transport technology need not imply welfare gains of the same magnitude. This is the welfare-relevant counterpart of the two channels in [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]: the labour released (or attracted) by infrastructure is not a friction to be minimized but a margin through which gains are transmitted. The same logic underlies the commuting-based counterfactuals of [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] and the general equilibrium treatment surveyed by [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]].

## Modern Extension: Digital Infrastructure

The logic of endogenous infrastructure extends beyond physical transport to digital networks. [[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]] develop a quantitative spatial equilibrium model in which broadband and mobile base stations reduce the information and search costs that function as trade costs in the modern economy. Their estimates indicate that China's network infrastructure construction between 2012 and 2019 generated approximately 3.26 percent welfare gains, with the narrowing of the digital divide across regions serving as a key mechanism (the precise figure is partially illegible in the OCR-garbled raw text, but the magnitude is consistent across the paper's summary and tables).

The parallel to the historical transport mechanism is instructive. Just as turnpikes and canals lowered the cost of moving goods in eighteenth-century England, network infrastructure lowers the cost of moving information in twenty-first-century China. In both cases, the productivity-enhancing effect is concentrated in regions where economic activity is already growing, while the narrowing of infrastructure gaps between regions serves as a convergence mechanism. Both studies also find that aggregate welfare effects depend on the spatial pattern of improvements, not merely their total magnitude.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The parallel between transport and digital infrastructure is compelling at the theoretical level, but direct empirical evidence on the degree of similarity in the underlying mechanisms -- particularly the labor reallocation channel for digital networks -- remains limited.

## Empirical Signatures

The endogenous infrastructure mechanism implies three empirical signatures that distinguish it from models with exogenous trade costs. First, the transport employment share rises during periods of rapid spatial transformation, reflecting the growing demand for interregional trade as production becomes more spatially concentrated -- evident in the rising tertiary employment share in the England and Wales data ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]) and, by analogy, in the growth of the information and communications technology sector in China ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

Second, infrastructure investment is concentrated in regions with growing industrial output. In the England and Wales model, improvements cluster around the northern industrial hotspot, declining where output is stagnant -- consistent with the historical record of local financing for turnpikes, canals, and railways in precisely the regions where industrial demand emerged.

Third, trade costs decline fastest in regions experiencing both output growth and infrastructure investment, generating a negative correlation between the rate of trade-cost decline and initial income or productivity levels. [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]] find that geographic location explains at least 20 percent of the spatial variation in U.S. incomes, but in a static framework without endogenous infrastructure. The endogenous mechanism suggests this share could evolve over the course of development, and the dynamic global model of [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] shows how the density-income correlation itself reverses as connectivity, diffusion, and migration play out over centuries.

## Open Questions

Three open questions delimit the mechanism's current reach. First, embedding endogenous infrastructure in a dynamic quantitative spatial model with capital accumulation -- of the kind developed by [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]] -- would let infrastructure investment compete with private capital for savings, a margin the current Trew framework (in which infrastructure companies maximize one-period dividends) abstracts from. Second, identification of the substitution parameter between transport labor and infrastructure stock remains unresolved, and it is the single parameter that determines whether the productivity or the labor-reallocation channel dominates ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]). Third, whether the historical transport mechanism generalizes to digital networks is open: the digital case studied by [[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]] lowers search and information costs rather than physical transport costs, and the welfare margin that responds is the digital divide rather than transport labor, so the two mechanisms may diverge in precisely the ways the cross-pattern callout above highlights.
