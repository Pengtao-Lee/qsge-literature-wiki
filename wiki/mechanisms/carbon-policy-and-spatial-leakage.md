Carbon-policy spatial leakage is the mechanism by which a carbon-control constraint imposed on one set of regions shifts emissions and economic activity to other, less-constrained regions, weakening the policy's environmental effectiveness and redistributing its economic costs. In a quantitative spatial general-equilibrium framework, a regional carbon constraint raises local unit production costs (or forces output contraction), and this shock travels to every other region through two endogenous channels rather than fixed input-output shares: trade linkages (intermediate-goods prices and total-demand shocks) and labor migration (workers relocate toward higher-productivity, less-constrained regions). Luo and Luo's quantitative spatial model of China's carbon-control transition is the anchor study: leakage averages about 49.7 percent of a controlling region's abatement, non-controlling regions' GDP gains offset about 38 percent of its GDP loss, and command-style instruments produce welfare losses two to three times those of carbon-pricing tools because they melt the carbon revenue into deadweight loss.

## The policy setting: from energy dual-control to carbon dual-control

The mechanism is studied against China's institutional transition from energy dual-control to carbon dual-control. From the Eleventh through the Thirteenth Five-Year Plans, China built targets on energy intensity, carbon intensity, and total energy consumption; the Thirteenth Plan's energy dual-control encountered divergent regional compliance, and the 20th Party Congress and the Third Plenum of the 20th Central Committee shifted the regime to carbon dual-control, with a 2024 State Council timetable moving from carbon budgets to rigid total caps ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). The transition matters for the mechanism because energy control is a blunter instrument than carbon control: it hits renewable and fossil energy alike and suppresses the renewable substitution that carbon control rewards.

The analytical premise is that regions are not isolated islands: a constraint in one region changes production costs and competitiveness everywhere, so a target decomposed region by region cannot be evaluated region by region ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). This is the same general-equilibrium logic that motivates unified-national-market analysis ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]) and the dynamic spatial framework's carbon application ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

## The model architecture

The model builds on the Eaton-Kortum trade structure with Fréchet-distributed productivity, adding energy as a produced input in a multi-level nested CES technology: labor, energy, and intermediates at the top; fossil and renewable energy in the middle; and coal, oil, and gas within fossil energy ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). A carbon or energy constraint enters as a policy wedge on each energy type's actual cost — proportional to its carbon coefficient under carbon control, its coal-equivalent coefficient under energy control — so the constraint's shadow price is endogenous. Households have heterogeneous location preferences and migrate subject to hukou-related frictions, modeled as differential access to local public services, and the economy is solved in exact-hat changes following standard quantitative spatial practice ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The economy has N−1 domestic regions plus a foreign region; domestic workers migrate across regions but not across the border.

Two parameter choices connect the model to the corpus: migration elasticity $\kappa = 1.5$ and trade elasticity $\theta = 4$, estimated for China by [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. Renewable energy is supplied regionally with an elasticity of 0.6 in the baseline, while fossil energy is priced internationally. Under pure command control the deadweight-loss rate is set to unity, so the carbon revenue disappears from the economic circuit rather than being rebated — the modelling choice that generates the central instrument-comparison result.

## The trade channel: constraint-cost versus output-reduction effects

A carbon constraint acts on a region's unit production cost through two opposing forces. The constraint-cost effect raises the price of fossil energy and pushes unit costs up; but the higher energy cost makes the pre-existing output scale unprofitable, output contracts, and reduced factor demand pushes factor prices down — the output-reduction effect — pulling unit costs back ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). In most regions the constraint-cost effect dominates and unit costs rise; in a few — Shandong, Hubei, and Sichuan in the simulations — the output-reduction effect wins, and which effect wins depends on factor-supply elasticities and, for labor, on the migration response.

These local unit-cost changes transmit to other regions through intermediate-goods prices and total demand. Rising controlling-region unit costs make its intermediate goods more expensive for importers, pushing their unit costs up (trade attenuation); falling unit costs give importers a relative competitiveness advantage (trade compensation). Independently, the controlling region's output contraction lowers the total demand its trading partners face, transmitting a deflationary impulse through the trade network ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). On average the intermediate-price effect dominates — carbon control in any region raises non-controlling regions' unit costs — and the transmission's strength is governed by the internal trade costs and elasticities that [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]] estimates for China's interprovincial economy.

## The migration channel: GDP–welfare separation

The second transmission channel runs through population. Carbon control lowers income in the constrained region, widening the gap between expected income there and elsewhere, and households migrate out — both the share of local residents staying and the share of outsiders moving in fall under any region's carbon control ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). This migration response produces a striking GDP–welfare separation. Because people move toward higher-productivity, less-constrained regions, aggregate efficiency can *rise*: non-controlling regions' GDP gains offset, on average, about 38 percent of the controlling region's GDP loss, and for a few regions the spillover is large enough that national GDP rises even as the controlling region loses output. But welfare falls everywhere: residents leave not because expected income elsewhere improved, but because the constraint reduced local income — a forced relocation in which migrants pay migration costs and arrive with lower welfare. Migration costs of the kind [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]] estimates for China are precisely what make this forced mobility welfare-reducing, and the result argues for coupling carbon policy with hukou-reform and labor-market measures that lower relocation costs.

## Carbon leakage and abatement effectiveness

Carbon leakage is the flip side of the transmission channels: the emissions the constrained region avoids reappear elsewhere. Luo and Luo decompose leakage into a domestic component — emissions increases in non-controlling Chinese regions — and a foreign component, both measured relative to the controlling region's initial emissions ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). Averaged across regions, a single region's 1 percent carbon control generates leakage equal to about 49.71 percent of its abatement, meaning the effective reduction is roughly halved; in Qinghai and Ningxia leakage fully offsets the local reduction. Leakage operates through both margins above — output reallocation that changes fossil-energy use elsewhere, and factor-price changes that alter the fossil-to-renewable mix in non-controlling regions.

A systematic feature is that foreign leakage exceeds domestic leakage. Because domestic workers can migrate to non-controlling regions, their increased labor supply lowers the relative cost of labor there and pushes firms to substitute labor for fossil energy, dampening domestic leakage; with the border closed, no such substitution occurs abroad, so the foreign component is larger ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). At the national level, all four instruments leak more than 20 percent of the domestic reduction abroad — actual abatement is only about 80 percent of the target — directly limiting China's mitigation contribution and trade competitiveness, echoing the cross-regional cost-shifting logic documented for investment and trade distortions by [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *This mechanism currently rests on a single paper (Luo–Luo 2025), and its magnitudes are point estimates with data caveats — the raw markdown is an OCR-degraded MinerU-VLM conversion in which some figure values are not legible, and the 49.71 percent and 38 percent figures are averages across many heterogeneous regional simulations. The qualitative transmission structure (trade cost pass-through plus forced migration) is robust to the parameter ranges reported, but the leakage numbers await independent replication.*

## Instrument comparison and the deadweight-loss wedge

The four instruments differ not in how much carbon they remove — each is calibrated to a national 1 percent reduction — but in what happens to the carbon revenue. Under a carbon market or carbon tax, the compliance cost becomes revenue that stays inside the economic circuit; under command control, the carbon revenue "melts," producing deadweight loss that disappears from the system and is amplified through the input-output multiplier ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). The counterfactual table is stark: the carbon market and carbon tax both cost 0.042 percent of GDP and 0.046 percent of welfare; command carbon control costs 0.045 percent of GDP but 0.114 percent of welfare; command energy control costs 0.046 percent of GDP and 0.116 percent of welfare. Command instruments thus deliver roughly two to three times the welfare loss of carbon-pricing instruments at nearly the same GDP cost — the gap is the melted revenue, plus the weakened substitution away from fossil energy that energy control fails to reward.

The instruments also differ in incidence. Carbon-market revenue is transferred toward regions with low marginal abatement costs, which are disproportionately the regions with lighter industry and lower energy dependence; this cross-regional revenue transfer tends to widen regional gaps, and the simulated Gini increase is slightly larger under the carbon market (0.024) than under the carbon tax (0.023) and well below the command-control values (0.032) ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). This is the spatial-efficiency-versus-spatial-equity tension in its fiscal form, analogous to the regional-gap effects of fiscal-expenditure equalization studied by [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] and to the incentive-compatibility problems [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]] identifies when national-market reform redistributes resources across localities.

## Policy translation: carbon control in the Chinese spatial equilibrium

The policy translation from this mechanism is threefold. First, the *dual-control transition itself* is a mechanism change: moving from energy control to carbon control shifts the wedge from the coal-equivalent coefficient to the carbon coefficient, which rewards renewable energy and removes the energy-control deadweight loss — a move from the command-energy column of the instrument table toward the market columns ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). Second, *why spatial general equilibrium rather than exogenous input-output shares*: the Lucas-critique argument for quantitative spatial models generally ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]) applies with special force, because a carbon constraint changes the structural parameters — energy shares, factor prices, trade flows — that fixed-coefficient approaches treat as given. Third, *market-based instruments with revenue redistribution*: the paper recommends expanding the national carbon market, using some carbon revenue as a reverse-adjustment fund for regional coordination, and pairing carbon policy with hukou and unified-market reforms that lower migration costs ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]).

The equity–efficiency tension connects the mechanism to the wider corpus. Smoother population mobility reduces the GDP and welfare losses from carbon control and roughly stabilizes leakage, while deeper trade linkages amplify the shock; higher renewable-supply elasticity and renewable-fossil substitution lower the losses — the paper's "事缓则圆" (steady-as-she-goes) logic that a gradual, elastic transition dominates a sharp one ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). This is the environmental-policy instance of the corpus-wide finding that market-based instruments dominate command instruments in the Chinese spatial economy ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]), and the policy domain the dynamic spatial framework singles out for carbon compensation and trading ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *Across [[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]], [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]], and [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]], the strongest environmental-policy conclusion in the corpus is that market-based instruments with explicit revenue handling dominate command-and-control instruments — command tools melt the scarcity rent into deadweight loss while pricing tools keep it circulating. This is the environmental mirror of the unified-national-market finding that replacing administrative allocation with market allocation raises efficiency.*
