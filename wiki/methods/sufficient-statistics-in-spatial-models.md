# Sufficient Statistics in Spatial Models

A sufficient statistic in quantitative spatial economics is a low-dimensional observable, or combination of observables, that fully summarises a counterfactual's welfare or treatment effect, so that computing the full general-equilibrium re-solution is unnecessary. The canonical example is the home-trade-share formula: in the Eaton–Kortum class of trade models the welfare gain from a trade-cost change is $\hat{W} = \hat{\pi}_{nn}^{-1/\theta}$, a function only of the change in the domestic expenditure share and the trade elasticity. Redding (2016) extends the logic to models with labour mobility, where the sufficient statistic becomes the change in domestic trade shares *plus* the reallocation of population across locations. Tsivanidis (2026) shows that an envelope-theorem sufficient statistic — the first-order value-of-travel-time-savings measure — approximates the full general-equilibrium welfare effect of a transit investment. This page covers what makes a statistic sufficient, when the shortcut is exact, and when it misleads.

## What Makes a Statistic Sufficient

The shortcut works because of a separation between the object of interest and the mechanism that produces it. In the quantitative spatial class of models reviewed by [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]], the welfare effect of a policy that changes trade costs can be expressed solely in terms of empirically observable sufficient statistics, even though the full equilibrium involves the entire vector of wages, populations, trade shares, and price indices across all locations. The sufficient statistics are functions of the initial equilibrium, so they can be measured without solving the counterfactual. [[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]] supplies the microfoundation: a Fréchet distribution of technology with shape parameter $\theta$ generates a gravity equation for bilateral trade and a price index that depends on the domestic technology and trade share, which is what makes the domestic share a sufficient statistic for welfare. [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]] shows that the same property survives the introduction of labour mobility, at the cost of adding a second statistic for population reallocation. The role of the initial equilibrium is the same as in [[exact-hat-algebra]] — counterfactual outcomes are computed from observed initial values plus an assumed change in primitives, and the sufficient statistic is the collapsed version of that computation for the welfare object. Not every outcome admits a sufficient statistic; in general it is aggregate welfare, not the distribution of economic activity, that can be summarised by a low-dimensional object, and [[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]] shows that in multi-sector economies even welfare requires the sector-level breakdown rather than a single number.

## The Canonical Sufficient Statistic: Home-Trade-Share Welfare

In the Eaton–Kortum model, country $n$'s expenditure share on its own goods is $\pi_{nn} = T_n c_n^{-\theta} / \Phi_n$ with $\Phi_n = \sum_i T_i (c_i d_{ni})^{-\theta}$, and the exact price index is $P_n = \gamma \Phi_n^{-1/\theta}$, so the welfare gain from any trade-cost change that leaves technology unchanged is the ratio of counterfactual to initial domestic expenditure shares raised to the power of the inverse trade elasticity,

$$\hat{W}_n = \hat{\pi}_{nn}^{-1/\theta}.$$

The same formula holds across the class of models identified by the literature: Armington differentiation by origin, Eaton–Kortum Ricardian competition, and Melitz with a Pareto productivity distribution all deliver it, because they share the gravity structure and the constant trade elasticity ([[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Applied work operationalises the formula directly, as [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]] do for Chinese internal and international trade liberalisation, where the welfare consequences of measured cost changes are evaluated through exactly such trade-share statistics. [[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]] emphasises what happens outside that class: in a multi-sector model with input–output linkages, a single aggregate elasticity is insufficient, and welfare must be computed from sector-level domestic shares and sector-specific trade elasticities, with the change in welfare decomposed into terms-of-trade and volume-of-trade effects. The formula is therefore not universal — it is conditional on the gravity class and on the elasticity being the correct structural object for the model at hand.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The home-trade-share formula is the most-used welfare number in this corpus — it underlies the headline gains reported in the trade and migration synthesis pages — and its validity everywhere hinges on the model being inside the gravity class with a single trade elasticity.

## Beyond the Static Formula: Factor Mobility

When labour is mobile across locations, the pure domestic-share formula fails because population reallocation itself carries welfare consequences through the price of the immobile factor, land. [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]] derives the factor-mobility generalisation: with heterogeneous worker preferences, expected utility conditional on living in a location is equalised across locations while real incomes differ, and the common welfare gain between an autarky equilibrium ($A$) and a trade equilibrium ($T$) is

$$\frac{\bar{U}_n^T}{\bar{U}_n^A} = \left(\frac{1}{\pi_{nn}^T}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\theta}} \left(\frac{L_n^A}{L_n^T}\right)^{\frac{1}{\epsilon} + (1-\alpha)},$$

where $\pi_{nn}^T$ is the open-economy domestic trade share, $L_n^A$ and $L_n^T$ are populations in the closed and open economies, $\theta$ the trade elasticity, $\epsilon$ the labour-supply elasticity, and $\alpha$ the consumption share. The domestic trade share and the population reallocation are together sufficient statistics for welfare; measuring welfare from the domestic share alone, without controlling for the population change, produces errors of the same order of magnitude as the true welfare gains. The [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]] survey writes the same result for its new economic geography example as $\hat{\bar{V}} = (\pi_{nn}^0/\pi_{nn}^1)^{\alpha/(\sigma-1)}(\lambda_n^0/\lambda_n^1)^{(\sigma(1-\alpha)-1)/(\sigma-1)}$, a direct analogue of the international-trade result, with the population term added because land is an immobile factor. Applied work shows the magnitude: [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]] finds that reducing China's migration costs so that one-third of workers migrate across provinces raises real GDP per worker by 12.8% and welfare by 45.6%, numbers computable because the model's welfare change is summarised by migration shares and real-income changes. [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] documents the commuting dimension of the same logic.

## Envelope-Theorem Sufficient Statistics in Urban Settings

In urban models the sufficient-statistics logic takes an envelope-theorem form. [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]] shows, first, that the change in the entire distribution of economic activity across a city in response to a transit improvement depends only on the change in two commuting-market-access terms and a structural residual of exogenous location fundamentals — the reduced-form elasticities of outcomes to commuting market access, together with the commuting elasticity and the change in commute costs, are sufficient statistics for the relative changes in activity. Second, Proposition 2 applies the envelope theorem: when the equilibrium is efficient, the first-order welfare impact of a change in infrastructure is precisely the value of travel time savings, the standard partial-equilibrium measure. This result builds directly on the commuting-gravity structure that [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]] and [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] established, and that [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]] surveys as a central building block of quantitative urban models. In Bogotá, the first-order travel-time-savings approach delivers a welfare gain of 1.25%, whereas the full sufficient-statistics implementation yields 2.39%; the travel-time measure thus accounts for only about 52% of the total gain, and of the gap between the two, roughly three-quarters is attributable to amenity and productivity externalities and one-quarter to approximation error. When the full multi-group model is estimated, average welfare effects of 1.93%–2.41% bracket the 2.39% sufficient-statistics number, so the aggregate shortcut performs well for total welfare.

*Across the corpus, aggregate sufficient statistics are accurate for total welfare but systematically silent on who wins and who loses — the distributional layer always requires re-solving the full model.*

## Aggregate Versus Local Effects

The same shortcut rarely works for local or distributional objects. [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]] shows that at the regional level, using each region's domestic trade share without controlling for its population change produces discrepancies from the true welfare gain of roughly the same magnitude as the gain itself, whereas aggregating to the national level and using the country-level domestic share gives a much better approximation, because both the true common gain and the measured country-level gain are weighted averages of region characteristics. [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]] makes the general point that the welfare statistic summarises the *common* change in utility while the reallocation of activity across locations — the object of most policy interest for individual places — is not summarised by any single scalar. [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] documents the empirical counterpart for local employment elasticities: the general-equilibrium elasticity of employment with respect to a local productivity shock varies from about 0.5 to 2.5 across US counties, so a single average elasticity, or a sufficient statistic derived from it, is misleading when applied to any individual county, and the heterogeneity is not captured by standard controls. [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]] is explicit that its sufficient-statistics approach speaks to aggregate effects and is silent on the distribution of impacts across worker groups, which is why the full model must be estimated for distributional questions. A researcher who needs the welfare of a particular group, or the reallocation of population and wages across locations, must re-solve rather than use the scalar shortcut.

## Structural Assumptions and the Limits of the Shortcut

What the sufficient statistic hides is exactly the structure that generates it. The number $\hat{\pi}_{nn}^{-1/\theta}$ is a function of the assumed trade elasticity, and different elasticity values — or different models — imply different welfare numbers from the same trade-share change. [[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]] is the canonical caution: once sectors have different elasticities, a single statistic is not merely imprecise but conceptually wrong, and the welfare calculation requires the full set of sectoral trade elasticities, which are rejected as equal by the data. [[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]] shows that $\theta$ governs the strength of comparative advantage, so the welfare number inherits the uncertainty in $\theta$; the elasticity is estimated, not given. The applied literature consequently treats the sufficient-statistic magnitude as conditional: [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]] reports welfare gains that depend on estimated migration and trade costs and on the Fréchet parameter, and [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]] notes that the same trade-share change delivers different welfare effects in constant- versus increasing-returns models once population is mobile. The practical risk is mistaking the sufficient statistic for a structural elasticity or a policy lever — the statistic summarises welfare under the assumed model but is not itself a parameter that can be moved.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The persistent pitfall in applied work is to treat a sufficient statistic as if it were a structural or policy elasticity; the correct reading is that the statistic is a function of the model's elasticity and inherits all of its identifying assumptions.

## Dynamic Sufficient Statistics

The static logic generalises to dynamic settings, at the cost of additional state variables. [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]] develops a dynamic spatial model with forward-looking migration and capital accumulation in which the welfare consequences of a shock are the present value of utility along the transition path, not a static comparison of steady states; the model preserves a form of exact-hat computation for counterfactuals and characterises the transition path analytically by spectral methods, so that dynamic welfare can be expressed in terms of the initial distribution and the change in fundamentals rather than a full simulation. [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] similarly evaluates welfare as discounted lifetime utility $\sum_t \beta^t u_t^i$, with a balanced-growth path in which welfare and real consumption grow at a common rate that depends on the spatial distribution of employment. [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]] supplies the bridge from the static result, since its sufficient statistic is derived from an initial-equilibrium condition that dynamic models inherit period by period. [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]] situates these dynamic extensions as the natural generalisation of the static toolkit, where the sufficient statistic is no longer a single number but the distribution of discounted welfare responses.

## Open Questions

Several boundaries of the sufficient-statistics method remain open. The known results cover the gravity class with CES demand and a single immobile factor; extensions to firm entry, capital, and non-CES demand do not in general admit the same low-dimensional summary, as the multi-sector results of [[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]] and the dynamic models of [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]] and [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]] illustrate. Whether a statistic is robust across model classes is a separate question: [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]] shows that the relative counterfactual impacts are invariant across a family of urban models that share a gravity equation for commuting, but the *level* of aggregate welfare depends on structural parameters that must be calibrated. Finally, the interface between sufficient statistics and estimation is unresolved — the statistic is only as good as the elasticity that enters it, so the gains from the shortcut in computation are paid back in sensitivity to estimated parameters.
