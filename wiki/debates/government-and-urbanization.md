# Government and Urbanization

The question of whether governments should restrict the growth of large cities through migration controls is one of the longest-standing debates in urban economics, and it has become acutely urgent for China, where the hukou (household registration) system governs the spatial distribution of 1.4 billion people. One position holds that because individual migrants ignore the congestion costs, environmental degradation, and pressure on public services they impose on incumbents, equilibrium city sizes exceed the social optimum, making migration restrictions a welfare-improving Pigouvian intervention. The opposing position contends that the welfare losses from preventing workers from reaching high-productivity locations far exceed any gains from congestion correction, and that cities with strong fundamentals should be allowed to grow beyond their locally optimal size from a national perspective. A growing body of quantitative spatial equilibrium (QSE) evidence, anchored on China's hukou system, now allows the debate to be adjudicated empirically — and the "government" in the debate now spans migration controls, centrally rationed land-supply quotas, fiscal equalization, and unified-market reform, each with different efficiency and equity properties.

## Theoretical foundations: optimal city size in the presence of externalities

The intellectual foundation is the Henderson (1974) model of a system of cities, in which agglomeration economies and congestion diseconomies jointly produce an inverted-U relationship between city size and average local welfare. Each city has a locally optimal size determined by the balance of these forces. Because both agglomeration and congestion are external to individual migration decisions, the equilibrium allocation generally deviates from both local and national optima. A central planner maximizing national welfare would let cities with better fundamentals surpass their local optima, because concentrating population in the most productive locations raises aggregate output [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. This generates the core tension: locally optimal sizes are systematically smaller than nationally optimal sizes for high-productivity cities, and a city government that restricts in-migration to protect locals moves closer to the local optimum but farther from the national optimum. The same tension appears in interregional land allocation: because construction-land quotas are rationed centrally, the state chooses not only how many people cities hold but how much land they get, and the two choices jointly determine city sizes [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

## Position A: migration restrictions correct for urban externalities

The case for controlling city sizes rests on the Pigouvian insight that migrants do not internalize the costs they impose on existing residents. Density raises housing prices, congests infrastructure, strains schools and hospitals, and degrades the environment; without a fiscal mechanism to charge migrants for these costs, cities may grow beyond their locally optimal size.

Wu and You (2025) provide direct support. Their structural estimates show that most large Chinese cities have surpassed their locally optimal sizes: 83.3% of tier-1 cities and 88.9% of tier-2 cities are locally oversized, and relaxing hukou restrictions there would reduce the welfare of the "representative resident" by 0.127–0.155% per period. Ma and Tang (2020) report a sharper version: Shanghai's migration barriers are 24% above the national average, yet removing them entirely would reduce local welfare by 8.3%, because the congestion from 6 million additional migrants would dominate the wage gains [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]].

The key mechanism is the inelastic supply of urban land. As cities exhaust developable land, housing supply becomes increasingly inelastic, raising prices and reducing incumbents' real incomes; restricting in-migration is then a second-best policy that protects locals when land markets cannot adjust [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. The logic extends to the interregional land quota: compressing eastern construction supply is itself a size-restricting instrument, and because cities are locally oversized, restricting them through land rationing can raise local average welfare [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Fiscal equalization offers a less distortionary corrective: equalizing productive expenditure raises output by 1.505% and welfare by 1.555%, and equalizing social-security expenditure raises welfare by 2.693% [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]].

## Position B: mobility frictions dominate externality correction

The opposing view holds that the first-order distortion is not the congestion externality but the barrier to labor mobility itself. The headline result from Wu and You (2025) is that the hukou system creates a GDP loss of 13.6% and a welfare loss of 4.7% averaged over 2000–2020, and that counterfactually removing all restrictions would make it almost always welfare-reducing to restrict any individual city, because the gains from mobility dominate the costs of allowing already-oversized cities to grow further. Gains would come from reallocating workers from less to more productive cities and from better matches between workers' idiosyncratic productivities and urban labor markets.

Tombe and Zhu (2019) reinforce this from a complementary Eaton-Kortum framework: the migration-cost reductions of 2000–2005 raised aggregate labor productivity by 4.8% and welfare by 11.1%, and further liberalization to a one-third inter-provincial migrant share (comparable to U.S. rates) would increase real GDP per worker by 12.8% and welfare by 45.6% [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) add a subtlety: migration liberalization and within-prefecture firm-level friction reduction are substitutes for aggregate welfare but have opposite distributional consequences — migration liberalization worsens spatial inequality, while friction reduction shrinks it [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]. The land-quota dimension reinforces Position B: the reasoning that large cities are nationally undersized implies that rationing their construction land — the land-supply regional tilt — is an additional mobility friction suppressing the most productive locations' growth [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

> ***Cross-pattern finding**: Across the QSE literature on China, the debate is increasingly framed not as "should we control city sizes or not" but as "what form of intervention best reconciles local and national welfare."*

## Where the two positions agree

Despite their disagreement on the net welfare effect, the positions converge on several points. Both recognize that large Chinese cities have surpassed their locally optimal sizes — this is robust across the Wu–You and Ma–Tang frameworks. Both agree that migration liberalization imposes genuine distributional costs on natives in desirable cities, even if national gains are positive. Third, Ma and Tang (2020) show that the largest cities (notably Shanghai) may exceed even the national optimum, so some restriction could in principle be welfare-improving at both levels for specific cities, though these are exceptions. Fourth, the fiscal and land evidence reinforces a shared premise: because cities are locally oversized but nationally undersized, some intervention is warranted, and the real question is which instrument — migration control, land rationing, fiscal equalization, or market integration — does the work with the least collateral damage [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The empirical evidence now strongly favors Position B at the national level — the welfare costs of mobility frictions dominate externality-correction benefits — but with important caveats about distributional effects and city-specific exceptions. The debate has shifted from whether intervention is needed to what form it should take.

## Empirical anchors

The empirical resolution depends on three structural parameters estimated by Wu and You (2025): the agglomeration elasticity (φ ≈ 0.10, estimated from Chinese microdata with historical city size as an instrument), the congestion elasticity (governed by housing-supply elasticities derived from satellite data on terrain ruggedness and land cover), and the fiscal wedge created by hukou policy (estimated at 50.7% of destination wage in 2000 via revealed preference, declining to 15.0% by 2010). These place China's agglomeration elasticity at the upper end of the international range of 0.04–0.07.

Ma and Tang (2020) provide complementary Melitz-style evidence: migration inflows raise local welfare in all destination cities except Shanghai, a result that depends critically on the extensive margin of firm entry, which expands variety and lowers prices and quantitatively dominates congestion. Tombe and Zhu (2019) estimate the income elasticity of migration (κ ≈ 1.5) and the trade elasticity (θ ≈ 4) within the international range, and document that migration costs declined 18% on average between 2000 and 2005, with between-province costs falling nearly 40%. At the interregional level, Zhao and Chen calibrate a QSE model with land quotas and find that the actual 2013–2017 land allocation raised GDP by 2.31% and welfare by 2.57% while cutting the per-capita gap by 0.73% — the quantitative backdrop against which city-size policy is now evaluated [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

## Distributional effects: who wins and who loses

A crucial dimension that neither position fully resolves is the distribution of gains and losses. Wu and You (2025) decompose welfare effects on natives, migrants, and a "representative" resident: under free migration, natives in tier-1 cities would lose 10.6–17.0% depending on skill, driven primarily by crowding in local publicly-provided private goods (chiefly education and healthcare), while migrants would gain 5.4–8.3%. This creates a political-economy barrier to reform: winners are diffuse, losers are concentrated and politically influential. The skill-based structure of hukou granting — in tier-1 cities in 2010, only 3.3% of primary-schooled migrants received local hukou versus 37.3% of college graduates — further skews outcomes toward protecting high-skilled incumbents [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Skill heterogeneity also shapes the target allocation: with cross-skill productivity and public-service spillovers, redistributing high-skill workers toward smaller cities and low-skill workers toward larger ones moves the economy toward the Pareto frontier, which is why transfer and property-tax design matter for who ultimately wins [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]].

## Land-supply policy as an instrument of urbanization

The state shapes urbanization not only through migration policy but through centrally rationed construction-land quotas. Since 2003 the eastern provinces' share of new urban construction land has fallen from about 65% to roughly one-third by 2017, with the central and western shares rising — a tilt pursued in the name of regional coordinated development [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. This is land policy as city-size policy: by controlling how much each region can build, the centre controls where urbanization occurs, and the tilt binds hardest in the eastern cities that attract migrants.

The quantitative evaluation mirrors the migration debate. The actual 2013–2017 allocation raised GDP by 2.31% and welfare by 2.57% while narrowing the per-capita gap by 0.73%, yet widened housing-price divergence by 1.23%; a GDP-maximising allocation would raise GDP by 5.26% but widen the gap by 8.24% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The tilt's efficiency-equity balance is thus the land-side analogue of the migration-side debate: a rationing instrument with a defensible local rationale and a national efficiency cost. The instruments interact: easing migration into the eastern/southern coast while allocating more land there achieves welfare gains of roughly 47% and a per-capita gap of about −13.5%, dominating either reform alone [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Wu and You reach the same conclusion from the city side: the land-supply price elasticity is the single most effective complementary policy, with doubling the semi-elasticity cutting tier-1 low-skill native losses from 17.0% to 9.3% [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Dynamic QSE work adds that population, not capital, determines long-run structure, so land and migration policy should steer population toward the most productive regions [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The land-supply tilt is the clearest case where the state's spatial instrument choice determines the efficiency–equity balance: the equity goal that migration controls pursue can be delivered through land quotas, but at a measurable aggregate-efficiency cost that only joint land-plus-migration reform removes.

## Fiscal instruments: equalization, transfers, and property taxes

Fiscal policy is a third instrument, with different efficiency-equity properties. Zhou and Gong show that equalizing per-capita productive expenditure raises aggregate output by 1.505% and welfare by 1.555%, while equalizing social-security expenditure raises welfare by 2.693% at a 0.625% output cost; output gains from productive-expenditure equalization peak at roughly 82% equalization and decline thereafter [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]. The policy implication is preference-dependent: a welfare-oriented government should equalize aggressively, while an output-oriented government should preserve high public spending in big cities. Fiscal equalization thus offers an alternative to migration and land restriction for achieving spatial equity, at a lower efficiency cost for the productive-expenditure component.

Transfers can also be targeted at the margin of urbanization. Conditional fiscal transfers keyed to migrant numbers would give receiving cities the incentive to accommodate larger populations, directly offsetting the local-incumbent losses that drive resistance to liberalization [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. On the revenue side, property-tax design matters for human-capital allocation: because skill groups generate different spillovers, a property tax financing local public services supports a more balanced distribution of high-skill workers across cities and substitutes for direct spatial transfers [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]. These instruments widen the toolkit and change the debate's terms: the equity case for restricting cities is weaker when the same goal can be achieved through transfers and taxes.

## Market integration and capital-flow distortions

A fourth channel is the integration of product and capital markets, which determines how well agglomeration translates into aggregate output. Zhao, Wang, Dai, and Chen document that cross-regional capital flows in China run against returns — the high-return southeast coast is a net capital exporter while lower-return interior regions receive more capital — driven by investment-promotion subsidies and product-market segmentation [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]. Their decomposition attributes capital misallocation to investment distortion (45.5%) and trade barriers (44.3%) in nearly equal measure, with a 10.2% interaction, and shows that reforming either market alone cannot reverse the against-returns flows; both must be liberalized together [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]. Market integration raises efficiency but compresses underdeveloped regions, widening gaps, so "equity and efficiency" require pairing it with labor co-agglomeration and with consumption-tax reform to make local incentives compatible [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]].

This capital-side evidence joins the migration and land results. Zhao and Chen find that lowering domestic trade costs delivers far larger gains than lowering international trade costs, because the residual trade frictions are institutional rather than physical [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The state's spatial role is thus not exhausted by controlling city sizes: it also sets the market integration that determines whether the agglomeration permitted by migration and land policy actually raises national output.

## Policy complementarities

Recognition that pure migration liberalization creates winners and losers has generated research on complementary policies. Wu and You (2025) show that increasing the price elasticity of urban land supply has the largest effect on reconciling local and national welfare among the policies they consider: doubling the semi-elasticity reduces tier-1 low-skill native losses from 17.0% to 9.3%. Market-provided substitutes for publicly-provided services also reduce the welfare wedge between migrants and natives, and conditional transfers keyed to migrant numbers incentivize receiving cities to accommodate larger populations.

The joint-reform results generalize the complementarity. Land allocation and migration reform are mutually reinforcing: eastern land relieves the housing-price surge that migration alone would cause, and migration relieves the inequality that eastern land alone would cause, so the combined welfare effect (~47%) exceeds the sum of the parts [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Fiscal equalization and market integration operate on the same principle — each addresses a different distortion, and combinations dominate single instruments [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]. Even property-tax design complements the portfolio by funding the public services receiving cities must provide to migrants [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Land supply elasticity emerges as the single most effective complementary policy in quantitative models, but it faces a fundamental constraint: the most productive cities have limited developable land, and further urban expansion conflicts with farmland protection and environmental goals. Fiscal-expenditure equalization offers a complementary equity lever with a milder output trade-off, and joint land-plus-migration reform dominates any single instrument.

## Frontier: QSE policy translation for China

QSE policy design faces a fundamental tension between the "common prosperity" agenda — which emphasizes spatial equity — and the aggregate efficiency case for migration liberalization. Wu and You (2025) provide a framework for sequencing reform: first relax hukou restrictions in cities below both local and national optima (most tier-2 and tier-3 cities), complemented by land-supply reform; then address the distributional consequences in tier-1 cities through fiscal transfers and market-provided public-service alternatives. The land and fiscal instruments slot into this sequence: move land and population in the same direction toward the coastal provinces, link new construction-land quotas to in-migrant numbers, and use fiscal equalization and property-tax design to fund public services in receiving cities [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]. Dynamic models add a sequencing caution: migration slows convergence to steady state and makes the path non-monotonic, so short- and long-run effects can diverge, and cadre evaluation should shift from aggregate to per-capita indicators [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]].

Emerging research suggests that digital infrastructure may alter the optimal city-size calculus by reducing the cost of spatial separation. Data market integration ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]) and network infrastructure improvements ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]) can enable the spatial decentralization of services that currently require physical presence, and network infrastructure reduces inter-regional trade costs and the digital divide, supporting a unified national market. To the extent that digital infrastructure substitutes for physical co-location, it shifts both the agglomeration benefits and congestion costs of large cities, potentially altering the welfare calculus of migration restrictions and the spatial allocation of land.
