# The Land-Supply Regional-Tilt Debate

China's urban construction land has been rationed centrally through a system of annual construction-land quotas, and since roughly 2003 the central government has tilted an increasing share of those quotas toward the central and western provinces while compressing supply on the eastern coast, as an instrument of "regional coordinated development." The land-supply regional-tilt debate asks whether that spatial allocation is efficient. One position treats the tilt as an equity lever: by channelling construction toward regions with slack land and lower housing costs, the quotas narrow regional income gaps, curb east-coast housing-price escalation, and can raise aggregate GDP and welfare when paired with complementary reforms. The opposing position treats it as an aggregate-efficiency cost: the tilt rations land away from the country's most productive locations, inflates eastern housing prices and labour costs, suppresses eastern output, and — because it binds hardest in the cities migrants want to enter — interacts with the hukou system to distort the joint allocation of land and labour. Quantitative spatial equilibrium (QSE) models now adjudicate the debate by treating land quotas as exogenous spatial policy and scoring the actual allocation against GDP- and welfare-maximising benchmarks.

## The institutional setting: centrally rationed construction-land quotas

Urban construction land in China is not allocated by a free land market. Since the 1990s it has been managed through a "land-use-master-plan-constrained, annual-land-use-plan and basic-farmland-protection" system whose core is planned, quota-based supply [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The 2004 State Council decision on deepening land-management reform formally made land administration an instrument of macroeconomic regulation, and construction-land quotas became a routine tool for supporting lagging regions [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The result is a documented tilt: the eastern provinces' share of new urban construction land fell from about 65% in 2003 to roughly one-third by 2017, while the central share rose from about 20% and the western share from about 15% (the west peaking near 40% in 2014) [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Central fiscal transfers shifted in tandem: subsidies rose from under 8% of western GDP in 1999 to about 16% in 2009 while the eastern share stayed at 3–4%, and after an inflection in 2004 the eastern GDP share fell from a peak of 59.5% to 54.5% in 2019 as the west climbed from 17% to 21% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

The land quota sits inside a larger toolkit of state spatial governance that also includes the hukou system and fiscal transfers. The same rationing logic operates at the city level, where the largest cities maintain the highest migration barriers — Shanghai's estimated at 24% above the national average [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]] — and where urban land supply is extremely price-inelastic [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Dynamic QSE treatments place these quotas in forward-looking models in which population, not capital, is the long-run determinant of spatial structure [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]].

## Position A: the tilt as an equity lever

The equity case treats construction-land quotas as a direct spatial-redistribution instrument. Because the centre rations land, redirecting it westward is a targeted lever on regional growth that operates alongside fiscal transfers, and the empirical record supports the objective: after 2004 the interior's GDP shares rose, and the actual 2013–2017 land allocation raised aggregate GDP by about 2.31% and total welfare by 2.57% while reducing the regional per-capita income gap by 0.73% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. On this reading the tilt did not force a stark trade-off — it delivered aggregate gains and equity together, because it moved land toward regions where additional construction had a positive general-equilibrium effect [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

The equity logic is reinforced by city-size evidence: 83.3% of tier-1 and 88.9% of tier-2 Chinese cities are locally oversized, so restricting growth in the most desirable cities protects incumbents from crowding and rising housing costs [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Sibling fiscal instruments sharpen the point: equalizing per-capita productive expenditure raises output by 1.505% and welfare by 1.555%, and equalizing social-security expenditure raises welfare by 2.693% — equity and efficiency need not conflict when the instrument is well chosen [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]. Even the human-capital margin supports spatial redistribution: with cross-skill productivity and public-service spillovers, moving workers from high-wage to low-wage cities can approach the Pareto frontier [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]. And because "regional balance" is ultimately a per-capita rather than an aggregate goal, guiding population toward productive areas while ensuring per-capita convergence is the welfare-relevant target — consistent with the dynamic-QSE result that population, not capital, determines long-run structure [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]].

## Position B: the tilt as an aggregate-efficiency cost

The opposing view holds that rationing land away from the most productive locations is a first-order efficiency cost. Zhao and Chen's GDP-maximising counterfactual is the anchor: allocating the 2013–2017 land increment to the eastern developed provinces (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong, Zhejiang) raises GDP by about 5.26% and welfare by 4.58%, versus 2.31% and 2.57% under the actual tilt — an efficiency gap of roughly three percentage points of GDP [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The tilt also produces the housing-price divergence it ostensibly addresses: cities whose land supply was tightened saw housing prices about 10.6% higher than loosened cities over 2004–2013, and the actual allocation widened regional housing divergence by 1.23% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

The cost operates through inelastic urban land supply. Wu and You estimate the land-supply elasticity at about 0.043 — land release barely responds to prices — so when the quota system compresses eastern supply, the most productive cities face binding land constraints that raise housing costs and native welfare losses [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Because the tilt binds hardest in the cities migrants want to enter, it distorts land and labour jointly: the hukou system alone imposes an estimated GDP loss of 13.6% and welfare loss of 4.7% averaged over 2000–2020, and restricting any single city's size is generally welfare-reducing [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. The largest cities are underpopulated relative to the national optimum, and removing the barriers in the four largest would raise national welfare by 0.7–3.9% [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]. Tombe and Zhu set the stakes: raising the inter-provincial migrant share to one-third would increase real GDP per worker by 12.8% and welfare by 45.6% [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. The same governance logic distorts capital — cross-regional flows run against returns, and single-market reform cannot reverse the misallocation alone [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]].

## Where the two positions actually disagree

The positions do not disagree about the direction of the tilt's effects; they disagree about the benchmark against which it is judged. Zhao and Chen show no single allocation simultaneously maximises GDP and minimises inequality: allocations ranked by GDP impact also raise per-capita gaps, and allocations ranked by gap reduction sacrifice GDP [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The verdict therefore hinges on which counterfactual is the reference — max-GDP, min-inequality, equal-area, or the actual compromise.

A deeper disagreement concerns whether the tilt is the binding distortion or a second-best offset to other distortions. If fiscal equalization delivers equity at negligible output cost — productive-expenditure equalization raises output and welfare together, and social-security equalization buys large welfare gains at a 0.625% output cost [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] — then the land tilt is not the unique equity instrument and its efficiency cost is partly avoidable. If per-capita gaps shrink naturally under free migration — lowering migration costs into the eastern/southern coast cuts the regional Gini by up to about 20.5% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]] — the tilt looks like a second-best substitute for a missing labour market [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. Wu and You sharpen the axis: the positions agree cities are locally oversized but nationally undersized, and disagree on whether restriction is therefore justified, since the mobility friction it introduces generally outweighs the externality correction [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Finally, the land debate cannot be isolated from product and capital markets, where investment distortion and segmentation contribute to misallocation in nearly equal measure (45.5% versus 44.3%, plus a 10.2% interaction) [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]].

## Empirical anchors: what the models measure

The anchors come from counterfactuals in calibrated QSE models. For the 2013–2017 land increment, Zhao and Chen report: actual allocation raises GDP by 2.31% and welfare by 2.57% while cutting the per-capita gap by 0.73%; GDP-maximising allocation raises GDP by 5.26% and welfare by 4.58% but widens the gap by 8.24%; welfare-maximising is similar (5.15% and 4.62%, gap +7.45%); and gap-minimising raises GDP by only 1.61% [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The GDP-maximising benchmark feeds Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong, and Zhejiang, sharply suppresses Beijing and Shanghai housing prices, and leaves other provinces roughly even — the efficient outcome is not "starve the interior" but "feed the east while keeping the rest level" [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

Wu and You supply the city-size facts: most large cities are locally oversized (83.3% of tier-1, 88.9% of tier-2) but nationally undersized (100% of tier-1, 94.4% of tier-2) — the configuration that lets the tilt's local-equity rationale and its national-efficiency cost coexist [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Ma and Tang document the largest-city case: Shanghai's migration barrier is 24% above the national average, and removing it entirely would attract about 6 million additional migrants while reducing Shanghai's local welfare by 8.3% [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]. Tombe and Zhu give the upper bound on mobility liberalisation: +12.8% real GDP per worker and +45.6% welfare at a one-third inter-provincial migrant share [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]].

> ***Cross-pattern finding**: Across the QSE literature on Chinese spatial policy, the land tilt is never evaluated on its own. It is always one instrument in a portfolio that also includes hukou reform, fiscal equalization, and unified-market reform — and joint-reform results systematically dominate any single instrument.*

## The efficiency-equity trade-off quantified

Zhao and Chen's headline assessment is that the actual allocation "found a good balance" between efficiency and fairness: given restricted migration and an incomplete unified market, the tilt achieved substantial GDP and welfare gains while markedly narrowing per-capita income gaps [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Yet the residual efficiency gap is measurable — actual GDP growth of 2.31% against 5.26% under the GDP-maximising benchmark, actual welfare of 2.57% against 4.62% — and the magnitudes are sensitive to the price index used to deflate real GDP: the reported GDP gain from easing eastern in-migration is 1.47% when housing is included in the deflator and 4.63% when only final-goods prices are used [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]].

The trade-off largely disappears under joint reform. Combining land allocation with migration-cost reduction delivers total welfare gains around 47% (against 35.5% from migration reform alone) and turns the per-capita gap negative at about −13.5%, with the combined effect exceeding the sum of the parts [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The two instruments cover each other's shortfalls: eastern land relieves the housing-price surge that migration alone would cause (divergence of 16–18% versus 26%), and migration relieves the inequality that eastern land alone would cause (gap of −13.5% versus +7.4%) [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The fiscal analogue shows the same structure: productive-expenditure equalization raises output by 1.505% and welfare by 1.555%, while social-security equalization buys 2.693% welfare at a 0.625% output cost, with output gains peaking at roughly 82% equalization [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The corpus verdict is that the actual tilt approximates a defensible efficiency–equity balance while still leaving an efficiency gap of several percentage points of GDP relative to the GDP-maximising benchmark. The size of that gap is model-dependent, and the joint land-plus-migration counterfactual eliminates it almost entirely.

## Complementary levers: land, hukou, fiscal, and market reform

The literature converges on a portfolio of complementary instruments. The most direct complement is hukou reform: Zhao and Chen's central proposal is "land allocation combined with household registration reform" — move population and land in the same direction, toward the eastern and southern coastal provinces — supported by a cross-regional land-quota trading mechanism and by linking new quotas to in-migrant numbers [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Wu and You reach the same conclusion from the city side: doubling the urban land-supply semi-elasticity is the single most effective policy for reconciling local and national welfare, cutting tier-1 low-skill native losses from 17.0% to 9.3% — though it would push tier-1 developed-land shares from 25.7% to 44.1%, raising farmland and environmental concerns [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Conditional transfers keyed to migrant numbers would give receiving cities the incentive to accommodate larger populations [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

The fiscal levers are well quantified: equalizing productive expenditure is output- and welfare-improving, while welfare-oriented governments should equalize social-security expenditure more aggressively [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]. Property-tax design offers a further instrument: a property tax financing local public services substitutes for direct transfers and supports a more balanced human-capital allocation [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]. Finally, unified-market reform — reducing investment distortions and product-market segmentation together, with consumption-tax reform to align local incentives — addresses the capital-side counterpart of the land misallocation [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]].

## Frontier: policy translation for China

The land-quota system is the corpus's clearest example of quantitative spatial equilibrium translated into a Chinese institutional question: an administrative land-rationing instrument evaluated with modern spatial general-equilibrium tools. The translation is not hypothetical. The "optimize territorial spatial layout" task in the 14th Five-Year Plan and 2035 vision, and the "common prosperity" agenda (共同富裕), make per-capita rather than aggregate convergence the operative standard [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. Dynamic applications draw out the implications: because population, not capital, determines long-run spatial structure, and because migration slows convergence and makes the path non-monotonic, cadre evaluation should shift to per-capita indicators and population should be guided toward agglomeration in advantageous regions [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]].

Open design questions remain. How should quotas be priced — by a trading market or administrative fiat? Where should the tilt sit as migration policy changes? And how should the fiscal system be reformed so local governments have compatible incentives to host migrants and join a unified market — through consumption-tax reallocation [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]] or property-tax-financed services in receiving cities [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]? These are the concrete translation problems the corpus leaves to policy design.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The unresolved frontier question is how to design quota pricing and placement under the common prosperity agenda — how to make the tilt's equity objective incentive-compatible once migration, fiscal equalization, and market integration are also on the table.

## Current state of the debate

The corpus lands between the positions. The tilt is neither pure efficiency loss nor pure equity gain: it is a defensible second-best allocation given migration frictions, and its aggregate effects are positive [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]. The efficiency case is strongest on the margin: GDP-maximising benchmarks imply that reorienting land eastward would raise output, and joint land-plus-migration reform dominates any single instrument [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Residual uncertainty is substantial — the Chinese papers are OCR-garbled conversions, so specific magnitudes (including the roughly 47% welfare gain from the joint counterfactual) should be treated as approximate, and the models' verdicts are sensitive to price deflation and to trade and migration elasticities [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. The dynamic path also matters: convergence is slow and non-monotonic once migration is introduced [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]. A decisive test would exploit variation in quota prices and allocations from cross-regional land-quota trading pilots, paired with the city-level outcome data the QSE models already use [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]].
