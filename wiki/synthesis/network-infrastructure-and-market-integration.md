Network infrastructure — broadband internet, mobile communication networks, and data centers — is the digital-era equivalent of transport infrastructure, reducing the costs of transmitting information across space and thereby lowering the search, matching, and information frictions that segment regional markets. When regions differ in their digital connectivity, welfare gains depend not only on average improvement but crucially on how much the gap between leading and lagging regions narrows: the same total investment yields substantially larger gains when it reduces inequality than when it deepens already-advantaged areas. Recent quantitative estimates indicate that China's network infrastructure expansion between 2012 and 2019 raised aggregate welfare by approximately 10.30 percent, a magnitude comparable to the estimated effects of major institutional reforms such as hukou liberalization or large-scale road construction, and allowing for interregional labor mobility raises these gains further to 11.09 percent. These welfare improvements operate through two distinct channels: a direct reduction in the iceberg trade costs faced by tradable-goods sectors, and a general-equilibrium spillover through input-output linkages that raises productivity in non-tradable sectors as well. The rapid narrowing of the digital divide — measured by a fall in the Gini coefficient of base station density from approximately 0.70 to 0.39 over the same period — is not merely an equity achievement but a quantitatively important efficiency mechanism driving the aggregate gains.

## Network Infrastructure as a Trade Cost-Reducing Technology

Trade costs comprise not only physical transport and tariff barriers but also the costs of searching for trading partners, verifying product quality, and coordinating supply chains across space. Network infrastructure primarily addresses this last category by lowering the cost of acquiring information about distant suppliers and buyers, reducing what can be called the "non-transport frictions" in interregional trade ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). The effect is nonlinear: in regions where network penetration is low, the marginal impact of additional infrastructure on trade costs is large; as connectivity approaches saturation, the marginal benefit diminishes. This carries a critical implication — investment allocated to narrowing the gap between connected and unconnected regions generates a higher aggregate return than the same investment concentrated in already-connected areas, because the marginal welfare gain per unit of infrastructure is decreasing in the initial stock of connectivity ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

*Across the empirical literature, the pattern that digital connectivity reduces trade costs more sharply in remote regions has proven robust. The trade-creating effect of the internet is consistently largest for pairs involving a developing or remote region, and smallest between already-connected advanced economies.*

## The Quantitative Spatial Equilibrium Framework with Network Infrastructure

The welfare effects of network infrastructure cannot be captured by partial-equilibrium analysis alone, because trade cost reductions ripple through price indices, factor markets, and input-output linkages. The model embeds network infrastructure into a multi-region, multi-sector QSE framework covering 269 Chinese cities, three tradable sectors (agriculture, mining, manufacturing) and three non-tradable sectors (construction, retail, financial services), linked through a city-level input-output structure calibrated to the China Carbon Accounting Database. Consumers maximize Cobb-Douglas utility over sectoral composites; intermediate goods are produced under constant returns with labor and composite intermediates, subject to Fréchet-distributed productivity draws ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]).

The theoretical innovation is the incorporation of network infrastructure into the trade cost equation. The iceberg cost from region \(i\) to region \(n\) in sector \(j\) takes the form \(\tau_{ni}^j = \kappa_{ni}^j / \exp(\beta_i int_i)\), where \(\kappa_{ni}^j\) is the geographic component and \(int_i\) is the base station density in the exporting region. The parameter \(\beta_i\) differs between regions above and below the mean connectivity level: \(\beta_1 = 0.03\) for leading regions and \(\beta_2 = 0.11\) for lagging regions, implying a unit increase in connectivity reduces trade costs nearly four times as much from a lagging region. These parameters are estimated through a gravity regression instrumenting infrastructure with "Broadband China" pilot status and distance to the "eight-vertical-eight-horizontal" fiber-optic backbone nodes. First-stage F-statistics range from 86 to 175 across specifications ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

## Digital Divide Narrowing as a Welfare Mechanism

Between 2012 and 2019, the spatial distribution of network infrastructure in China underwent a dramatic equalization. The Gini coefficient of base station density across Chinese cities fell from approximately 0.70 to 0.39, reflecting the rapid build-out of mobile networks in the central and western regions that had previously lagged far behind the coast ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). This convergence was not a natural byproduct of market forces alone — it was actively shaped by the "Broadband China" strategy (announced 2013), subsequent "Digital China" and "Network Power" initiatives, and specific large-scale projects such as the Guizhou Big Data Center, which transformed a previously landlocked province into a national data-processing hub.

The welfare significance of this narrowing lies in the nonlinearity documented above. Because lagging regions have a higher marginal return to connectivity, catch-up from the low-infrastructure tail generates disproportionately large welfare gains. A counterfactual that optimally reallocates new base stations to maximize a Foster welfare function — balancing average connectivity against equality — finds that welfare gains could have been raised from 10.30 percent to approximately 15.1 percent under a more equal distribution. Notably, an allocation that maximizes only average connectivity or only minimizes the Gini coefficient both yield lower welfare than the balanced optimum, confirming that the digital divide channel is not merely an equity concern but an efficiency mechanism ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

> **Cross-pattern observation:** The finding that infrastructure has higher marginal returns in lagging regions parallels a robust result in the transport literature. Studies of China's highway network — including [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]] — find that road improvements in interior provinces generated larger trade cost reductions per kilometer than comparable coastal investments. This pattern appears general: the marginal benefit of reducing a spatial friction is decreasing in the initial level of access.

## Decomposing the Welfare Estimates

The headline result from the structural estimation — a 10.30 percent aggregate welfare gain from 2012 to 2019 — is stable across a battery of robustness checks: alternative trade elasticities (ranging from 2 to 8), changes in the base year (2012 versus 2007), controls for concurrent tariff reductions, and variations in the estimated infrastructure parameters ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). When the model is extended to allow for interregional labor mobility — rather than assuming a fixed population in each region — the welfare gain rises to 11.09 percent, because workers reallocate toward the regions where trade cost reductions have raised labor demand, amplifying the productivity response.

A decomposition of the welfare gains reveals a striking regional pattern. At the national level, roughly 53 percent operates through input-output linkages — as trade costs fall, regions expand imports of intermediate inputs, lowering production costs across supply chains — and 47 percent through the direct trade-share channel. In the eastern coastal region, input-output linkages account for nearly 60 percent of the gain, reflecting dense production networks. In the western region, the direct trade-share channel dominates at over 63 percent, because western economies rely more heavily on importing manufactured goods from the east than on supplying intermediates to complex supply chains ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

Sectoral heterogeneity is equally pronounced. Tradable sectors with high non-transport frictions — petroleum processing, chemicals, and non-metallic mineral products — experience the largest welfare gains. Some tradable sectors, including textiles and transport equipment, record small negative effects because improved connectivity allows production to concentrate in advantaged locations. Among non-tradable sectors, wholesale and retail trade benefit most, reflecting the role of e-commerce in expanding search scope.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The 10.30 percent estimate captures general-equilibrium effects through trade costs and input-output linkages but does not incorporate dynamic effects on firm innovation, technology adoption, or new service varieties. If these channels were included, the estimate should be interpreted as a lower bound on the total contribution of digital connectivity during this period.

## The Data Market Complementarity

Network infrastructure provides the physical backbone without which the modern data economy cannot function at scale. Lin, Liu, and Zhou (2026) develop a QSE model with a data production sector to study the construction of a nationally integrated data market. Their counterfactual simulations show that unifying fragmented regional data markets — where "data islands" and "data chimneys" segment access across provincial boundaries — would generate additional welfare gains beyond those captured by the infrastructure channel alone, because unification enables the non-rival data factor to achieve its full scale economy.

In the integrated data market, an intermediary collects raw data from consumers in all regions, processes it into workable data (primarily in western regions following the "East Data, West Computing" logic, where cooling and electricity costs are lower), and sells it to firms in both the east and the west. The simulated sales price of data falls by approximately 41 percent in the east and 36 percent in the west relative to the segmented baseline, driven by two mechanisms: a resource allocation effect (processing shifts to lower-cost western locations) and a scale economy effect unique to non-rival factors (the same dataset sold to many firms). Firms increase data input, and aggregate GDP rises by about 12.3 percent, with eastern welfare increasing by approximately 6.5 percent and western welfare by 3.5 percent ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]).

The complementarity between network infrastructure and data market integration is bidirectional. Infrastructure provides the physical backbone, but its value is magnified by institutional data market integration: without common standards and data property rights frameworks, even the best physical network cannot eliminate the market segmentation that prevents data from achieving scale economies. *Infrastructure investment and data market reform are strategic complements — the marginal return to each is increasing in the level of the other.*

## Historical Parallel: Transport Infrastructure Then, Digital Now

The endogeneity between infrastructure and spatial economic change has a clear historical precedent. Trew (2020) models the evolution of the transport sector in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century England and Wales alongside the growth of trade and output, using registration-district data on occupational structure across 1710–1881. Transport infrastructure was not a top-down public investment but largely driven by local private enterprise — turnpike trusts, canal companies, and railway firms — that responded to local demand. As industrial hotspots emerged in the North and Midlands, demand for transportation improvements grew to move output over increasing distances; those improvements then fed back into the location and scale of production, accelerating the industrial takeoff.

Trew's counterfactual analysis yields two findings relevant to contemporary China. First, the spatial distribution of infrastructure matters as much as its timing: a uniform investment program delivers far less growth acceleration than one concentrated where local demand has emerged. Second, exogenously imposed transport improvements can, under certain conditions, delay growth by interfering with agglomeration dynamics. The Liu-Zhang-Cai results echo this: concentrating network infrastructure on the coastal manufacturing belt would have maximized short-run output growth, but the welfare-optimal allocation is more spatially balanced because the digital divide channel generates larger welfare gains per unit of investment in lagging regions ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]; [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]).

## Regional Heterogeneity and Remaining Policy Scope

The welfare gains from network infrastructure are distributed unevenly. By 2019, eastern regions gained approximately 10–12 percent, western regions 9–11 percent, and central regions 7–9 percent. The central region's smaller gain reflects a composition effect: its initial infrastructure was moderate, so it benefited less from catch-up than the west, and its production networks are less integrated than the east's. The reallocation counterfactual raises the national gain from 10.30 percent to 15.11 percent, with the central region experiencing the largest improvement (from approximately 8 percent to 15.6 percent), confirming substantial room for optimization ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

Wu and You (2025) provide a complementary perspective through the lens of the hukou system, the world's largest internal migration restriction. Their calibrated spatial equilibrium model estimates that the hukou system reduced GDP by 13.6 percent and welfare by 4.7 percent between 2000 and 2020. This connects to network infrastructure in two ways. First, infrastructure and labor mobility are partial substitutes: better digital connectivity in interior regions enables remote access to public services (telemedicine, online education, remote government administration), reducing the marginal benefit of migrating to a coastal city and thereby lowering the effective hukou wedge. Second, because large Chinese cities have already surpassed their locally optimal sizes, further hukou liberalization could worsen congestion; network infrastructure partly relaxes this constraint by making public services partially delocalizable ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Whether further digital infrastructure investment in lagging regions can substantially reduce spatial inequality depends on the strength of offsetting agglomeration forces. If connectivity primarily enables interior regions to export goods more cheaply, it narrows spatial disparities. If it primarily enables coastal firms to manage remote operations more efficiently, it could reinforce the coastal core's dominance. The Liu-Zhang-Cai results suggest the former effect dominates in China's current stage, but this balance could shift as the economy matures.

## Frontier: QSE Policy Translation for China

The integration of network infrastructure and data market reforms has direct implications for three policy domains central to China's "Digital China" and "common prosperity" agendas. The first concerns digital infrastructure as a substitute for physical mobility. The post-COVID acceleration of telecommuting, e-commerce, and remote service delivery suggests that network quality in interior regions can reduce the need to migrate to coastal cities, lowering congestion in megacities while improving welfare in sending regions. Wu and You (2025) show that the hukou wedge is economically large, with the market valuation of an urban hukou in a tier-2 city estimated at 90,000–126,000 RMB in 2017. If high-quality digital connectivity makes education and healthcare partially remotely accessible, this wedge shrinks, and the welfare cost of maintaining migration barriers rises accordingly. A quantitative extension of the Liu-Zhang-Cai framework that endogenizes the substitutability between digital access and physical presence for public-service consumption would allow policymakers to calibrate the optimal sequencing of infrastructure investment and hukou reform.

The second policy pathway concerns the complementarity between infrastructure, data markets, and labor reform as a coordinated package. Lin, Liu, and Zhou (2026) identify a multiplier effect: a unified data market amplifies the growth impact of any given productivity or trade-cost shock. Combined with the finding that network infrastructure's welfare effect rises from 10.30 to 11.09 percent under labor mobility ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]), this implies that the three reforms should be pursued as a sequenced package: better networks raise the return to data market integration, data market integration raises the demand for networks, and labor mobility amplifies both.

The third policy pathway concerns the resilience function of digital infrastructure. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated that regions with robust network connectivity sustained smaller output losses during mobility restrictions, because firms could shift to remote operation. Network infrastructure thus serves as insurance against asymmetric shocks — a function not captured in steady-state welfare estimates. Embedding stochastic disruptions into a dynamic QSE framework ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]) would allow a quantitative assessment of the optimal level of network investment when resilience is valued alongside steady-state efficiency. This direction is particularly relevant as China's "Digital China" strategy enters its implementation phase, with projected investment in 5G networks, data centers, and the "East Data, West Computing" architecture expected to exceed several trillion yuan over the coming decade ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]; [[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).
