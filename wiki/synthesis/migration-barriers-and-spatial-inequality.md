## Migration Barriers and Spatial Inequality

Spatial inequality — the dispersion of real incomes, productivity, and welfare across locations within a country — is shaped by the interaction between migration barriers (hukou restrictions, moving costs, information frictions) and the underlying spatial distribution of productivity, amenities, and firm-level distortions. While migration barriers preserve spatial income differences by preventing labor from moving to high-productivity locations, their removal does not unambiguously reduce spatial inequality: when workers migrate to already-productive regions, the resulting agglomeration economies can widen the gap between winning and losing regions even as they raise national welfare. In China these forces are compounded by the state's own spatial instruments — rationed construction-land quotas, cross-regional capital allocation, and fiscal transfers — which are themselves drivers of regional gaps. The literature now spans migration frictions, firm-level misallocation, land-supply tilt, capital-flow distortions, and skill-heterogeneous human-capital allocation, together producing a fundamental tension between aggregate efficiency and spatial equity.

## Measuring Spatial Inequality in China

China exhibits among the highest levels of spatial income inequality of any major economy. The ratio of real GDP per worker between the top five and bottom five provinces was approximately 4:1 in 2000 ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]), and the cross-province dispersion of nominal GDP per worker substantially exceeded that across U.S. states. Within provinces, the average ratio of real GDP per worker in non-agriculture relative to agriculture was also around 4:1, reflecting both productivity differentials and the barriers that prevented workers from moving out of agriculture. The large within-sector income gaps across provinces — even after controlling for rural-urban price differences — pointed to significant frictions in both goods and labor markets as the fundamental drivers of persistent spatial inequality ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). The sectoral divide is thus as large as the geographic one, and the two interact: the same barriers that keep workers in poor provinces also keep them in agriculture, so decompositions of China's spatial inequality must separate frictions to moving across locations from frictions to moving across sectors.

## Migration Barriers as Determinants of Spatial Inequality

The hukou (household registration) system is the largest and most consequential migration barrier in modern China. Each citizen is assigned a hukou in a specific location and category, and changing either requires government approval; since the 1980s the prohibition on moving has been relaxed but replaced by restricted access to public services, higher costs for healthcare and children's education, and limited employment rights for migrants without local hukou ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

Tombe and Zhu (2019) estimate that the average cost of moving from rural to urban areas within a province in 2000 was equivalent to shrinking one's real income by a factor of nearly three; between-province moves were an order of magnitude more costly. In their decomposition, migration contributed roughly one-tenth to aggregate labor productivity growth between 2000 and 2005, with about five-sixths of these gains coming from the reallocation of labor from agriculture to non-agriculture rather than from equalization across locations — so the largest gains from liberalization come from moving workers out of agriculture, not from narrowing inter-regional gaps.

Wu and You (2025) estimate that the overall hukou system created an average GDP loss of 13.6% and a welfare loss of 4.7% over 2000–2020, and show that it is generally welfare-reducing to restrict the size of any city through migration barriers. These aggregate results, however, mask important distributional consequences.

## Firm-Level Frictions and the Spatial Distribution of Distortions

Spatial inequality in China is not only a story of migration barriers keeping workers away from productive regions. Firm-level frictions — output and labor wedges that distort firm decisions within each location — are themselves spatially distributed in a way that amplifies income disparities. Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) estimate that friction dispersion is systematically higher in poorer prefectures: the median standard deviation of output frictions is 0.086–0.084 in the richest coastal regions but 0.108 inland, and the correlation between frictions and productivity is substantially positive in poor regions (0.242) but near zero on the coasts. These spatial patterns explain approximately 13% of the observed cross-prefecture variation in per-capita GDP. The spatial distribution of frictions also reinforces migration flows: because the most distorted locations are also the poorest, the friction gradient and the income gradient point in the same direction, so migration toward less-distorted regions and friction reduction in distorted regions operate on the same spatial fault lines. Crucially, policies that reduce this dispersion reduce spatial inequality — the opposite of what migration liberalization does — because poorer prefectures, starting from higher dispersion, benefit disproportionately from uniform reductions ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

## The Migration-Misallocation Interaction

The most striking finding in this literature is that migration and firm-level misallocation interact in opposing ways for aggregate welfare versus spatial equity. Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) show that reducing the dispersion of firm-level frictions by 0.01 raises aggregate welfare by 3.19% and reduces the Gini coefficient of real wages by 0.30 percentage points. When the same 3.19% welfare gain is achieved through migration liberalization instead, the Gini coefficient **increases** by 0.002, because migrants move to richer, less-distorted prefectures and the resulting population concentration amplifies productivity advantages. Without migration, the same friction reduction yields a larger welfare gain (3.37% vs. 3.19%) since workers cannot escape distorted regions, but the reduction in spatial inequality is smaller (Gini declines 0.25 vs. 0.30). Migration is thus a safety valve for aggregate welfare but an amplifier of spatial disparities ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

> **Cross-pattern observation:** The opposing effects of migration liberalization and misallocation reduction on spatial inequality are robust across multiple model specifications in Li, Ma, and Tang (2024), including alternative congestion, migration, and substitution elasticities, and the qualitative pattern has been corroborated in dynamic QSE frameworks ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]).

## The Agglomeration Channel

The reason migration to productive regions worsens spatial inequality lies partly in agglomeration economies. Ma and Tang (2020) show that destination cities benefit from migrant inflows because endogenous firm entry expands the variety of available goods and lowers prices, dominating congestion: over 2000–2005 the 40 cities that received net population inflows experienced a 4.7% increase in real income and a 4.0% increase in welfare after congestion disutility. However, Wu and You (2025) reveal a critical tension: most large Chinese cities have surpassed their **locally optimal** sizes but remain below their **nationally optimal** sizes. When migration barriers are removed, the inflow of workers — especially low-skilled workers, disproportionately affected by hukou restrictions — depresses native welfare in the most productive cities through crowding in publicly-provided private goods, rising housing prices, and dilution of land-rent income shares. Agglomeration thus creates a virtuous cycle for receiving cities and a vicious cycle for sending regions, widening spatial inequality even as it raises national output.

## The Land-Supply-Tilt Channel

Spatial inequality is also manufactured by the state's allocation of a second immobile factor: land. Since 2003, centrally rationed construction-land quotas have tilted toward central and western provinces: the eastern share of national land supply fell from 65% in 2003 to roughly one-third by 2017, while the western share rose to about 40% by 2014 ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). This narrowed aggregate regional GDP gaps — the eastern GDP share fell from a 59.5% peak to 54.5% by 2019 — but rationed land precisely where productivity and migrant inflows were highest, contributing to coastal house-price growth (110% versus 80% in central/western provinces over 2002–2012 in the model's calibration).

Zhao and Chen (2021) quantify the trade-off. A GDP-maximizing land allocation would give more land to the eastern provinces, raising GDP by 5.26% and welfare by 4.58% while suppressing Beijing and Shanghai house prices, but it would widen the per-capita income gap by 8.24%. A gap-minimizing allocation would shrink the gap by 2.50% yet raise GDP by only 1.61%. The actual 2013–2017 allocation sat between these poles (GDP +2.31%, welfare +2.57%, per-capita gap −0.73%, house-price divergence +1.23%) — a balance between efficiency and equity. Land rationing and hukou rationing are two halves of the same problem: allocating land east without relaxing migration widens per-capita gaps (+8.24%), while relaxing migration without loosening land supply raises house-price divergence (+26.22%). Jointly reallocating land and population eastward achieves both goals — welfare +47.13%, per-capita gap −13.50% ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]).

> **Cross-pattern observation:** Across the corpus, the state's spatial instruments — land quotas, capital allocation, and fiscal transfers — are themselves drivers of spatial inequality, not just correctives for it: Zhao–Chen show the land tilt trades aggregate efficiency for regional equity, Zhao–Wang–Dai–Chen show state-directed capital flows compress efficiency in return for narrowing regional totals, and Zhou–Gong show fiscal equalization buys equity only at a measured efficiency cost.

## The Cross-Regional Capital-Flow Channel

A third state-driven channel operates through capital. Zhao, Wang, Dai, and Chen (2025) document, from firm registration data, that China's capital flows run *against* unit capital returns: the highest-return southeast coast is in net capital outflow, while lower-return central and western regions receive a disproportionate share of new investment. Two interacting distortions explain the reversal. Cross-regional investment distortions — local-government tax incentives, cheap land, and subsidies offered to attract external capital — drive a wedge between the effective and the observed return. Product-market segmentation operates like tariff-jumping in international trade: to avoid high internal trade barriers, firms build plants directly in protected local markets, so capital follows protected markets rather than high returns. In a quantitative spatial general-equilibrium model, the two factors contribute roughly equal shares — 45.5% to investment distortions, 44.3% to trade barriers, 10.2% to their interaction — and ignoring the interaction understates the gains from domestic trade opening by about 20% ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]).

No single-market reform suffices: lowering either distortion alone mitigates but does not reverse the against-returns pattern — only simultaneous liberalization of both markets makes returns and flows positively correlated. The spatial-inequality consequences cut both ways: state-directed capital toward lagging regions narrows regional total-output gaps but depresses aggregate efficiency, while market integration raises efficiency but compresses the resources of underdeveloped regions, widening per-capita gaps unless labor co-moves toward productive regions ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]; [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]). This is the capital-market counterpart to the within-prefecture wedge story.

## Human-Capital Spatial Allocation

The fourth channel concerns who moves, not just how many. Liu, Wang, and Chen (2024) build a quantitative spatial model with heterogeneous skills, cross-skill productivity spillovers, and public-service congestion, and ask whether China's spatial distribution of human capital is efficient. It is not: the Pareto-efficient allocation distributes high-skill workers more evenly across regions than the current distribution — high-skill workers should move toward initially smaller cities and low-skill workers toward initially larger ones — and optimizing this allocation raises social welfare. The gains operate through cross-skill spillovers: ignoring the productivity and public-service spillovers between skill groups drastically shrinks the measured gain from reallocation ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]).

Two fiscal instruments can decentralize the efficient allocation. A transfer system redistributing from high-skill workers in high-wage cities to low-skill workers in low-wage cities moves the economy onto the Pareto frontier; a local property tax financing public services acts as a partial substitute, shrinking the required transfers while preserving the welfare gain. Hukou mobility frictions cut the achievable welfare gain by around 2.4 percentage points, echoing the finding that migration barriers distort not only the level of labor but also its skill composition ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]). Because high-skill workers are the most mobile and the most complementary to agglomeration, this channel connects directly to the migration-misallocation and agglomeration results ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

## Counterfactual Policy Analysis

**Full hukou liberalization** would raise national GDP by an average of 13.6% and welfare by 4.7% over 2000–2020; tier-1 cities would absorb 87.6% more population, tier-3 cities would lose 9.1%, natives in tier-1 cities would lose 10–17% in welfare, and migrants would gain 5–8%. The gains are front-loaded — GDP rises 23.3% in 2000 but only 4.3% by 2020 ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

**Misallocation reduction** (SOE reform, financial deepening) raises aggregate welfare and reduces spatial inequality simultaneously, benefiting poorer prefectures most ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]). **Targeted liberalization with complements** — notably raising the price elasticity of urban land supply, the largest single reliever of the local-national tension — faces the constraint that developable land in the most productive sites is scarce, with the built-up share of tier-1 cities' developable land rising from 25.7% to 44.1% under a doubling of the land-supply elasticity ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

**Land and fiscal instruments.** Jointly reallocating land quotas and relaxing migration into the coastal provinces raises welfare by roughly 47% while cutting the per-capita gap by about 13.5% ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). Equalizing per-capita productive expenditure raises output by 1.505% and welfare by 1.555%; equalizing social-security expenditure raises welfare by 2.693% at a small output cost (−0.625%), with social-security equalization the more efficient inequality-reducing instrument ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]). Land-ownership reform that lets migrants retain land rights regardless of residence raises welfare by nearly 12% ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]).

**Transport and digital infrastructure.** Internal trade cost reductions produce larger gains than external ones (11.4% vs. 2.9% in the Tombe-Zhu framework), and network infrastructure can narrow the digital divide between regions ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

> **Current wiki assessment (2026-07):** No single policy simultaneously maximizes aggregate welfare and minimizes spatial inequality in China. Migration liberalization delivers large aggregate gains but worsens spatial inequality; misallocation reduction improves both but addresses a different margin. The land, capital-flow, and human-capital channels add instruments — land-quota reallocation, unified-market reform, property-tax and transfer design — that, combined with migration liberalization and compensatory fiscal transfers, form the emerging policy consensus for managing the efficiency-equity trade-off.

## Cross-Country Perspective on Spatial Inequality and Migration

China's spatial inequality is large by international standards. Tombe and Zhu (2019) estimate that lowering migration costs to a one-third inter-provincial migrant share (comparable to the U.S.) would raise real GDP by 12.8% and welfare by 45.6%. Institutional differences in land markets play a key role: because collective rural land ownership gives migrants no land-based benefit upon moving, land reform that preserves land rights regardless of residence yields a welfare gain of nearly 12%. Dynamic considerations also matter: Kleinman, Liu, and Redding (2023) estimate an average half-life of around 20 years for income convergence driven by capital-migration dynamics, and adding migration to macro models slows convergence because workers move toward initially productive regions rather than toward regions where their marginal product is highest in the long run ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

At the global scale the gains are far larger. Desmet, Nagy, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018), in a dynamic spatial growth model of the world at $1^{\circ}\times1^{\circ}$ resolution, find that fully liberalizing migration would raise world welfare by roughly 306% (and output by about 126%) in present discounted value, with 70.3% of the world's population moving across countries at impact; under current frictions the world takes around 400 years to reach its balanced-growth path ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]).

> **Current wiki assessment (2026-07):** Chinese estimates of the welfare gain from domestic migration liberalization (Tombe–Zhu 45.6%; Wu–You 4.7%) sit far below the Desmet–Nagy–Rossi-Hansberg global free-migration upper bound of roughly 306%. Part of the gap reflects scope — the global exercise includes international migration, where barriers are far higher than domestic hukou costs — but the magnitude also indicates that China's internal barriers remain economically first-order, and that the efficiency-equity tension documented for China is one instance of a general spatial-development problem.

## Frontier: QSE Policy Translation for China

The synthesis of firm-level frictions, migration barriers, and agglomeration economies carries direct implications for China's "common prosperity" agenda. First, **policy packages that combine multiple instruments** are essential: pairing hukou reform with SOE reform and financial deepening in lagging regions can achieve the welfare gains of migration liberalization while offsetting its distributional costs ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]), and the land-plus-hukou and unified-market-plus-labor packages dominate any single instrument ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]). Second, **fiscal compensation mechanisms** need calibration: the welfare losses of tier-1 natives (10–17%) require compensation, and fiscal-equalization results identify productive-expenditure equalization for output and social-security equalization for welfare and inequality, with conditional transfers tied to migrant inflows as a targeted complement ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Third, **land reform** deserves a central place: loosening land quotas in productive cities, linking new construction-land quotas to migrant inflows, and shifting local-official assessment from aggregate to per-capita targets directly attacks the land-rationing channel ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). Fourth, **unified national market reform** — reducing cross-regional investment distortions and local trade protection, with consumption-tax reform to align local incentives — addresses the against-returns capital flows ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]). Finally, **digital infrastructure and data-market integration** offer novel channels for reducing spatial inequality without physical relocation ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]; [[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]).

*Current wiki assessment (2026-07): the degree to which digital infrastructure and data-market integration have reduced spatial inequality in practice is still being evaluated, and whether improved connectivity promotes out-migration from lagging regions or attracts investment remains context-dependent. The calibration of a full package that moves land, capital, people, and fiscal transfers simultaneously remains an open frontier.*
