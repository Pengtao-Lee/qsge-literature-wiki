Spatial public finance is the study of how government tax and expenditure policy reshapes the spatial distribution of economic activity through general-equilibrium rather than direct-incidence channels. In a quantitative spatial equilibrium, a fiscal instrument applied in one region does not stay there: it alters local wages, prices, migration flows, and trade shares, which feed back onto output and welfare everywhere through factor mobility and goods-market linkages. The Chinese sub-literature ports this toolkit onto the country's defining fiscal institutions — regionally targeted expenditure equalization, centrally rationed construction-land quotas, the hukou household-registration system, and the tax-and-transfer architecture between central and local government — and arrives at two headline results. First, removing spatial misallocation can raise national output and welfare simultaneously: equalizing per-capita productive expenditure raises output by roughly 1.5 percent and welfare by a comparable margin. Second, the efficiency–equity trade-off reappears precisely when fiscal instruments are used for pure redistribution: equalizing social-security spending delivers a larger welfare gain at a small output cost, while land and migration reforms that maximize efficiency widen regional income gaps unless paired with complementary population policies.

## Fiscal policy in spatial equilibrium: the conceptual frame

In the quantitative spatial equilibrium paradigm, locations are not independent observations: they are systematically linked through costly goods trade, commuting, and migration, so a policy in one region shifts the balance of agglomeration and dispersion forces everywhere ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). A tax, transfer, or public-expenditure program acts as a wedge on locational choice — it changes the real income a household can attain in each region, and hence the migration margin, and it changes unit production costs, and hence trade shares. The structural approach makes counterfactuals tractable by inverting the model to recover unobserved regional productivity and amenity from observed data, then solving for the equilibrium response to a policy change; reduced-form estimates of the same policy are exposed to the Lucas critique because the spatial linkages that transmit the shock are held fixed ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

Two distinct normative problems run through the Chinese applications. The first is *misallocation removal*: when labor is trapped in low-productivity regions by frictions, reallocating factors toward more productive places raises aggregate output and welfare together ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]). The second is *redistribution*: when the goal is to narrow regional income gaps, fiscal instruments deliberately pull factors toward lower-productivity regions, which can lower aggregate output even as they raise average welfare and reduce inequality ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]). The central contribution of this literature is to show that the same instrument can sit on either side of that line, and that general-equilibrium effects — not the direct transfer itself — determine which outcome dominates ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]; [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]).

## Expenditure equalization: productive versus social-security spending

Zhou and Gong's quantitative spatial model of regional expenditure equalization draws the cleanest distinction between the two faces of fiscal policy. The model nests heterogeneous residential preferences and interregional migration with two expenditure types: productive expenditure, which raises local total factor productivity, and social-security expenditure, which raises the public good available to residents ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]). Holding total public spending fixed, equalizing per-capita productive expenditure raises aggregate output by 1.505 percent and resident welfare by 1.555 percent: reallocating public inputs toward lower-productivity regions removes a misallocation whose efficiency cost exceeds any loss from relocating labor. Equalizing social-security expenditure instead raises welfare by 2.693 percent while reducing output by 0.625 percent — the spending pulls labor toward regions with lower wages and productivity, improving average welfare at a real efficiency cost. Both policies reduce measured welfare inequality, but social-security equalization is the more effective instrument for that purpose: the Gini coefficient of welfare falls by 2.469 percent under social-security equalization against 0.990 percent under productive-expenditure equalization.

The distinction is not knife-edge. As the degree of equalization rises continuously, the output gain from productive-expenditure equalization first rises and then falls, peaking at roughly 82 percent equalization, while welfare keeps improving monotonically; for social-security expenditure, every step toward equalization raises welfare and lowers output further ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]). A selective-subsidy variant — adding new spending to low-expenditure regions rather than cutting high-expenditure regions — produces larger gains on both margins (output +3.467 percent and welfare +2.263 percent for productive spending; welfare +4.896 percent for social-security spending), because high-spending regions are never harmed.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *Across [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] and [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]], the removal of spatial misallocation raises output and welfare together; the trade-off between the two only re-emerges when policy is aimed at pure redistribution. Instruments operating on factor allocation (productive spending, friction dispersion) dominate on both margins, whereas instruments operating on locational amenities (social-security spending, migration liberalization) trade efficiency against equity.*

## Human capital, cross-skill spillovers, and the property tax

Fiscal instruments also shape the spatial allocation of *types* of labor. Liu, Wang, and Chen build a quantitative spatial model with two skill groups whose productivity and public-service consumption spill over across skill types, and ask what fiscal policy implements the constrained Pareto-efficient allocation of human capital ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]). Two spillover forces are key: positive productivity spillovers from agglomeration, which favor concentrating high-skill workers in large cities, and public-service spillovers, where low-skill population exerts congestion partly offset by high-skill residents. Because the market fails to internalize these externalities, China's observed human-capital distribution sits inside the Pareto frontier, and reallocating workers across cities can raise both groups' welfare; when cross-type spillovers are shut down, the same reallocation buys almost nothing, indicating that skill complementarity is the operative mechanism.

The implementing policy is a spatial transfer program: reaching the Pareto-efficient frontier requires redistributing from high-skill workers in high-wage cities to low-skill workers in low-wage cities, and the optimal equilibrium is more spatially balanced than the status quo — high-skill workers move toward initially smaller cities and low-skill workers toward larger ones ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]). A property tax that finances local public services partially substitutes for these transfers: when a regionally differentiated property tax is introduced, the required transfers shrink, and the welfare gain from optimal reallocation is if anything larger than in the no-tax baseline, because the tax ties local revenue to service provision. Hukou-related migration frictions blunt the policy: using measured city-level settlement-threshold indices, the welfare gain from optimal reallocation falls by roughly 2.4 percent under the hukou friction — the same institutional wedge that [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]] and [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] identify as the principal barrier to factor mobility.

## Land allocation, transfers, and the central-local fiscal structure

A second fiscal channel operates on the supply side: the central government's rationing of construction-land quotas, which since 2003 has tilted new urban land toward central and western provinces and compressed it in the east ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). Zhao and Chen calibrate a quantitative spatial model with residential and industrial land, interprovincial migration, and domestic and international trade, and evaluate the actual 2013–2017 allocation against counterfactual targets. The actual westward tilt raised GDP by 2.31 percent and total welfare by 2.57 percent while narrowing the interregional income gap by 0.73 percent — but it also widened regional housing-price divergence by 1.23 percent, since fast-growing eastern cities received less land. The pure efficiency target, maximization of GDP, allocates more land to eastern provinces: it raises GDP by 5.26 percent and suppresses eastern house prices (price divergence falls by 8.53 percent) but at the cost of widening the income gap by 8.24 percent. Minimizing the income gap raises GDP only 1.61 percent. The real-world allocation sits between these poles, and the paper reads it as a deliberate balance of efficiency against equity.

The deeper finding is that land allocation alone cannot escape the efficiency–equity trade-off, because the income gap is fundamentally a migration problem. When land policy is combined with hukou reform that lowers migration costs into eastern provinces — moving people and land in the same direction — both objectives improve simultaneously: the combined policy raises welfare by roughly 47 percent and narrows the income gap by 13.5 percent, though destination-province house prices rise. The same logic underlies the paper's trade result: lowering domestic interprovincial trade costs delivers far larger gains than lowering international trade costs, consistent with the dominant role of internal over external frictions in [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]].

## Local government incentives and market integration

Where land and expenditure policies are decided, local-government objectives intrude, and the resulting distortions are a fiscal phenomenon in their own right. Zhao, Wang, Dai, and Chen document that capital in China flows *against* the rate of return — more capital is allocated to regions with lower unit capital returns — and decompose this "capital flows against returns" pattern into two sources ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]). Using a quantitative spatial model with micro-founded cross-regional investment decisions and product-market segmentation, they find that removing cross-regional investment distortions accounts for 45.489 percent of the improvement in capital allocation, trade barriers for 44.321 percent, and the interaction reinforcement between the two markets for the remaining 10.19 percent. Ignoring the product–capital market interaction understates the gains from domestic trade opening by roughly 20 percent. Strikingly, reforming either market alone does not reverse the misallocation: capital still flows toward low-return regions because firms jump trade barriers by investing in the protected market (tariff-jumping); only simultaneous integration of both markets makes capital flow toward high-return regions.

The root cause is local-government behavior. Under an objective that values both local output and fiscal revenue, cutting the capital tax rate to attract investment raises local-government utility up to a point and then lowers it (the inverted-U of investment competition), and the observed economy sits on the rising segment — so governments keep distorting. Trade protection, in contrast, is monotonically costly to local government utility once trade opens, because with production-based taxation the local government loses revenue when its market is penetrated. The incentive-compatibility fix is fiscal: moving the consumption tax from the production site to the consumption site converts the local government's response to trade opening from monotone decline into a U-shape that rewards open, consumption-friendly environments ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *Across [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]] and [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]], local-government objectives that reward aggregate local output or fiscal revenue distort spatial allocation — through investment attraction, trade protection, and land-leasing — and the correction is not to forbid local discretion but to change the fiscal incentive (per-capita evaluation, consumption-tax reform). The theme also runs through [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]], where spatially dispersed firm-level frictions in poorer regions are partly policy-created.*

## The hukou system as a fiscal wedge

The hukou system is the most studied fiscal institution in the Chinese spatial literature, and its modern treatment is as a *resource transfer* between natives and migrants rather than a pure iceberg cost on mobility. Wu and You model hukou as a fiscal wedge — migrants pay more for local public services and receive a smaller share of local land income than natives — and estimate that removing all hukou restrictions from 2000 onward would have raised GDP by 13.6 percent and welfare by 4.7 percent on average across 2000–2020, with the GDP effect large early (23.3 percent in 2000) and fading as migration adjusts ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). The welfare gain is far smaller than the GDP gain because migrants moving to high-wage cities face higher housing costs. Because the system transfers resources, its distributional logic differs from an iceberg friction: natives in the most desirable cities lose welfare when restrictions are relaxed, and their representative resident — who shares in local land rents — is already worse off under existing sizes, implying that China's largest cities are *too large locally but too small nationally*.

The magnitudes are sensitive to how the wedge is modeled. Tombe and Zhu's quantitative analysis of China's trade and migration frictions calibrates a migration elasticity of $\kappa = 1.5$ and a trade elasticity of $\theta = 4$ and finds that lowering migration costs to US inter-state mobility levels would raise welfare by roughly 46 percent ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Their framework captures a second, land-based fiscal wedge: because land is collectively owned and migrants forfeit claims to land income when they leave their registration, the effective cost of migration in 2000 averaged a factor of about 5.3 — a migration utility cost of 2.8 combined with a land-income loss of 1.9 — making the land–hukou interaction quantitatively central. Combining land reallocation with lower migration costs raises welfare by roughly 47 percent in [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]], and the hukou friction measurably shrinks the welfare gains from optimal reallocation in [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]].

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The magnitude of the aggregate welfare gains from hukou reform is genuinely contested in this corpus — roughly 4.7 percent (Wu–You, fiscal-wedge model), 46 percent (Tombe–Zhu, iceberg-cost plus land-forfeiture model at US mobility levels), and about 47 percent when land reform is bundled with migration liberalization (Zhao–Chen). The spread is not noise: it tracks whether hukou is modeled as a transfer (smaller gain, larger distributional conflict) or as a pure mobility cost (larger gain). The directional conclusion — removing the wedge raises aggregate welfare — is unanimous; the size is not.*

## Place-based versus people-based policy

A sharp contrast emerges when the same aggregate welfare gain is achieved by two very different instruments. Li, Ma, and Tang structurally estimate firm-level output and labor frictions across Chinese prefectures and show that reducing the dispersion of these frictions by 0.01 raises aggregate welfare by 3.19 percent ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]). Because poorer regions carry higher friction dispersion, the same uniform reduction benefits them disproportionately, draws population *toward* smaller and poorer prefectures, and lowers the Gini coefficient of real wages by about 0.26 percent — a place-based improvement that reduces spatial inequality. Liberalizing migration barriers to deliver the same 3.19 percent welfare gain achieves the opposite on distribution: migrants move to richer, less-distorted prefectures, agglomeration amplifies the productivity advantage of large cities, and spatial inequality rises. Migration also changes the incidence of the frictions themselves — with migration shut down, the same friction-dispersion cut yields a larger aggregate gain (3.37 percent), because people can no longer escape the most distorted prefectures, and a smaller inequality improvement.

This welfare-equivalence-with-opposite-incidence result is the analytical core of the place-based-versus-people-based debate in the Chinese context. It echoes [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]], where the welfare gain from removing hukou restrictions coexists with losses to natives in destination cities, and it frames the policy-design lesson drawn across the corpus: an efficiency-oriented spatial policy generally needs a complementary people-based or place-based transfer to keep regional inequality in check ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]; [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]). It also explains why [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] finds social-security equalization to be the more effective inequality instrument: it operates directly on the locational utility of poorer regions rather than on the factor allocation margin.

## Policy translation: Chinese spatial fiscal institutions

Read together, the five Chinese-application papers constitute a program of "policy translation" — porting the quantitative spatial toolkit onto the institutions through which Chinese fiscal policy operates. Four translations stand out. First, the *unified national market* (全国统一大市场) agenda: [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]] and [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]] both find that integration gains are large but conditional — capital and product markets must be reformed together, and land and population must move in the same direction — converting an abstract slogan into a set of complementarity conditions. Second, *land-quota trading*: [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]] argues for flexible, cross-provincial tradable construction-land quotas tied to migrant inflows, so that land follows population. Third, *hukou liberalization tied to fiscal equalization*: the welfare losses from the hukou wedge ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]) are largest where local public services are most unequal, so equalizing per-capita fiscal expenditure — the policy [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]] quantifies — is the fiscal counterpart of migration reform. Fourth, *property-tax design*: [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]] shows a local property tax financing public services can substitute for central transfers and help implement the optimal allocation of human capital.

A fifth translation concerns how policy is *evaluated*. [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]] argues that the final determinant of economic structure is population distribution rather than capital accumulation, and that aggregate convergence hides divergence in regional output levels — a warning against evaluating spatial policy on aggregate totals. The fiscal papers operationalize this by reporting income gaps, Gini coefficients, and welfare inequality alongside GDP, and the recurring pattern is that per-capita and welfare measures can move opposite to aggregate measures ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]; [[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The five-paper consensus on the unified national market is that its core content is fiscal-institutional: equalize per-capita productive and social expenditure, let land quotas trade and follow population, replace hukou-rationed access with equalized services, align local-government incentives through consumption-tax and per-capita-evaluation reform, and treat property tax as a service-financing instrument. No single instrument does all the work; the recurring failure mode is a single-market or single-instrument reform that ignores complementarities.*

The frontier beyond these static results is dynamic and political-economic. The dynamic framework of [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]] shows that convergence with migration is slow and non-monotonic, so steady-state comparisons may mislead policy; capital dynamics, intertemporal fiscal choice, and the migration–savings interaction all remain open. Politically, the local-government incentive results ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]) point to deeper reform of intergovernmental fiscal relations — expenditure assignment, revenue sharing, and official evaluation — that static models only proxy. External validity is also limited: magnitudes are calibrated to Chinese data of the 2000s and 2010s, and the OCR-degraded tables in the raw markdowns mean several point estimates should be read with caution rather than as precise benchmarks.
