# Structural Transformation and Regional Convergence

## Lead

Structural transformation — the secular reallocation of employment out of agriculture toward manufacturing and then services that accompanies economic growth — is, in a spatial economy, never purely an aggregate phenomenon. Because regions enter the process with heterogeneous sectoral specialization, the same aggregate shift lands differently across space: regions specialized in a contracting sector are disproportionately exposed to its decline, while regions that already host expanding industries benefit disproportionately from their growth. This page integrates a body of evidence spanning the first US structural transformation of 1880–1920, the postwar US services transition, the global development path, and contemporary China around a central claim: whether sectoral reallocation raises or lowers regional inequality is governed less by the reallocation itself than by the spatial forces — technology diffusion, trade, and migration — that accompany it. In the theory reviewed here, structural change is inherently urban-biased, yet in the data the initially most agricultural US regions grew fastest in wages precisely when the agricultural share was halving. The resolution of that puzzle turns on productivity catch-up through technology diffusion — a mechanism that, carried into services, global development, and the Chinese economy, determines how much of the gains from growth spread to lagging regions.

## Structural Change Is Inherently Spatial

The starting point of the modern treatment is that sectoral reallocation operates across regions whose pre-existing specialization differs. In a static quantitative spatial framework, location-specific wages, employment, and sectoral composition are jointly determined by productivity, amenities, trade costs, and land endowments, so that the same aggregate shock has heterogeneous regional incidence ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Superimposing structural change on such an economy generates a Bartik-like exposure logic: regions specialized in the sector that is shrinking are the ones most exposed to its decline, even when every region faces the same aggregate productivity path. Eckert and Peters formalize this logic in a two-sector model of the United States and show that a region's agricultural employment share is a sufficient statistic for its exposure to the agricultural decline: the wage elasticity with respect to agricultural revenue productivity falls monotonically with the local agricultural share, while the industrialization elasticity is U-shaped, largest at intermediate specialization ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]).

Because standard macroeconomic models of structural change abstract from geography, they remain silent on who gains and loses regionally; the theories reviewed here instead treat aggregate growth, sectoral reallocation, and regional development as simultaneously determined equilibrium objects ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]; [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]; [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). The spatial content is not incidental. In the 1880 United States, one-fifth of workers lived in commuting zones that were 80 percent agricultural while another fifth lived in zones that were only 10 percent agricultural, and wages in the industrialized zones were nearly twice as high — initial conditions that, combined with differential exposure, determine which regions feel the subsequent structural shift most acutely ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]).

## The Urban Bias of Sectoral Reallocation — and What Reversed It

Given the exposure structure, the sign of the spatial response is determined analytically. Eckert and Peters show that if sectoral productivity grows uniformly across space, trade is free, and population growth is spatially balanced, then a falling aggregate agricultural share necessarily produces urban-biased wage growth: rural regions are more exposed to the relatively slow-growing sector, so the same productivity growth translates into lower wage growth where agriculture looms large ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). This inherent urban bias does not require faster physical productivity growth in manufacturing than in agriculture — it can arise even when agricultural physical productivity grows faster, because revenue productivity is shaped by relative prices and by the falling expenditure share of food under non-homothetic preferences.

The first US structural transformation contradicts this prediction sharply. Between 1880 and 1920 the agricultural employment share fell from 50 to 25 percent, yet wages in the initially most agricultural commuting zones grew 1.5 to 2 percentage points per year faster than in urban areas, and the standard deviation of log wages across commuting zones fell from 0.41 to 0.22 ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). The model's analytical decomposition attributes local wage growth and industrialization to two margins — the incidence of sectoral shocks and the region's exposure to them — and implies that the reversal must have come from spatially uneven productivity growth tilted toward rural areas. The same conclusion emerges from the historical takeoff literature, where the spatial concentration of activity is itself a precondition for industrialization: Trew's calibrated model of the English Industrial Revolution finds that manufacturing takeoff occurs in the North (location 305, Lancashire) only because a geographically small region of high productivity there reaches the scale required to overcome the fixed costs of energy-intensive innovation; when the manufacturing productivity distribution is flattened, takeoff happens immediately and everywhere, and when only the London productivity peak is retained, no industrial takeoff occurs within 150 periods ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]). Geography, in short, determines not just who benefits from structural change but whether structural change takes off at all.

## Technology Diffusion and the "Benefit of Backwardness"

Across the models in this corpus, technology diffusion is the spatial force that can offset — and even reverse — the urban bias. In Eckert and Peters' framework, regions adopt technologies from each other, and the resulting law of motion for local productivity embeds a catch-up parameter per sector: adoption costs depend on a region's lagged own productivity and a distance-weighted aggregate of other regions' productivity, so that when the catch-up parameters are positive, lagging regions grow faster — a "benefit of backwardness" that produces convergence in spatial productivity levels ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). Estimating the model on 495 commuting zones between 1880 and 1920 delivers $\lambda_A = 0.73$ in agriculture and $\lambda_M = 0.28$ in manufacturing, implying that backward regions experienced faster productivity growth in both sectors. The empirical counterpart is the data's rural wage premium: wages in the initially most agricultural regions grew 1.5 to 2 percentage points per year faster than in urban areas, and the model's decomposition attributes nearly 2 percentage points of that annual rural wage growth to agricultural TFP growth alone.

The quantitative importance of this diffusion is established by a counterfactual that shuts down cross-region technology adoption while re-estimating aggregate productivity growth so that the time-series facts are unchanged. This "no catch-up" calibration still matches aggregate structural change, but its spatial predictions reverse: wage growth becomes about 0.5 percentage points per year faster in cities, rural industrialization slows sharply, and the coefficient on initial agricultural specialization in the wage-growth regression flips sign ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). The same logic operates at global scale in the dynamic model of Desmet, Nagy, and Rossi-Hansberg, where the degree of spatial diffusion enters the technology-accumulation equation and acts as a dispersion force: without any diffusion, no balanced growth path with a non-degenerate distribution of employment exists, and weaker diffusion lowers the incentive to concentrate and innovate ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]; [[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). Trew's endogenous-infrastructure model embeds the same mechanism in the historical takeoff, where the spatial distribution of transport improvements determines whether lagging industrial regions catch up ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]).

> *Cross-paper pattern:* Across the historical and global models in this collection — Eckert–Peters' catch-up parameters, Desmet–Nagy–Rossi-Hansberg's diffusion frontier, and Trew's infrastructure-mediated diffusion — technology diffusion consistently appears as the spatial force that offsets the inherent urban bias of structural change and delivers rural and lagging-region convergence. When diffusion is weak, the Bartik-exposure logic dominates and regional inequality rises; when it is strong, catch-up growth can overturn the bias entirely.

## Structural Transformation and the Take-Off of Services

Structural transformation does not end with agriculture. In the Desmet–Rossi-Hansberg framework, non-homothetic preferences with an elasticity of substitution below one imply that, as manufacturing productivity grows faster than services, the relative price of manufactured goods falls and employment shifts toward the sector with the rising relative price — services ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). Because this reallocation enlarges the scale of service-producing locations, it endogenously accelerates service-sector innovation: the calibrated model generates a service productivity take-off around the mid-1990s in the United States, accompanied by increasing spatial concentration of services and rising dispersion of land rents across metropolitan areas. This mechanism reverses the Baumol cost-disease prediction of stagnating services — rather than the low-productivity sector dragging down aggregate growth, structural transformation makes services concentrated enough for innovation to take off endogenously.

The historical models embed an analogous channel through agricultural subsistence. Stone–Geary preferences with a subsistence agricultural requirement mean that productivity gains in agriculture release labor into manufacturing, powering the earliest stages of industrialization ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]; [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]). Eckert and Peters use a richer non-homothetic class — the Price-Independent Generalized Linear (PIGL) preferences of Boppart — in which the agricultural expenditure share declines with real income and asymptotes to a positive floor; the income-elasticity channel operates alongside sectoral productivity growth in driving the sectoral reallocation ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). Across these specifications the same force is at work: the shift out of a maturing sector is driven by income effects, and its spatial footprint is shaped by where the expanding sector can agglomerate enough to innovate.

## Regional Convergence: Beta-Convergence and Its Slowdown

Where diffusion is strong, structural change and growth should produce convergence in regional incomes; where it is weak, divergence. The most direct evidence on the pace of regional convergence comes from the United States. Kleinman, Liu, and Redding measure the correlation between ten-year-ahead income growth and initial income per capita across US states and document a secular decline in this convergence coefficient across 1963–2017 — from −0.0257 in 1963–1980 to −0.0148 in 1980–2000 and to +0.0076 (statistically indistinguishable from zero) in 2000–2017 ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]). Their dynamic spatial model, in which forward-looking capital accumulation by immobile landlords interacts with migration, yields average convergence half-lives of about 20 years and a maximum around 80 years; the spectral decomposition shows that the decline in convergence is largely explained by initial conditions — the inherited gap of capital and labor from steady state — rather than by the pattern of subsequent fundamental shocks, because the empirical productivity and amenity shocks load on eigencomponents with relatively fast convergence while the steady-state gaps load on slow-moving ones ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]).

The dynamic quantitative spatial equilibrium survey applies the same half-life concept to China and finds that introducing interregional migration dramatically slows convergence: GDP and capital half-lives exceed thirty years and tend to rise over time, in contrast to the roughly decade-scale half-lives of a no-migration new-classical benchmark, because population and capital dynamics interact and slow each other's adjustment ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]). Population, not capital, ultimately determines the long-run spatial structure: capital adjusts to the distribution of people, so regional outcomes hinge on where workers can move.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The evidence on the US convergence slowdown points to initial conditions — the inherited gap of capital and labor from steady state — as the dominant explanation, with subsequent productivity and amenity shocks playing a secondary, faster-converging role ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]; [[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]). For China, the same toolkit suggests that migration frictions slow convergence so much that steady-state analysis is a poor guide to the transition path.

## Global Development Paths and the Density–Income Reversal

At the global scale, the interaction between diffusion, structural change, and migration frictions produces a striking prediction about which regions will be rich in the long run. Desmet, Nagy, and Rossi-Hansberg solve a dynamic spatial growth model on a $1^\circ \times 1^\circ$ grid (about 65,000 cells), in which firms invest in local innovation, technology diffuses across space, and migration is subject to origin- and destination-specific frictions ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). With current migration restrictions unchanged, the transition to the balanced growth path takes about 400 years, and the correlation between GDP per capita and population density — currently about −0.41 — rises to +0.65: today's dense-but-poor regions (much of sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and East Asia) become tomorrow's dense-and-rich regions, a productivity reversal in which North America and Europe fall behind in relative population and productivity terms.

Migration policy reshapes which regions benefit. With full liberalization, about 70 percent of the world's population would move across countries at impact, Europe and the eastern United States remain strong, and the present-discounted gains are large — output up 125.8 percent and welfare up 305.9 percent ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). The mechanism behind the reversal is exactly the diffusion-plus-density logic of this page: dense locations invest more in innovation, and when migration frictions trap people in dense low-productivity regions, those regions converge to the frontier; freer migration instead lets people move to already-productive, high-amenity places, aligning the future high-density regions with current high-income ones.

## Contemporary Relevance: The Services Transition, China, and Open Questions

Whether the second great sectoral transition — manufacturing to services — raises or lowers regional inequality remains the central open question, and the corpus's answer is conditional on diffusion. Eckert and Peters conjecture that today's shift toward services may be amplifying regional inequality precisely because technology appears to diffuse more slowly in the service sector than it did in agriculture and manufacturing; this is a conjecture about parameter values, not a direct test, and no paper in this collection estimates a services catch-up parameter ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]).

The Chinese evidence is indirect but suggestive. Zhao, Chen, and Fu's application of the dynamic toolkit finds that population distribution, not capital accumulation, ultimately determines the long-run spatial structure of the Chinese economy — capital adjusts to population in the long run, so regional outcomes hinge on where people can move — and that per-capita incomes converge across provinces only partially, because idiosyncratic locational preferences prevent absolute convergence ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]). Wu and You calibrate a system-of-cities model to China and show that most large cities have already surpassed their locally optimal size while remaining below their nationally optimal size, so migration restrictions that restrain city growth tend to reduce aggregate welfare even where they correct local overexpansion ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Li, Ma, and Tang quantify a complementary margin: firm-level frictions are more dispersed in poorer prefectures, and reducing that dispersion raises aggregate welfare while lowering spatial inequality, whereas migration liberalization with unchanged frictions raises welfare but worsens spatial inequality because people move toward already-large, less-distorted cities ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Whether the manufacturing-to-services transition is amplifying regional inequality is conjectural in this corpus. Eckert and Peters flag weaker diffusion in services as the likely culprit, but no direct estimate of a services catch-up parameter exists here. The Chinese applications are consistent with the diffusion mechanism — spatial inequality falls when lagging regions can catch up — but none of them isolates the services channel, and the historical results' external validity for contemporary service economies remains untested.
