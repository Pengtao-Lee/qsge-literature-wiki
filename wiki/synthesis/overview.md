# Overview

This wiki synthesises a single-paradigm literature built on quantitative spatial and trade general-equilibrium (QSE) models. Its twenty-six papers all descend from one toolkit — Eaton and Kortum's (2002) Ricardian model with Fréchet-distributed productivity and the structural gravity equation it generates — extended over two decades into a general theory of where economic activity locates, how that location responds to costs and policies, and how much aggregate welfare and inequality are shaped by space. The corpus divides into two bodies: a general apparatus (quantitative trade with input-output linkages, spatial equilibrium with costly goods trade and mobile factors, agglomeration and the internal structure of cities, transport infrastructure, and dynamic spatial growth with structural transformation) and a large applied sub-literature that ports that apparatus onto China's institutions — the hukou registration system, centrally allocated land quotas, fiscal expenditure equalization, the unified national market, and the energy-to-carbon dual-control transition. This page maps the corpus along five axes, each developed in dedicated pages, lays out the tensions between the threads, and describes the literature's frontier: policy translation of quantitative spatial equilibrium to Chinese institutions.

## The quantitative trade / spatial-equilibrium backbone

The corpus's foundation is Eaton and Kortum's (2002) Ricardian model, in which each location draws productivity for each good from a Fréchet distribution with location parameter $T_i$ (absolute advantage) and dispersion parameter $\theta$ (comparative advantage). Trade follows a structural gravity equation in which the share of importer $n$'s spending from source $i$ is $\pi_{ni} = T_i(c_i d_{ni})^{-\theta}/\Phi_n$, where $d_{ni}$ are iceberg trade costs and $\Phi_n$ summarizes world technology and barriers. The dispersion parameter doubles as the trade elasticity, estimated at a central value of 8.28 with bounds from 3.60 (wage data) to 12.86 (price data), and it scales the welfare gains from trade: a country's real income is a function of its domestic expenditure share, making the "home share" a sufficient statistic for gains ([[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

Two extensions turn this single-sector trade model into the quantitative spatial equilibrium toolkit. Caliendo and Parro (2015) add many sectors, sector-specific trade elasticities, and input-output linkages, solving the model in relative changes ("exact-hat algebra") from base-year data alone. Because sectoral input-bundle costs depend on the price indices of input-supplying sectors, trade-cost changes propagate through the production network; welfare effects are on average 71% lower in a one-sector model than in the full model, and sectoral elasticities range from 0.37 to 51.08 ([[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]]). The trade framework was then fused with labor mobility across many asymmetric locations. Allen and Arkolakis (2014) characterize the equilibrium of a continuous-space economy with costly trade and mobile labor as a system of integral equations, with existence and uniqueness governed by a balance condition on agglomeration and dispersion forces, and show the model can be inverted to recover unobserved productivities and amenities from observed wages and populations ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). Redding and Rossi-Hansberg's (2017) survey codifies the resulting menu of building blocks — preferences, production technology, and frictions on the movement of goods, ideas, and people — and the properties that make the framework tractable: gravity equations for all bilateral flows, exact inversion, and exact-hat counterfactuals from initial-equilibrium data alone ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

Redding (2016) supplies the refinement that matters for the rest of the corpus: with labor mobile, welfare gains from a trade-cost change depend jointly on changes in domestic trade shares *and* in population, because the price of the immobile factor (land) adjusts and locations with better access must pay higher real incomes to attract heterogeneous-taste workers. The same model can be built on constant-returns or increasing-returns microfoundations, which predict identically when labor is immobile but diverge once workers can relocate ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** The entire corpus — international trade, within-country trade, commuting, migration, and internal city structure — rests on a single Fréchet/gravity structure. The three microfoundations (Armington CES, Eaton–Kortum Fréchet, Melitz firm heterogeneity) deliver the same aggregate gravity equation and are isomorphic for many predictions, differing mainly in the structural interpretation of the elasticity and in predictions that involve firm entry ([[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]).

## Agglomeration, density, and internal city structure

A second thread asks what happens *within* the distribution of locations: why economic activity concentrates into cities and dense blocks. The organizing quantities are agglomeration forces (productivity and amenity externalities from surrounding density) and dispersion forces (inelastic land, commuting costs, congestion, and idiosyncratic taste heterogeneity). The canonical evidence comes from the quantitative urban model estimated on Berlin's division and reunification by Ahlfeldt, Redding, Sturm, and Wolf (2015), which yields a gravity equation for commuting flows and structural estimates of the elasticity of productivity with respect to workplace-employment density of about 0.07 and of amenities with respect to resident density of about 0.15, both highly localized in space ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Tsivanidis' (2026) study of Bogotá's TransMilenio bus rapid transit extends the model to a developing-city setting with multiple skill groups and transit modes, estimating productivity spillovers and constructing residence and firm commuting market access (RCMA/FCMA) as the objects through which transport shocks reallocate activity within a city ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

Across cities, commuting is the margin that ties labor markets together: commuting flows obey a gravity equation far more distance-sensitive than goods trade (a distance elasticity around −4.4 for commuting versus −1.3 for goods), and the openness of a county's commuting market determines how local employment responds to labor-demand shocks ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The balance of agglomeration and dispersion forces also determines whether spatial equilibria are unique, whether agglomeration self-reinforces into multiple equilibria, and how large a city should be; in China, Wu and You (2025) find that most large cities are already too large from a local perspective but too small from a national one, so that the hukou system both corrects and distorts city size at once ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Density is also a growth force: Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg's (2014) spatial development model makes innovation a scale effect of density, so agglomeration and technology interact dynamically ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]).

## Transport infrastructure and welfare

The most policy-relevant application of the QSE toolkit is the welfare evaluation of transport infrastructure, and the corpus now spans every scale of the question. The unifying result is that *market access is the causal channel*: a transport improvement raises access to goods, jobs, and workers, shifting prices, wages, employment, and land values, and the general-equilibrium gains are systematically larger than the direct time-savings benefit. Removing the U.S. Interstate Highway System would lower welfare by 1.1–1.4% in Allen and Arkolakis' model ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]); a transport improvement in Redding's (2016) synthetic grid economy raises common welfare by roughly 9% while reallocating up to about 60% of population, so reduced-form infrastructure regressions cannot separate reallocation from creation of activity ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]); and for Bogotá's TransMilenio, the standard value-of-travel-time-savings measure captures only about 52% of the full general-equilibrium welfare gain ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

The same logic holds through different channels: reducing commuting costs raises US welfare by about 3.3% for the median observed reduction ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]); removing the automobile from Berlin would cut city employment by about 14%, showing how transport interacts with agglomeration ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]); and China's network-infrastructure build-out — the digital analogue of roads — lowered inter-regional trade costs by roughly 3% by 2019 and raised aggregate welfare by about 10%, with about half the gain routed through input-output linkages ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). Historically, infrastructure is itself endogenous: Trew's models of the First Industrial Revolution show that the timing and *spatial distribution* of transport investment determine whether it accelerates or delays takeoff, and that lower transport costs can slow innovation by diluting agglomeration ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]; [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]). The welfare and incidence results across these settings are developed in [[transport-infrastructure-and-urban-welfare]].

## Structural transformation, technology diffusion, and regional convergence

A fourth thread connects spatial equilibrium to growth and development. Structural change is inherently spatial: sectoral reallocation operates across regions with heterogeneous specialization, so the same aggregate shift produces regional winners and losers. Eckert and Peters (2022) show that during the first US structural transformation (1880–1920, when the agricultural employment share fell from 50% to 25%) wages rose *fastest* in the most agricultural regions — the opposite of the standard "exposure" intuition — because technology diffusion let lagging rural regions catch up; their estimated catch-up parameters imply that without diffusion, rural America would have fallen further behind and industrialization would have been slower ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]). Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg (2014) document the modern mirror image: the take-off of services productivity in the mid-1990s, the spatial concentration of services, and the rise in land-rent dispersion ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]).

At the global scale, Desmet, Nagy, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) embed Fréchet trade, innovation, and migration frictions in a $1^\circ\times1^\circ$ dynamic spatial growth model, finding a transition to the balanced-growth path lasting roughly 400 years and a reversal in the density-income correlation from about −0.41 to +0.65: today's dense poor regions become tomorrow's dense rich regions. Fully liberalizing migration would raise welfare by about 306% (amenity-adjusted real GDP) and output by 126% in present-discounted value, with 70% of the world population moving across countries at impact ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). Within countries, convergence has been slowing: Kleinman, Liu, and Redding (2023) show that the correlation between income growth and initial income across US states fell from negative to roughly zero between 1963 and 2017, with average convergence half-lives of about 20 years, driven by the interaction of capital accumulation and migration frictions ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]). China converges even more slowly once migration enters the dynamic QSE framework, with GDP-convergence half-lives exceeding 30 years and population distribution — not capital — determining the long-run spatial structure ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]). The spatial-takeoff literature rounds out the thread: the initial concentration of manufacturing productivity was necessary for England's industrial takeoff, and energy costs shaped where innovation first occurred ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]).

## Chinese applications: hukou, land, fiscal, and environmental policy

A large applied sub-literature ports the QSE toolkit onto China's institutions and asks how much of the country's growth and inequality is attributable to spatial frictions. Tombe and Zhu's (2019) canonical model of internal and international trade with migration finds that reductions in internal trade and migration costs account for about 28% of China's aggregate labor-productivity growth between 2000 and 2005, versus about 8% from reductions in international trade costs — an internal-frictions-dominant result that reframes the "export-led growth" narrative ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Subsequent papers each isolate a specific institutional margin. Wu and You (2025) model the hukou system as a fiscal wedge between natives and migrants, estimating that removing all internal migration restrictions would raise average GDP by 13.6% and welfare by 4.7%, while the largest cities are locally too large but nationally too small ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Zhao and Chen (2021) analyze the central allocation of new urban land quotas, which tilted toward the interior after 2003, documenting an efficiency-equity tension: allocating land to the efficient coast maximizes GDP but widens regional income gaps, and the actual allocation sits between the extremes ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). Zhou and Gong (2024) find that equalizing productive fiscal expenditure raises output and welfare together (output +1.5%, welfare +1.6%), while equalizing social-security spending raises welfare more (about +2.7%) at a small output cost (−0.6%) ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]).

Other papers examine capital, people, and the environment. Zhao, Wang, Dai, and Chen (2025) model cross-regional investment distortions under local-government incentives alongside product-market segmentation, finding the two contribute roughly equally (about 45% and 44%) to the "capital flowing against returns" pattern and that single-market reform alone cannot reverse it ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]). Liu, Wang, and Chen (2024) show that with cross-skill productivity and public-service spillovers, redistributing from high-skill, high-wage cities to low-skill, low-wage cities reaches the Pareto frontier, and that a property tax partially substitutes for spatial transfers ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]). Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) estimate that reducing within-prefecture misallocation and liberalizing migration achieve similar aggregate welfare gains but have opposite effects on spatial inequality ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]), while Ma and Tang (2020) find, in a Melitz model with firm entry, that destination cities *benefit* from migrant inflows — the opposite of the Tombe–Zhu result without an extensive margin ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). On the environmental margin, Luo and Luo (2025) build a quantitative spatial general-equilibrium model with trade, migration, and carbon constraints, finding that command-style carbon control produces a welfare loss (−0.114%) roughly two and a half times that of a carbon market (−0.046%), with carbon leakage exceeding 20% of the domestic reduction under every national instrument ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). Where the underlying Chinese raw texts are MinerU-VLM conversions, a few headline magnitudes carry OCR-uncertainty flags on the individual pages.

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** Across the hukou, land-quota, fiscal-equalization, and carbon-policy papers, the same efficiency-equity tension recurs: removing a spatial friction raises aggregate output and welfare, while the equalizing reform that narrows regional gaps usually trades away some efficiency — and the optimal policy is a *combination* (land reform with hukou reform, market integration with labor co-location, carbon pricing with revenue redistribution) rather than any single instrument ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]; [[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]; [[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]).

## Tensions across the threads

Read together, the threads pull in different directions. The most visible disagreement is over the *magnitude of the gains from removing migration frictions*: Tombe and Zhu's counterfactual of lowering migration costs to US-comparable levels implies welfare gains of roughly 46%, whereas Wu and You's hukou-as-fiscal-wedge model implies a welfare gain of only 4.7% from removing all internal migration restrictions. The gap reflects substance, not arbitrariness — different counterfactuals (US-mobility costs versus zero hukou wedge), different treatment of agglomeration and city-size externalities, and different welfare concepts. A second tension concerns *reallocation versus welfare*: population reallocations can be an order of magnitude larger than common welfare gains, so evaluations keyed to where activity moves can misstate who actually benefits ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]). A third concerns the *welfare effects of migration on destination cities*: without an extensive margin, Tombe and Zhu find migrants depress destination real wages, while Ma and Tang, with endogenous firm entry, find all destination cities benefit — the sign of the local effect turns on whether firm entry is modeled ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). Finally, the *constant-versus-increasing-returns* distinction, innocuous when labor is immobile, changes counterfactual predictions for population and wages once workers can relocate ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]). These are scope conditions rather than contradictions, and the wiki's debate pages, notably [[government-and-urbanization]] and the migration-welfare mechanism pages, keep them explicit.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The mobility-gains magnitude disagreement is real and unresolved. The 46%-versus-4.7% spread reflects model differences (agglomeration and city-size externalities, the intensive margin of the hukou wedge) more than measurement error; the wiki's current best reading is that the aggregate gains from migration reform are large but smaller than the earliest QSE estimates, and that distributional consequences for destination-city natives are the binding political constraint ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]).

## Policy translation: quantitative spatial equilibrium adapted to Chinese institutions

The frontier of this corpus is its self-identifying sub-literature: a set of papers that take the QSE toolkit — Fréchet mobility, model inversion, exact-hat counterfactuals — and point it at China-specific institutions rather than at the abstract space of the theory papers. The recurring template is the same across every paper. The model nests a Chinese policy wedge inside the standard spatial structure: the hukou system enters as a migration cost or a fiscal wedge in Tombe–Zhu and Wu–You ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]); centrally allocated construction-land quotas enter as a spatial supply shifter in Zhao–Chen ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]); local-government investment and trade-protection incentives enter as wedges on capital and goods flows in Zhao–Wang–Dai–Chen ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]); fiscal expenditure and the property tax enter as spatially differentiated public goods and taxes in Zhou–Gong and Liu–Wang–Chen ([[zhou-gong-2024-public-expenditure-equalization]]; [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]); and the energy-to-carbon dual-control constraint enters as a compliance cost on energy use in Luo–Luo ([[luo-luo-2025-carbon-control-spatial-transmission]]). The dynamic-QSE survey of Zhao, Chen, and Fu (2022) explicitly frames the "new development pattern" and the unified national market as the agenda to which this toolkit is being applied ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

The shared method explains why this thread is a frontier rather than a collection of case studies. Because each paper solves for general equilibrium, the policy conclusions are statements about *spatial allocation*: hukou reform is worth more when land quotas move with people; fiscal equalization can raise output and welfare simultaneously only when it corrects misallocation rather than merely redistributing; market integration must pair capital-market and product-market reform or it fails to reverse capital flowing against returns; carbon pricing beats command control because the latter melts carbon revenue into deadweight loss. The same toolkit also carries the literature forward into new factor markets — data, modeled as a non-rival factor whose market segmentation leaves large gains on the table in a unified data market ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The Chinese-applied sub-literature is the corpus's most active frontier and its weakest-evidence zone at the same time: it is where the strongest policy claims are made (hukou and land reform raising GDP by double digits; market integration reversing capital misallocation) but where the underlying raw texts are the least clean (OCR conversions), the corpora are single-country, and the identification of structural elasticities is thinnest. The wiki treats these magnitudes as model-conditional point estimates rather than established facts.

## Open questions

- **Dynamic multi-sector models.** Caliendo–Parro style input-output amplification has not yet been fully combined with forward-looking capital accumulation and innovation; the corpus currently separates static multi-sector trade from dynamic single-sector growth ([[caliendo-parro-2015-trade-welfare-nafta]]; [[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]).
- **Services diffusion.** Whether technology diffuses as readily in services as in agriculture and manufacturing is untested; Eckert and Peters conjecture weaker services diffusion could amplify regional inequality ([[eckert-peters-2022-spatial-structural-change]]).
- **Identification of elasticities.** The trade, commuting, and migration elasticities are the single most important inputs to every counterfactual, yet they are estimated from cross-sections or a few natural experiments, and the calibration of Chinese applications reuses a small set of values (θ = 4, migration elasticity around 1.5) across papers ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]; [[eaton-kortum-2002-technology-geography-trade]]).
- **Political economy.** The fiscal and hukou papers show that local governments have incentives to distort spatial allocation, but who pays for reform and how it becomes incentive-compatible is only beginning to be modeled ([[zhao-wang-dai-chen-2025-cross-regional-investment-distortions]]).
- **External validity.** City-level results from Berlin and Bogotá, and historical results from England, are each identified from one setting; whether they transfer to other cities and eras is open ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]).
