Between 2000 and 2007, China experienced simultaneous reductions in internal trade costs — driven by the dismantling of inter-provincial trade barriers, state-owned enterprise reforms, and transport infrastructure expansion — and in migration costs, following the relaxation of the hukou household registration system. These cost declines fueled the largest internal migration in human history and a rapid increase in inter-provincial trade. Using an Eaton-Kortum quantitative spatial equilibrium model with endogenous migration, Tombe and Zhu (2019) find that the combined reduction in internal trade and migration costs accounts for roughly 28 percent of China's aggregate labor productivity growth between 2000 and 2005, while external trade cost reductions associated with WTO accession account for only 8 percent. This finding fundamentally challenges the "export-led growth" narrative for China and redirects attention to the productivity gains from internal market integration and labor mobility. Subsequent work using a Melitz-based framework ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]) reaches complementary results for aggregate welfare but reveals sharply different predictions for the local distribution of migration's benefits, a tension with direct implications for hukou reform policy.

## Background: Internal Barriers in 2000

At the turn of the century, China's internal economic integration was severely constrained by overlapping frictions in goods and labor markets. Inter-provincial trade costs were substantial: the trade-weighted average cost of shipping goods between Chinese provinces was comparable to or exceeded the cost of shipping goods between China and many foreign trading partners ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Home shares of provincial expenditure — the fraction of spending allocated to locally produced goods — exceeded 80 percent in most interior provinces, reflecting a combination of local protectionist policies, underdeveloped transport infrastructure, and the legacy of planned-economy fragmentation.

On the labor side, the hukou system imposed the dominant barrier. Each Chinese citizen was assigned an agricultural or non-agricultural registration tied to a specific administrative unit. Changing either the category or location of one's hukou required government approval, which was extremely difficult to obtain. Workers who migrated without changing their hukou faced restricted access to local public services, higher costs for healthcare and children's education, and — prior to the 2003 administrative reforms — the risk of arrest and deportation ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). As a consequence, despite a 4:1 ratio in real GDP per worker between the top and bottom five provinces, only 4.2 percent of total employment consisted of inter-provincial migrants in 2000. Within-province rural-to-urban migration was more common, at 14.3 percent of employment, but still modest relative to the agricultural productivity gap, which averaged roughly 4:1 within provinces as well.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *The coexistence of high internal trade barriers and severe migration restrictions in China around 2000 reflects a dual-friction structure common to many large developing economies, but the institutional form — a household registration system governing social service access combined with local government market protection — is specific to China's transitional institutional environment ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).*

## Quantitative Framework: Eaton-Kortum with Migration

The core decomposition results rely on a two-sector, multi-region general equilibrium model that extends the Eaton and Kortum (2002) framework to include internal trade, international trade, and worker migration ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The model features N Chinese provinces plus a rest-of-world region, each with agricultural and non-agricultural sectors. Productivity in each location-sector follows a Fréchet distribution with dispersion parameter $\theta = 4$, governing the elasticity of trade flows to trade costs. Goods trade is subject to iceberg costs $\tau_{ni}^j$, which are estimated from observed trade shares using the Head-Ries index.

Worker mobility is the key extension. Workers differ in idiosyncratic location-sector preferences, drawn from a Fréchet distribution with dispersion parameter $\kappa = 1.5$, estimated from bilateral migration flows. Migration costs $\mu_{ni}^{jk}$ are modeled as flow costs — recurring utility penalties rather than one-time fixed costs — reflecting the institutional reality of the hukou system, where migrants must permanently forgo access to local public services. The collective ownership of land creates an additional barrier: workers who leave their hukou location lose claims to land income, captured by a "rebate rate" $\delta_{nn}^{jj}$ that raises the effective cost of migration. This institutional realism is essential for matching the observed migration patterns, particularly the predominance of young, temporary migrants.

## Estimated Cost Declines (2000–2007)

Both trade and migration costs fell substantially over the first half of the 2000s. Internal trade costs declined by 10–15 percent on average between 2002 and 2007, while external trade costs fell by nearly 10 percent in non-agriculture and roughly 25 percent in agriculture ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The internal cost declines reflected a combination of deliberate policy reforms — including a 2001 State Council directive prohibiting local market protections, SOE reforms that reduced local governments' incentives for protectionism, and major investments in highway infrastructure — though the model does not separately identify the contribution of each factor.

Migration costs declined even more dramatically. By 2005, average migration costs fell to 82 percent of their 2000 level, with between-province migration costs dropping to 61 percent of their initial level. In levels, the average cost of moving from rural to urban within a province in 2000 was equivalent to shrinking one's real income by a factor of nearly 3, while between-province agriculture-to-non-agriculture moves carried costs averaging 25 times income — an order of magnitude higher ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The decline was driven by the 2003 national administrative reform that streamlined temporary residence permits, targeted hukou reforms in Beijing, Shanghai, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, and Shandong, and the expansion of the highway network that reduced the effective distance cost of moving.

## Decomposition Results: Internal versus External

The headline quantitative result of Tombe and Zhu (2019) is that reductions in internal trade and migration costs together account for roughly 28 percent of China's aggregate labor productivity growth between 2000 and 2005 (15.8 percentage points out of 57.1 percent total growth), while external trade cost reductions account for only 8 percent (4.5 percentage points). This result is robust across all possible orders of introducing the cost changes into the counterfactual decomposition, with small standard deviations across permutations.

The disproportionate importance of internal relative to external trade cost reductions stems from a simple structural fact: most Chinese provinces trade substantially more with other Chinese provinces than with foreign countries. In 2002, the average province's spending share on goods from other Chinese provinces was roughly 10–20 percent, compared to 2–14 percent on foreign imports, and the increase in internal trade shares over the period was nearly seven times larger than the increase in external trade shares ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Because the direct welfare effect of a trade cost reduction is proportional to the initial volume of trade, the larger base of internal trade delivers larger gains from a comparable proportional cost reduction. This mechanism is reinforced by input-output linkages: intermediate inputs magnify the gains from trade cost reductions, and when the model shuts down intermediate inputs, the contribution of internal trade cost reductions falls from 11.2 percent to 3.1 percent of GDP per worker.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The dominance of internal over external trade cost reductions in explaining China's growth during the WTO accession period has been broadly accepted in the QSE literature and is consistent with similar findings for other large economies (Caliendo, Parro, and Tsyvinski 2017). The result has shifted policy attention toward internal market integration as a growth strategy.*

## Gains from Migration Reallocation

The estimated reduction in migration costs generates substantial reallocation of labor from agriculture to non-agriculture and from interior to coastal provinces. In response to the measured cost declines, the stock of within-province migrants increased by roughly 15 percent and between-province migrants by roughly 82 percent ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The national share of labor in agriculture declined by 3 percentage points. Approximately 90 percent of the aggregate productivity gains from migration come from agriculture-to-non-agriculture reallocation, reflecting the large and persistent sectoral productivity gap.

Aggregate labor productivity rises by 4.8 percent and aggregate welfare by 11.1 percent from the migration cost reductions alone. The larger welfare gain reflects the direct utility benefit of lower migration costs for migrants themselves, in addition to the productivity gain from reallocation. Importantly, these gains are substantially smaller than the 10.8 percent productivity gain implied by a back-of-the-envelope calculation that ignores diminishing returns. Land as a fixed factor dampens the productivity gain from 4.8 to 5.3 percent when removed, and regional comparative advantage further reduces it to 6.5 percent when goods are assumed to be perfect substitutes. The full model's lower estimate highlights the importance of general equilibrium forces in migration analysis.

## Remaining Scope for Reform

Despite the substantial declines, internal trade and migration costs in China remain high relative to developed-economy benchmarks, implying large potential gains from further reform. Lowering China's average internal trade costs to the levels measured in Canada would generate welfare gains of roughly 12.5 percent in real GDP and 16.3 percent in aggregate welfare ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Reducing migration costs to levels consistent with US inter-state mobility rates — where roughly one-third of workers live outside their state of birth — would increase real GDP per worker by 12.8 percent and welfare by a dramatic 45.6 percent, as the direct welfare effect of lower migration costs compounds the productivity gain.

Land reform offers a third channel. Under China's collective land ownership system, migrants forfeit the returns to land in their home registration area. Allowing workers to retain land income regardless of residence — equivalent to private land ownership with a functioning rental market — would increase the stock of migrants dramatically: within-province migrants nearly double and between-province migrants rise by 38 percent ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The welfare gain from land reform alone is nearly 12 percent, though real GDP per worker falls by 2.4 percent as workers move disproportionately from high-GDP urban areas to lower-GDP rural areas, revealing an important distinction between welfare and measured output effects.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The estimated potential gains from further hukou reform in Tombe and Zhu (2019) are consistent with the broader range estimated in the optimal city size literature, including Wu and You (2025), who find that most large Chinese cities are above their locally optimal population but below the nationally optimal level.*

## Modeling Contrast: Eaton-Kortum versus Melitz

The Tombe and Zhu (2019) framework builds on the Eaton-Kortum model, which assumes perfect competition, constant returns to scale, and — crucially — no firm entry and exit margin. This modeling choice has important implications for the predicted local welfare effects of migration. In the Eaton-Kortum structure, inflows of workers depress local nominal wages through increased labor supply, and because there is no endogenous increase in the number of firms or product varieties, the wage reduction translates directly into lower local real income. The aggregate gains from migration in this framework come entirely from the reallocation of labor across space and sectors, not from local variety or scale effects.

Ma and Tang (2020) develop an alternative framework based on the Melitz (2003) model, featuring monopolistic competition, heterogeneous firms, fixed entry costs, and endogenous firm entry and exit. In this structure, migration inflows have two countervailing local effects. On the one hand, they depress nominal wages and increase congestion, as in the Eaton-Kortum framework. On the other hand, they expand local market size, induce entry of new firms, increase product variety, and lower the ideal price index. The net local welfare effect is theoretically ambiguous. In the quantitative application to 279 Chinese prefecture-level cities, Ma and Tang (2020) find that the variety channel dominates: all 40 cities receiving net population inflows experience higher real wages and welfare, with an average real income gain of 4.7 percent and welfare gain of 4.0 percent. When they shut down the firm entry margin — thereby replicating the Eaton-Kortum structure within their model — the local impacts of migration turn negative, confirming that the modeling choice is the decisive factor.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *The Eaton-Kortum versus Melitz contrast is one of the most consequential modeling choices in the applied QSE literature for China, as it reverses the sign of migration's local welfare effect and therefore inverts the policy implications for hukou liberalization. Resolving this tension is an active frontier.*

## Frontier: QSE Policy Translation for China

The Tombe-Zhu framework has been extended and refined along several dimensions that deepen its relevance for Chinese policy design. Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) introduce firm-level output and labor frictions into a multi-region Melitz framework with migration, showing that within-prefecture misallocation of resources across firms interacts meaningfully with migration flows. They find that reducing the dispersion of firm-level frictions increases aggregate welfare by 3.19 percent (for a 0.01 reduction in standard deviations), and crucially, that migration alleviates the aggregate impact of these micro-frictions — people leave heavily distorted regions for less distorted ones — but worsens spatial inequality. This extension demonstrates that the gains from migration liberalization and the gains from reducing within-region misallocation are substitutes for aggregate welfare but have opposite effects on spatial equity.

The post-2005 expansion of digital infrastructure has further reduced internal trade costs through channels not captured in the 2000–2005 period studied by Tombe and Zhu. The construction of fiber-optic networks and the development of data markets create new mechanisms for spatial integration — data as a non-rival factor can reduce coordination costs across regions and enable new forms of inter-provincial trade in services ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]). Network infrastructure improvements continue to lower the effective distance between Chinese cities, complementing the earlier wave of highway expansion ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). These developments suggest that the internal trade cost reductions documented for 2000–2007 may have continued, with implications for the ongoing policy push toward a "unified domestic market."

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The QSE framework for China has matured from a single-model decomposition exercise into a family of extended models incorporating firm heterogeneity, dynamic migration, infrastructure network effects, and non-rival data factors. The challenge for policy translation is to determine which modeling assumptions best capture the specific mechanism targeted by a given reform — Eaton-Kortum for aggregate reallocation effects, Melitz for local welfare and variety effects, and Li-Ma-Tang for firm-level distortion interactions.*
