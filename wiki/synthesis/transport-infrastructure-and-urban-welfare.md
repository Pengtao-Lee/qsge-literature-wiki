# Transport Infrastructure and Urban Welfare

Transport infrastructure is the single most policy-relevant application of the quantitative spatial equilibrium (QSE) toolkit, and the corpus assembled here spans every scale of the question: the U.S. Interstate Highway System in a continuous-space model, a transport improvement in a synthetic grid economy with mobile labor, Bogotá's TransMilenio bus rapid transit within a single city, the removal of the automobile from Berlin, commuting-cost reductions across U.S. counties, endogenous infrastructure in the First Industrial Revolution, and China's network and digital infrastructure. The unifying answer is threefold. First, the causal channel through which transport investment raises welfare is market access — the general-equilibrium reorganization of where people live, where firms produce, and what goods trade where — rather than the direct saving of travel time. Second, standard cost-benefit measures built on time savings or direct transport-cost reductions capture only part of the welfare gain, because the rest arrives through reallocation, agglomeration, and trade linkages that those measures miss. Third, the gains are heterogeneous: they depend on the characteristics and centrality of the locations connected, on who can substitute away from costly commutes, and on whether the activity that appears in one place is created or merely moved from another.

## Market Access as the Causal Channel

The mechanism common to every scale is market access. A transport improvement lowers the cost of reaching jobs, workers, and suppliers, and in general equilibrium this shifts the price index, wages, employment, and land prices across space — effects that accrue even to locations not directly on the new infrastructure.

The canonical statement comes from [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]], whose continuous-space model of the U.S. economy estimates trade costs from the actual transportation network and computes the counterfactual removal of the Interstate Highway System. The price index rises most in the Rocky Mountains — the most remote region — while California and the Eastern Seaboard, with better alternative modes and closer production, lose least; population redistributes accordingly. Welfare depends on the change in the share of expenditure on locally produced goods, a sufficient statistic for a location's accessibility to the rest of the economy.

Within cities, Tsivanidis operationalizes market access as commuter market access, decomposed into a residential component (RCMA, residents' access to jobs) and a firm component (FCMA, firms' access to workers). TransMilenio increased access to jobs most in Bogotá's outskirts and firms' access to workers most in the center; the model predicts — and the data confirm — that changes in these two access terms, together with reduced-form elasticities of population, employment, and floorspace prices to them, are sufficient statistics for the change in the entire distribution of activity across the city ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). At the county scale, [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]] shows commuting openness is itself a margin of infrastructure policy: reductions in the ease of commuting between counties — the empirical counterpart of road and transit investment — raise aggregate welfare by around 3.3% for the median observed improvement, large relative to the gains from opening the closed economy to international trade.

*Across the corpus the framing is the same: infrastructure is not a local treatment but a change in the access of every location to every other, and the welfare object is the general-equilibrium response to that change ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).*

## Time Savings vs. General Equilibrium

The benchmark against which all of these results are measured is the value of travel time saved (VTTS): the standard practice multiplies the reduction in travel time by the value of time and sums it up. The corpus shows this first-order measure is systematically incomplete. Tsivanidis provides the sharpest statement: an application of the envelope theorem to the social planner's problem in an efficient economy shows that the elasticity of welfare to a transit change is exactly proportional to a weighted average of time savings, so in an efficient equilibrium with an infinitesimal shock the VTTS is the correct first-order welfare effect. In Bogotá, however, the VTTS delivers a welfare gain of 1.25% against 2.39% for the full general-equilibrium model — the VTTS captures only about 52% of the total, with roughly one quarter of the gap approximation error from the finite shock and three quarters due to amenity and productivity externalities; Tsivanidis rejects the hypothesis that the VTTS accounts for the whole gain ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). The Interstate-Highway counterfactual points the same way: the welfare loss from removing the system (1.1–1.4%) comes substantially from general-equilibrium price-index and reallocation effects rather than from direct time savings alone ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). *Across Tsivanidis, Redding, and Allen–Arkolakis, general-equilibrium and indirect effects are roughly half or more of measured infrastructure welfare, so a cost-benefit analysis that values only time savings understates the returns to transport investment ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]; [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]).*

## Reallocation vs. Creation of Activity

A second, related distinction separates the movement of existing activity across space from the creation of new activity, and reduced-form infrastructure regressions — comparing locations directly treated by a new road or railroad to untreated locations — cannot tell the two apart. In Redding's grid economy, the transport improvement raises the common level of expected utility by around 9% while population reallocations range up to roughly 60% — reallocations are large relative to welfare, echoing Fogel's point that large-scale reallocation need not imply welfare gains of the same magnitude. Because expected utility is equalized across locations, the welfare effect on treated locations is shared with the economy as a whole, and the true welfare treatment effect is zero: the reduced-form effects the model generates — positive for population, wages, land rents, and even for an "immobile welfare" measure computed under the false assumption of immobile labor — mask this, and an analyst using that measure would estimate a treatment effect of 0.2179, an order of magnitude larger than the truth ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]).

This is the general-equilibrium-treatment-effect critique of infrastructure evaluation. Treated locations gain population and wages partly at the expense of untreated locations and partly through genuine gains in access; only a structural model that separates the market-access channel from the reallocation channel can distinguish creation from movement. Tsivanidis makes the same point within a city, where the general-equilibrium adjustment of commuting and land and labor markets is the mechanism through which transit gains are realized, not a nuisance to be differenced out ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Reduced-form infrastructure regressions cannot separate reallocation from creation. When welfare — rather than local population or output — is the object, a structural model with mobile factors and market access is required; the QSE results imply the ratio of reallocation to net welfare creation can be as large as six or seven to one ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

## Infrastructure, Density, and Agglomeration Feedbacks

Transport investment interacts with agglomeration, and the interaction runs in both directions: infrastructure that raises access to dense centers can strengthen agglomeration economies, but lower transport costs can also weaken the density that generates agglomeration, with dynamic consequences that are often counterintuitive.

The Berlin evidence makes the density channel concrete. Ahlfeldt, Redding, Sturm, and Wolf estimate a quantitative urban model on the natural experiment of Berlin's division and reunification, in which the loss and restoration of access to the pre-war central business district shifted land prices, workplace, and residence employment exactly as the model predicts. Their out-of-sample counterfactual removes the automobile: if travel times were those of public transport alone, total city employment and output would fall by around 14% and 12%, with floor prices declining by 20% and locations becoming less specialized. The commuting technology itself — the infrastructure that lets people live apart from where they work — is what allows cities to realize the productivity of their densest locations ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

TransMilenio shows the same feedbacks within a developing city, raising productivity in newly accessible employment locations and reshaping amenities through changes in neighborhood composition — the externalities behind most of the VTTS gap ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

But the relationship between lower transport costs and agglomeration is not monotonic. Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg show that although increases in transport costs lead to the standard static losses familiar from trade models, they also generate dynamic gains by producing denser areas that, together with a scale effect in innovation, imply faster growth ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). Trew finds the same tension in the First Industrial Revolution: higher transport costs encourage the spatial concentration that lets firms reach the scale required to overcome the fixed costs of innovation, so a reduction in transport costs can actually delay the industrial takeoff ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** A transport-cost/agglomeration tension runs through the growth models. In Desmet–Rossi-Hansberg and Trew, lower transport costs weaken density and slow the innovation density supports, so static welfare gains can be partially offset by slower dynamic growth. In the static urban models of Ahlfeldt et al. and Tsivanidis, by contrast, density feedbacks amplify the static gains. The sign depends on whether the density channel operates through static productivity spillovers (amplifying) or scale-dependent innovation (potentially dampening) ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]; [[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

## Distributional and Incidence Effects

Who captures the gains from transport infrastructure is as important as their total size, and the corpus shows that the incidence is not what simple intuition suggests.

In Bogotá, the poor rely most heavily on public transit — before TransMilenio, low-educated workers were about 23% more likely than the rich to use buses rather than cars, and buses were 36% slower. Yet the full model shows that welfare inequality actually rises by a mild 0.59% because of the BRT. Two forces favor the high-skilled. First, incidence depends on the ability to substitute away from costly commutes: high-skilled workers have more inelastic demand for particular home and work locations (a lower commuting elasticity), so they bear more of the costs of long commutes and benefit more when those commutes are shortened. Second, the large shift in the supply of low-skilled labor across the city lowers low-skilled wages. These forces offset the direct benefit the poor receive from using the system more ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

The pattern generalizes. Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg show that the elasticity of local employment to a labor-demand shock varies from around 0.5 to 2.5 across U.S. counties and commuting zones, well explained by commuting openness — so the incidence of any infrastructure or productivity change depends on the pre-existing structure of commuting linkages, not just on who is treated ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). In Berlin, the auto-removal counterfactual shows floor prices fall substantially more in remote locations with poor public transport than in central ones, so the welfare cost of losing the automobile is borne disproportionately by the least accessible residents ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

The within-city versus across-city distinction also shapes incidence. Wu and You find, in a system of cities calibrated to China, that most large cities have surpassed their locally optimal sizes even though they are too small from the national perspective; relaxing internal migration restrictions would raise migrant and aggregate welfare but lower the average welfare of natives in the most desirable cities through crowding and higher housing costs ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). This local-versus-national tension is the distributional heart of the infrastructure question, and it is why land-value capture is attractive as a financing and incidence instrument: Tsivanidis estimates that a well-targeted scheme selling development rights near stations would have increased the welfare gains of TransMilenio by around 36% while covering 19–64% of its capital costs ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

## Endogenous Infrastructure and Path Dependence

Infrastructure is not only an exogenous shock; it is also an endogenous outcome, and the corpus shows that the spatial pattern of infrastructure investment — not just its total amount — determines its growth effects.

Trew develops a model of the First Industrial Revolution in which transport infrastructure is built endogenously by infrastructure companies that lease land and collect rents from the firms that use the improved transport. The counterfactuals are striking: bringing the spatially heterogeneous infrastructure improvements forward by 50 years accelerates the industrial takeoff from 1782.7 to 1777.6, while bringing the same improvements forward uniformly — spreading each period's investment evenly across all locations — delays takeoff to 1787.3, with growth never reaching 1.5% in the simulated period. The mechanism is the tension identified above: localized infrastructure releases transport labor into manufacturing and agriculture, making firms large enough to amortize the fixed costs of innovation, while uniform improvements lower transport costs everywhere and mute the gains from releasing labor out of transport services ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]).

The path dependence is historical as well as spatial. In Trew's earlier work on the spatial takeoff, the location of the industrial revolution depends on initial manufacturing productivity and energy costs — Newcastle's cheap coal cannot overcome its distance from London and low initial productivity, so the North of England remains the cradle of industrialization across all transport-policy counterfactuals ([[trew-2014-spatial-takeoff]]). Infrastructure, once built, is persistent, and the locations it connects inherit an advantage that later policy can reinforce or fail to reverse.

*Current wiki assessment: The endogenous-infrastructure result — that localized, targeted infrastructure investment can accelerate growth while uniform investment may not — is the strongest policy message of the historical papers, and it resonates with the modern market-access evidence: what matters is not the miles built but the access to dense markets they create ([[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]).*

## Digital and Network Infrastructure as Trade-Cost Reducers

The Chinese extension treats digital and network infrastructure as the modern analogue of roads and rails: an investment that lowers the cost of transacting across regions. Liu, Zhang, and Cai build a multi-industry QSE model with input-output linkages and measure the welfare effect of China's network-infrastructure build-out. They estimate that network infrastructure caused a cumulative decline in inter-regional trade costs of about 3.19% and raised aggregate welfare by roughly 10.3% over 2012–2019 — robust to the trade elasticity, base year, and tariff assumptions, and rising to 11.09% when interregional labor mobility is allowed. The gain decomposes into a direct trade-cost channel and an input-output channel: network infrastructure both lowers the cost of moving goods, benefiting tradable industries, and strengthens input-output linkages that spill over to non-tradable industries ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]).

The mechanism is the same as for physical infrastructure — reduced trade costs raise market access — but the welfare magnitude matches or exceeds that of major institutional reforms. Tombe and Zhu estimate that reductions in China's internal trade and migration costs between 2000 and 2005 account for a large share of aggregate labor productivity growth, with internal reforms more important than external trade liberalization; moving internal trade costs to Canadian levels would raise aggregate welfare by 16.3% (real GDP up 12.5%), while lowering migration costs to U.S. mobility levels would raise welfare by 45.6% ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). Ma and Tang similarly show that inter-city migration amplifies the welfare gains from trade through endogenous firm entry, with migrant inflows raising destination-city welfare by about 4% despite congestion ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). Network investment thereby joins these institutional reforms on the same footing.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** China's network infrastructure delivers welfare gains (~10.3%) comparable to those from major institutional reforms such as hukou relaxation and road construction, and operates through the same market-access and input-output channels the transport literature identifies. Narrowing the digital divide is welfare-enhancing for efficiency as well as equity, because it is the reduction in trade costs — which requires balanced rather than lopsided network coverage — that drives the aggregate gain ([[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]; [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]).

## Open Questions

Several questions remain open across the corpus. First, measuring the full general-equilibrium welfare gains requires taking congestion, housing supply, and land-value capture seriously: Tsivanidis shows congestion slightly raises the measured welfare gain of transit, while the failure of housing supply to respond is precisely why land-value capture would have added a third to the gains ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). Second, external validity across city types is uncertain — parameters estimated in Bogotá or Berlin may not transfer to cities with different transport networks, housing markets, or income distributions ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Third, dynamic welfare remains largely unmeasured: the static models capture the equilibrium response to infrastructure but not the long-run growth effects, which can amplify or offset the static gains depending on whether density strengthens or weakens innovation ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]; [[trew-2020-endogenous-infrastructure]]). Fourth, the political economy of who pays for and captures infrastructure gains — the local-versus-national tension in Wu and You, the natives-versus-migrants incidence in Tsivanidis, the fiscal-equalization trade-offs in the Chinese applications — is the least understood dimension ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).
