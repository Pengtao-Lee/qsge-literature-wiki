# Agglomeration and Dispersion Forces

Economic activity is intensely concentrated in space — in cities, and within cities in neighborhoods such as Manhattan or the Square Mile in London — and explaining this concentration is the central organizing question of spatial economics. Agglomeration forces make density productive and pleasant: knowledge spillovers, input sharing, labor-market matching, love of variety from increasing returns, and consumption or amenity externalities all raise productivity and utility where people and firms cluster. Dispersion forces push activity apart: the fixed supply of land and housing, the cost of commuting, congestion in amenities and transport, and the idiosyncratic heterogeneity of preferences that keeps workers willing to remain in less productive places. Quantitative spatial economics parameterizes both force families, most canonically as density elasticities of productivity and amenities, and shows that their balance determines whether a spatial equilibrium is unique, whether agglomeration is self-reinforcing, and how responsive the spatial distribution is to trade, transport, and policy shocks. This page develops the taxonomy, the canonical estimates, and the formal uniqueness conditions that recur across the corpus.

## The taxonomy of forces

The microfoundations of agglomeration have been classified since Marshall as knowledge spillovers, thick labor markets, and backward-and-forward linkages; the modern survey convention, following Duranton and Puga, groups them into *sharing, matching, and learning* ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Quantitative models rarely model these mechanisms separately. Instead, the survey's menu of building blocks captures them through a small set of reduced-form devices: increasing returns and love of variety that make a location more attractive the larger its market, endogenous productivity that depends on surrounding economic activity, and endogenous amenities that respond to the density of residents ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The dispersion side of the menu is equally spare: a fixed factor such as land in utility or production is a congestion force, commuting costs disperse workplaces from residences, congestion in transportation raises travel times with use, and idiosyncratic preferences make the supply of labor to each location upward-sloping rather than perfectly elastic ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]).

A compact formalization that unifies much of the menu is the local-spillover specification of Allen and Arkolakis, in which overall productivity and amenity in a location depend on its own population density:

$$A(i) = \overline{A}(i)L(i)^{\alpha}, \qquad u(i) = \overline{u}(i)L(i)^{\beta},$$

where $\alpha$ is a productivity spillover and $\beta$ an amenity spillover, the latter often negative because it absorbs inelastic housing supply ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). For particular parameter values the framework is isomorphic to the Krugman monopolistic-competition model, the Helpman model with residential land, and Ricardian and Armington trade models with external economies; the same agglomeration-and-congestion building blocks appear in trade models and in models of a single city's internal structure ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

## Production externalities

The canonical intra-city specification makes a block's productivity an increasing, spatially decaying function of the travel-time-weighted density of workplace employment around it:

$$A_{j} = a_{j}Y_{j}^{\lambda}, \qquad Y_{j} \equiv \sum_{s=1}^{S} e^{-\delta\tau_{js}}\left(\frac{H_{Ms}}{K_{s}}\right),$$

where $H_{Ms}/K_{s}$ is workplace employment density per unit of land, $\delta$ governs spatial decay with travel time, and $\lambda$ is the elasticity of productivity with respect to the externality ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Structural GMM estimation on the Berlin Wall natural experiment yields $\lambda \approx 0.07$ with decay $\delta \approx 0.36$, so production externalities fall to near zero within roughly ten minutes of travel time — a figure toward the high end of the 3–8% range estimated across cities ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The within-city estimate holds travel times constant; a doubling of total city population typically combines densification with an expansion of built-up area that raises average travel times and attenuates the externality at rate $\delta$ ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

Evidence from other settings is broadly consistent. For Bogotá's bus rapid transit expansion, Tsivanidis estimates a productivity spillover of $\mu_{A} \approx 0.25$, larger than Berlin's but identified off a transport shock in a developing city with substantial reallocation ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). For Chinese cities, Wu and You estimate a city-size elasticity of wages and productivity of roughly 0.1 using historical city size as an instrument, embedded in a production function with skill-specific externalities of about $\varphi_{H} \approx 0.08$ for high-skill and $\varphi_{L} \approx 0.02$ for low-skill labor ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Across all of these settings, a doubling of density raises productivity and wages by on the order of 3–10 percent, with the exact number depending on spatial scale and on whether travel times or land area are held fixed.

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** Within-city density elasticities are consistent in sign and magnitude across very different settings — $\lambda \approx 0.07$ in Berlin, $\mu_{A} \approx 0.25$ in Bogotá, $\varphi \approx 0.1$ for Chinese cities — and all are highly localized, decaying to near zero within minutes of travel time ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Comparison is nonetheless hazardous: the estimates differ in whether they hold travel times fixed, in the density measure used, and in the identifying shock (severed externalities, expanded transit access, or a level regression on city size).

## Residential and amenity externalities

Amenities are themselves endogenous to density. Ahlfeldt and coauthors model residential amenities symmetrically to production: $B_{i} = b_{i}\Omega_{i}^{\eta}$ with $\Omega_{i} \equiv \sum_{r=1}^{S} e^{-\rho\tau_{ir}}(H_{Rr}/K_{r})$, estimating an elasticity of amenities with respect to residence density of $\eta \approx 0.15$ and a decay rate $\rho \approx 0.76$, again near zero within about ten minutes of travel time ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). This underpins the "consumer city" view that consumption externalities are an agglomeration force comparable to production externalities ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The same logic operates at the level of skill composition: in Bogotá, Tsivanidis finds that residential amenities depend on the college share of a neighborhood's residents, so a transit improvement that attracts high-skilled workers raises amenities and pulls in further high-skilled residents — a residential-externality channel that acts as a force for skill segregation ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). Cross-city evidence points the same way: Diamond shows that the rising geographic sorting of US college graduates into high-wage, high-rent cities was partly fueled by endogenous amenity growth within higher-skill cities, widening inequality beyond the direct effect of the wage premium ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Because amenities and productivity are correlated, the central identification problem is separating residential externalities from shared locational fundamentals — high land prices and dense residence could simply reflect an attractive natural site ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

## Dispersion forces: land, commuting, congestion, taste

Four dispersion forces recur. The first is the immobile factor: land used residentially or commercially is a congestion force because concentration bids up land prices and reduces real wages — the force behind the Helpman uniqueness condition $\sigma(1-\alpha) > 1$, where a higher land share $(1-\alpha)$ strengthens dispersion ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). The second is commuting, which disperses workplaces from residences and, by letting workers access high-productivity employment without living there, relieves the land-price congestion of central locations ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). Commuting flows follow a gravity equation with a travel-time semi-elasticity of roughly $\nu \approx 0.07$ per minute in Berlin, and a distance elasticity of about $-4.4$ versus roughly $-1.3$ for goods trade across US counties — moving people is costlier than moving goods ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The third is congestion, modeled either as increasing travel times with use or as a negative density elasticity of amenities, as in $a_{t}(r) = \bar{a}(r)\bar{L}_{t}(r)^{-\lambda}$ in the dynamic models of Desmet and coauthors ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). The fourth is taste dispersion: when workers draw idiosyncratic preferences from a Fréchet distribution, the supply of labor to each location is upward-sloping in real income, and the elasticity is governed by the dispersion parameter $\varepsilon$ (or $\Omega$ dynamically) — so greater preference heterogeneity is itself a dispersion force ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]). The degree of heterogeneity is quantitatively decisive for counterfactuals: as the Fréchet shape parameter moves from 3 to 5, the implied average treatment effects on population and land rents vary by around 50–70% ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]).

## Equilibrium uniqueness and the balance of forces

The central analytical result is that the sign of the *net* elasticity of welfare with respect to local population determines uniqueness and stability. In the Allen–Arkolakis framework the relevant object is

$$\gamma_{1} \equiv 1 - \alpha(\sigma-1) - \beta\sigma > 0,$$

together with $\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1} \in [-1,1]$ for uniqueness: $\gamma_{1}$ is (up to a constant) the partial elasticity of welfare with respect to population, so $\gamma_{1} > 0$ means a marginal migrant reduces incumbent welfare — dispersion dominates — giving local stability, while $\gamma_{1} \le 0$ means agglomeration can concentrate the economy into a "black hole" ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). In the empirically relevant range, uniqueness holds when $\alpha + \beta \le 0$; when $\alpha + \beta > 0$ the equilibrium is stable but need not be unique — the one-dimensional circle exhibits a continuum of equilibria each concentrating activity around a different point ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]). The same logic appears with different notation in every framework the survey considers: the Helpman model requires $\sigma(1-\alpha) > 1$; the internal-city model has a unique equilibrium when productivity and amenities are exogenous and potential multiplicity when agglomeration is added, handled by conditioning on observed data and selecting the counterfactual equilibrium closest to the initial one ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). In the dynamic model of Desmet, Nagy and Rossi-Hansberg the balance is struck between static agglomeration $\alpha/\theta$, returns to innovation $\gamma_{1}/\xi$, and the three dispersion forces of amenity congestion $\lambda$, land congestion $1-\mu$, and taste dispersion $\Omega$:

$$\frac{\alpha}{\theta} + \frac{\gamma_{1}}{\xi} \le \lambda + 1 - \mu + \Omega.$$

When this holds, a unique equilibrium — and with an additional term, a unique balanced-growth path with a non-degenerate spatial distribution — exists; otherwise the economy may cycle or concentrate into a measure-zero region ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** The substantive content of the uniqueness condition is identical across the Armington, Eaton–Kortum, Helpman, internal-city, and dynamic frameworks: *the spatial equilibrium is unique and stable if and only if dispersion forces — land, commuting, congestion, and taste heterogeneity — dominate agglomeration forces* ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). Only the notation changes; the economics is the same balance of centripetal and centrifugal forces.

## Identifying forces empirically

Because cross-sectional correlations between density and productivity are consistent with strong agglomeration forces *or* with shared natural advantages, the gold standard is a natural experiment that exogenously shifts the surrounding concentration of economic activity. The Berlin Wall is the canonical within-city example: its division severed all local economic interactions between East and West Berlin — in the model, prohibitive commuting costs and infinite decay of production and residential externalities across the Wall — while leaving physical fundamentals unchanged, and reunification reversed the shock ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The structural estimate exploits the model's recursive structure: given observed floor prices, employment, land area, and travel times, the equilibrium conditions can be inverted to recover unique values of the unobserved fundamentals even when the model has multiple equilibria, and the agglomeration parameters are then identified from moment conditions requiring that changes in structural residuals be uncorrelated with the distance-grid-cell variation in the shock ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

Equally important, models with exogenous productivity and amenities are quantitatively unable to account for the data: simulating division while holding productivity and amenities at their 1936 values predicts a floor-price treatment effect of about $-0.41$ log points against the actual $-0.80$ for the closest blocks to the pre-war central business district, and the reunification counterfactual predicts close to zero against the actual $+0.40$ ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Only with the estimated externalities does the model reproduce the observed reallocations. Other natural experiments converge on the same conclusion: million-dollar-plant location decisions confirm that local employment responses are larger where commuting markets are more open ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]); the Bogotá transit expansion identifies productivity and amenity spillovers from a shock that shifts the supply of labor and residents differentially across tracts ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]); and the Tennessee Valley Authority and the Richmond revitalization program provide place-based evidence of substantial, localized spillovers ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Natural experiments remain the gold standard for identifying agglomeration and dispersion forces; cross-sectional density-wage regressions deliver reduced-form coefficients that are not structurally interpretable and need not be invariant to policy. Confidence is highest for the within-city externalities identified off the Berlin Wall and the Bogotá transit shock, and the recursive-inversion argument of Ahlfeldt et al. is why identification survives the presence of multiple equilibria ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

## Dynamic agglomeration: innovation and diffusion

The static balance has a dynamic counterpart: density raises the incentive to innovate because a larger local market lets the fixed cost of innovation be shared among more consumers, and this *scale effect* turns spatial concentration into a growth engine. In the spatial development model of Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg, firms invest in innovation by bidding for land, and because future returns are capitalized into land rents, the dynamic problem collapses into a sequence of static ones — but innovation incentives are stronger where market size is larger, so density, innovation, and productivity growth are positively linked ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). This channel helps explain the postwar rise in the spatial concentration of US services and the mid-1990s take-off of service productivity growth ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The dynamic dispersion force is spatial technology diffusion: innovations diffuse to neighbors at a rate declining with distance, so no location keeps a permanent advantage. In Desmet, Nagy and Rossi-Hansberg's world-development model, local productivity evolves as the product of past innovation and a spatially diffused aggregate technology, and because dynamic agglomeration adds a third agglomeration term, the condition for a unique balanced-growth path is strictly stricter than the static uniqueness condition — without diffusion the economy would concentrate into a point in the long run ([[desmet-nagy-rossi-hansberg-2018-geography-development]]). The interaction of agglomeration, innovation, and structural transformation is developed on its own page ([[spatial-innovation-and-structural-transformation]]).

## Scale dependence and open questions

Agglomeration estimates depend on spatial scale. Within-city estimates from Berlin are somewhat higher than the across-city estimates surveyed by Rosenthal and Strange, but the two are reconciled by the rapid spatial decay of externalities and by the fact that a larger city combines higher density with a larger land area and longer average commutes ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). A structural elasticity estimated in one city need not generalize to another: the local employment elasticity is heterogeneous across locations depending on commuting openness, so a single reduced-form estimate applied out of sample can mislead ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]). Whether agglomeration estimates are structural parameters or reduced-form features of a specific equilibrium remains open, as does the frontier the survey identifies: most models concern goods while much activity is in services, most are static while the important forces are dynamic, and the geography of firm and worker networks is underexplored ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The literature is only beginning to extend these estimates to developing cities, where the parameters — as the Bogotá and Chinese evidence suggests — may differ ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).
