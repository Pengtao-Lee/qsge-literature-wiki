# Economics of the Hukou System

The hukou (household registration) system is China's institutional mechanism for controlling internal migration and determining access to local public services. Established in the 1950s, it assigns each citizen a registration status (agricultural or non-agricultural) in a specific location; changing one's hukou location or category requires government approval, which is difficult to obtain, especially in large cities. In economic terms, the hukou system functions as a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives: migrants without local hukou face higher prices for publicly-provided services such as education and healthcare, restricted access to social security, and limited employment rights. Unlike sunk moving costs, hukou costs are recurring flow costs that persist as long as the migrant lacks local hukou. In quantitative spatial models, these costs are parameterized as multiplicative disutility or fiscal wedges that reduce the effective wage migrants receive, creating a gap between the marginal product of labor and the private return to migration. The friction is not a single scalar: it is skill-differentiated (the probability of obtaining local hukou rises steeply with education), city-tier-differentiated, and dynamic, entering forward-looking migration decisions as a state variable that shapes the entire future migration stream. The system is the single most important spatial distortion in the Chinese economy and a central friction in every major QSE study of China.

## Institutional background (1950s–2020s)

The hukou system was formally instituted in 1958 to control population mobility and maintain social stability. Before the economic reforms began in 1978, individuals were prohibited from living or working outside their hukou county. This prohibition was relaxed in the 1980s, but workers without local hukou still had to apply for a temporary residence permit, a difficult and risky process. A nationwide administrative reform in 2003 greatly streamlined the process for obtaining permits, and several coastal provinces eliminated the requirement altogether [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. By 2000, the share of Chinese living outside their birth county had risen to 15%, up from only 3% in 1990; by 2010, this share had grown to 18% [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

Despite these relaxations, the system has not been abolished. Today, each Chinese city operates its own hukou policy under national guidelines, with city-specific quotas and point-based systems. The most desirable cities—Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen—maintain the most restrictive policies, while small and medium cities have largely liberalized their hukou requirements. The result is a highly differentiated system: hukou policy is not a single national barrier but a spectrum of city-specific restrictions that vary with a city's economic appeal, and within each city a two-track system that naturalizes skilled migrants far more readily than the low-skilled [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

## The hukou as a fiscal wedge

The economic essence of the hukou system is that it creates a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives. Migrants without local hukou face higher user fees for publicly-provided private goods—education, healthcare, and social benefits—while paying the same taxes as natives. This wedge enters the individual's location choice as a reduction in the effective real wage available outside one's hukou city.

Wu and You (2025) estimate this wedge using a revealed-preference approach in the spirit of Rosen (1979) and Roback (1982): individuals' location choices reveal how they trade off local hukou status against income differences. The estimated average fiscal wedge was 50.7% of destination wage income in 2000, declining to 32.5% in 2005, 15.0% in 2010, and 15.0% in 2015. These magnitudes indicate that the costs of lacking local hukou were initially very high and declined quickly but remained substantial. Chen, Shi, and Tang (2019) provide corroborating evidence from housing markets, estimating the market valuation of urban hukou in Jinan—a tier-2 city—at approximately 90,000–126,000 RMB ($13,314–$18,639, or 1.5–2.1 times GDP per capita) in 2017 [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. Because the wedge is a resource transfer between natives and migrants rather than a deadweight loss, its welfare consequence depends on who gains and who loses, which is why the native-vs-migrant split of welfare effects is central to evaluating the system [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

## Modeling hukou in QSE frameworks

The hukou system enters QSE models through four distinct approaches, each capturing different features of the friction.

**Tombe-Zhu approach.** Tombe and Zhu (2019) model migration costs as recurring flow costs (rather than sunk costs), motivated by the hukou system's ongoing nature: a migrant without local hukou faces higher costs every period. In their Eaton-Kortum framework with 30 provinces and two sectors (agriculture and non-agriculture), workers choose locations based on real wages, migration costs, land-ownership rebates, and idiosyncratic Fréchet-distributed preferences. Migration costs include both symmetric geographic costs (distance) and an additional loss of land income when leaving one's home region—a feature motivated by China's collective land ownership, where migrants forfeit access to collectively-owned land [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]].

**Wu-You approach.** Wu and You (2025) develop a richer dynamic framework with 340 prefectures, two skill groups, and forward-looking migration decisions. The hukou system enters through two channels: (1) skill-specific hukou lottery probabilities (θ), capturing the chance that a migrant obtains local hukou, and (2) a fiscal wedge (λ), capturing the price discount that natives enjoy for publicly-provided private goods. The hukou-granting probability is estimated directly from census microdata: in tier-1 cities in 2010, only 3.3% of migrants with primary schooling received a local hukou, compared to 37.3% of college graduates, and the probability is monotonically increasing in educational attainment across all city tiers [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. The fiscal wedge is then estimated from a structural revealed-preference equation. This approach captures the fact that hukou status is a state variable in workers' dynamic optimization: obtaining local hukou changes a worker's entire future stream of migration options.

**Ma-Tang approach.** Ma and Tang (2020) model migration barriers as origin-destination-specific disutility parameters (δ) in a Melitz-style framework with endogenous firm entry. They estimate these parameters using Simulated Method of Moments (SMM) on bilateral migration flow moments from 279 prefectures. The destination-specific barriers are identified from the difference between predicted and observed migration flows into each city, controlling for geographic costs. Their estimates reveal that Shanghai's barriers are 24% above the national average, followed by Guangzhou (13%) and Beijing (8%) [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]].

**Liu-Wang-Chen approach.** Liu, Wang, and Chen (2024) treat the hukou as a skill-differentiated iceberg cost on labor mobility, interpreted through the labor-compensation channel: migrating workers forgo a fraction of effective labor income. This treatment is embedded in a heterogeneous-aggregation framework with cross-skill productivity and public-service spillovers, where the friction's removal interacts with skill composition and with property-tax design [[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]].

> ***Cross-pattern convergence**: Despite using different modeling frameworks—Eaton-Kortum (Tombe-Zhu), dynamic discrete choice with fiscal wedges (Wu-You), Melitz with SMM (Ma-Tang), and heterogeneous iceberg costs (Liu-Wang-Chen)—all four approaches converge on the finding that hukou costs are large and economically significant, though their mapping to specific parameter values differs across methods.*

## Skill heterogeneity in the hukou friction

The hukou friction is not a uniform tax on mobility; it is a skill-selective device. The hukou-granting probabilities estimated by Wu and You (2025) rise steeply with education in every city tier: in tier-1 cities in 2010 the share of migrants receiving local hukou was 3.3% for primary school or less, 3.9% for junior high, 11.6% for senior high, and 37.3% for college graduates, and the same monotone pattern holds in tier-2 and tier-3 cities with higher overall probabilities. This skill selectivity is a deliberate design of the two-track system: cities grant hukou to high-human-capital migrants while keeping the low-skilled on temporary status, so the distributional bite of the friction falls hardest on the low-skilled in the largest cities [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

The skill dimension also changes the welfare economics of reform. Liu, Wang, and Chen (2024) show that, once cross-skill productivity and public-service spillovers are present, optimizing the spatial allocation of human capital raises social welfare, and the Pareto-efficient frontier is reached by redistributing from high-skill workers in high-wage cities toward low-skill workers in low-wage cities; a property tax financing local public services can implement much of this redistribution and partially substitute for the hukou's allocative role, reducing the required transfers from high-wage to low-wage cities ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]). Because hukou liberalization would disproportionately raise the low-skilled population of the largest cities (116% versus 50% for the high-skilled in Wu–You's counterfactual), the skill composition of the marginal migrant is itself a welfare-relevant outcome that skill-selective reform policies directly manipulate ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *Across the corpus the hukou friction is not a single scalar — it is a skill-differentiated, city-tier-differentiated wedge whose distributional bite is largest for the low-skilled in the largest cities. Every major quantitative treatment reflects this, whether through skill-specific hukou-granting probabilities ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]), skill-differentiated iceberg costs ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]), or city-specific origin-destination barriers ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]).*

## Estimated magnitudes of hukou costs

The estimated magnitude of hukou costs varies across frameworks but consistently points to very large barriers. Tombe and Zhu (2019) find that in 2000, the average cost of rural-to-urban migration within a province was equivalent to shrinking real income by a factor of nearly 3, while between-province moves were an order of magnitude more costly (a factor of ~25). By 2005, migration costs had declined to 82% of their 2000 level, with between-province costs falling to 61% of their initial level—a drop driven by the 2003 administrative reforms and province-specific hukou liberalizations.

Wu and You (2025) translate the aggregate consequences into macro-level losses: the hukou system as a whole created a GDP loss of 13.6% and a welfare loss of 4.7% averaged over 2000–2020. The aggregate welfare loss is smaller than the GDP loss because rising housing prices in destination cities partially offset wage gains, and because the wedge is modeled as a resource transfer rather than an iceberg cost. In a robustness check varying key parameters, the 20-year-average GDP effect ranges from 8.6% to 19.1% and the welfare effect from 2.1% to 7.9%. Ma and Tang (2020) find that the additional barriers in the four largest cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen) result in a 0.7% to 3.9% loss in national welfare, and Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) embed migration frictions of the hukou type alongside firm-level wedges, where the interaction between the two determines both aggregate welfare and spatial inequality ([[li-ma-tang-2024-migration-misallocation]]).

## Hukou reform and its effects

The hukou system has undergone significant but incomplete reform. The 2003 administrative reform that streamlined temporary residence permits reduced between-province migration costs by nearly 40% and was the single largest policy-driven reduction in measured migration costs [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. Between 2000 and 2005, within-province rural-to-urban migrants increased by roughly 15% and between-province migrants by 82%, largely due to these cost reductions.

The post-2014 period saw a new wave of reform: the central government pushed for hukou liberalization in small and medium cities (urban population under 3 million) while maintaining or tightening restrictions in the largest cities. Tier-1 cities introduced point-based systems that favor college graduates, skilled workers, and investors. The result is a "two-track" system: hukou has become nearly irrelevant for moving to small cities but remains a binding constraint for moving to Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. The Wu-You estimates of the fiscal wedge confirm this: the wedge declined from 50.7% to 15.0% of destination wage between 2000 and 2015, reflecting substantial liberalization, but the remaining wedge is concentrated in the largest cities and is increasingly skill-selective ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

## Hukou vs. other migration barriers

The hukou system differs from standard migration barriers in several economically important ways. First, hukou costs are policy-determined rather than geography-determined: while geographic distance and cultural differences affect migration everywhere, hukou costs are created and enforced by government regulation. Second, hukou costs are recurring flow costs, not one-time moving expenses: a migrant must pay the hukou premium every period, whereas typical migration barriers (information frictions, moving expenses, cultural adjustment) are largely sunk. Third, hukou interacts with land institutions: because rural land is collectively owned and lacks a rental market, migrants who leave their hukou location lose access to land-based income, which Tombe and Zhu (2019) estimate adds a factor of ~1.9 to the overall migration cost, and whose elimination through private land ownership and functioning land rental markets would raise welfare by nearly 12 percent. This institutional interaction means that hukou reform alone may be insufficient—land reform that severs the link between registration and land rights is a natural complement ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]).

## The hukou–land nexus

The hukou system is inseparable from China's land-quota system: both are instruments by which the central state rations the spatial allocation of factors. Zhao and Chen (2021) quantify the interaction in a quantitative spatial model of interregional land allocation. Since 2003, urban construction-land supply has been tilted toward central and western provinces—the eastern share fell from 65% in 2003 to about one-third by 2017—which narrowed regional gaps but raised eastern housing prices and distorted factor allocation. Their counterfactuals show that maximizing GDP requires allocating more land to the eastern provinces (raising GDP 5.3% and total welfare 4.6% but widening the regional per-capita income gap by 8.2%), while minimizing the income gap achieves the opposite at little GDP cost; the actual land allocation balances the two, raising GDP 2.3% and welfare 2.6% while cutting the gap by 0.7% ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]).

The key policy result is the complementarity of the two systems. Lowering migration costs into the eastern and southern coastal provinces raises welfare 35.5% and cuts the regional income gap 20.5% but widens house-price divergence 26.2% as migrants crowd into the developed coast; combining this with east-leaning land allocation yields welfare gains of 47.1%, a gap reduction of 13.5%, and a much smaller house-price divergence of 16.4% — the joint "land + hukou reform" guides both population and land toward the east and achieves efficiency, equity, and housing-price objectives simultaneously, whereas either reform alone founders on one of the objectives ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]). This is why the policy literature treats the hukou and land-quota systems as a single reform package rather than separable distortions, and why hukou reform alone is judged insufficient for regional equalization ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]).

## Hukou in dynamic QSE

The hukou wedge is not a static parameter; in forward-looking models it is a state variable whose persistence shapes the entire transition path. Wu and You (2025) model hukou as a Markov process: each period a migrant draws a lottery for the destination hukou, and winning changes the value of every future migration option, so the wedge's removal has dynamic as well as static effects ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). The dynamic QSE survey of Zhao, Chen, and Fu (2022) frames migration frictions as a dynamic choice problem in which workers form expectations over future utility; their Chinese application shows that introducing population migration dramatically slows the economy's convergence — real GDP and capital half-lives lengthen far beyond the classic neoclassical benchmark — and that the wedge's persistence, by slowing the reallocation of population toward high-productivity locations, delays regional convergence and the "new demographic dividend" from improved labor allocation ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

The persistence of the hukou wedge also feeds into the dynamic interaction between migration and capital accumulation. Kleinman, Liu, and Redding (2023) show that forward-looking capital and migration dynamics interact systematically: convergence is slow when the gaps of capital and labor from steady state are positively correlated, and the average half-life of income convergence across US states is around 20 years. In the Chinese context, the recurring-flow nature of the hukou cost means the wedge operates period after period, so its removal accelerates not only the migration margin but also the capital-accumulation margin that responds to the anticipated population distribution ([[kleinman-liu-redding-2023-dynamic-spatial]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The implication for reform sequencing is that gradual, anticipated hukou liberalization has different welfare and convergence consequences than a one-off removal, because forward-looking workers and firms re-optimize along the transition path ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).

## Frontier: QSE policy translation for China

The evolution of hukou policy in the 2020s raises new questions for QSE modeling. Most Chinese cities have now relaxed hukou restrictions entirely, leaving only the very largest cities—Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and a few others—with binding constraints. The key policy question is therefore not whether to abolish the system but how to manage the transition in the remaining restricted cities.

Partial reform creates complex dynamics that QSE models are only beginning to capture. For example, liberalizing hukou for college graduates only (as Beijing and Shanghai currently do) changes the skill composition of migration, with implications for local public finances, housing markets, and aggregate productivity. The hukou–land evidence of Zhao and Chen (2021) implies that residual barriers should be relaxed in tandem with land allocation and fiscal transfers toward the receiving regions, so that the distributional losses to incumbent natives are compensated by complementary supply-side policies rather than by continued restriction. The digitalization of public services—e-health consultations, online education platforms, and digital social security portability—may reduce the hukou wedge by enabling migrants to access services remotely without changing their registration status ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]; [[liu-zhang-cai-2026-network-infrastructure]]). To the extent that these digital substitutes lower the fiscal wedge, they reduce both the distortionary effect of the hukou system and the welfare gain from fully abolishing it. Dynamic treatments further suggest that the timing of reform matters: because the wedge is a persistent state variable, announcing and sequencing liberalization affects the transition path, and skill-differentiated reform (property tax, transfers, settlement-point reform) can redistribute toward the low-skilled who bear the largest residual burden ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Post-2020 reforms have substantially reduced measured hukou costs, but the remaining barriers in the largest cities continue to generate economically significant distortions. The QSE literature provides robust evidence that eliminating these residual barriers would raise aggregate welfare substantially, though the distributional consequences require complementary policies — most importantly joint land-allocation reform and fiscal transfers ([[zhao-chen-2021-interregional-land-allocation]]), skill-aware redistribution ([[liu-wang-chen-2024-human-capital-spatial-allocation]]), and dynamic sequencing of reform ([[zhao-chen-fu-2022-dynamic-qse-china]]).
