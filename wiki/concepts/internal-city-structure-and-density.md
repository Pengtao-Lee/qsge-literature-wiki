# Internal City Structure and Density

The internal structure of a city — where people live, where they work, how much floor space costs, and how land is divided between commercial and residential use across a city's many heterogeneous blocks — is itself an object of spatial equilibrium. Quantitative urban models treat a city not as a point but as a collection of discrete locations connected by a commuting network, in which workers choose residence-workplace pairs under idiosyncratic taste shocks, firms hire labor supplied through that network, and a land market allocates floor space between competing uses. The canonical model, operationalized on Berlin by Ahlfeldt, Redding, Sturm and Wolf and extended to a developing city by Tsivanidis, delivers a gravity equation for commuting flows, measures of commuter market access, and endogenous productivity and amenity externalities from surrounding density, balanced against commuting costs and inelastic land supply to shape the city's employment and residence gradients. Density — the concentration of employment and residents per unit of built land — is the central endogenous variable, and its equilibrium pattern, together with the dispersion of land prices, is the model's empirical signature. This page develops the architecture, the key parameters, and the natural experiments that identify them.

## From monocentric to quantitative city models

The classical theory of urban structure is the monocentric city of Alonso, Muth and Mills, in which all production occurs at a central business district and workers trade off commuting costs against cheaper land, generating a land-price gradient that declines with distance from the center ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The monocentric benchmark imposes symmetry and a single exogenous center, abstracting from the locational fundamentals and polycentric patterns of real cities. Fujita and Ogawa and Lucas and Rossi-Hansberg generalized it to non-monocentric equilibrium in continuous, symmetric space; but symmetry still made distance from the center the summary statistic, and most data are reported for discrete units such as blocks or census tracts ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

The quantitative urban model completes the break. It keeps the agglomeration forces of the non-monocentric tradition — production externalities from surrounding workplace density and residential externalities from surrounding residence density — but allows an arbitrary number of heterogeneous blocks differing in productivity, amenities, density of development, and access to the transport network, and makes the model tractable by adding idiosyncratic commuting shocks that generate a gravity equation for commuting flows ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The toolkit is portable to continuous space: Allen and Arkolakis model the spatial economy on any surface with arbitrary geography, show the price index is a sufficient statistic for geographic location, and estimate that removing the Interstate Highway System would reduce US welfare by 1.1–1.4 percent ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]).

## The quantitative urban model architecture

The model's building blocks are the residence-workplace choice, production with commuting-linked labor supply, and a land market. Workers have preferences over a final good, residential floor space, residential amenities, the disutility of commuting, and an idiosyncratic taste shock for each residence-workplace pair. The shock is drawn from a Fréchet distribution, so the probability that a worker resides in block $i$ and works in block $j$ takes the gravity form

$$\pi_{ij} = \frac{T_{i}E_{j}\left(d_{ij}Q_{i}^{1-\beta}\right)^{-\varepsilon}(B_{i}w_{j})^{\varepsilon}}{\sum_{r=1}^{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}T_{r}E_{s}\left(d_{rs}Q_{r}^{1-\beta}\right)^{-\varepsilon}(B_{r}w_{s})^{\varepsilon}},$$

where the iceberg commuting cost $d_{ij} = e^{\kappa\tau_{ij}}$ rises with travel time, $\varepsilon$ controls the dispersion of idiosyncratic utility, and the scale parameters $T_{i}$ and $E_{j}$ capture average utility from residing in $i$ and working in $j$ ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Firms produce a single final good under constant returns, taking the supply of workers who commute to each block as given, and the land market allocates each block's floor space between commercial and residential use through a no-arbitrage condition, the observed floor price being the maximum of the commercial and residential prices ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

A decisive property of this architecture is its recursive invertibility. Given the parameters and observed data on floor prices, workplace and residence employment, land area, and travel times, the equilibrium conditions can be solved for unique values of the unobserved location fundamentals — productivity, amenities, and density of development — even when the model has multiple equilibria, because the observed data are held fixed as an equilibrium and the fundamentals are solved as structural residuals ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Tsivanidis extends the architecture to a developing city with multiple worker groups, transit modes, car ownership, and an endogenous supply of migrants ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

## Commuting gravity and commuter market access

The model's empirical workhorse is its gravity prediction for commuting flows: a semi-log relationship between log commuting probability and travel time, with residence and workplace fixed effects absorbing all other origin and destination characteristics. Estimating this on micro data for Berlin's 12 districts in 2008 yields a semi-elasticity of commuting flows with respect to travel time of $\nu = \varepsilon\kappa \approx 0.07$ per minute — each additional minute reduces commuter flows by about 7 percent — and the specification explains 83–91 percent of the variation in bilateral commuting, robust across OLS, Poisson, and Gamma estimators ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The same gravity structure characterizes commuting across US counties, where the distance elasticity of about $-4.4$ is more than three times the goods-trade elasticity of about $-1.3$ ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]).

Commuting gravity is the foundation of *commuter market access* — the two directional measures through which a transport shock propagates. A resident's access to jobs (residence commuter market access, RCMA) is the travel-time-discounted sum of workplace opportunities reachable from a residence; a firm's access to workers (workplace commuter market access, FCMA) is the analogous sum of residents reachable from a workplace, both constructed in the Berlin model from the transformed wages that solve commuting market clearing ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). In Bogotá, Tsivanidis shows that the value of TransMilenio is precisely that it raises RCMA for some neighborhoods and FCMA for others, and identifies productivity and amenity spillovers by regressing model-inferred outcomes on these plausibly exogenous changes in market access ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). The log-linear relationship is validated empirically: adjusted productivity and amenities recovered from the gravity model correlate with observable block characteristics such as green areas, water, and war-time destruction ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern (2026-07):** Commuting flows are highly distance-sensitive and follow gravity across very different settings — Berlin districts (semi-elasticity $\nu \approx 0.07$ per minute), US counties (distance elasticity $-4.4$, far above the $-1.3$ for goods trade), and Bogotá tracts (commute elasticities $\theta_{H}=2.4$, $\theta_{L}=4$, differing by skill) ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). The consistent finding is that moving people is substantially costlier than moving goods, and that more educated workers' commute choices are less sensitive to travel time.

## Density, externalities, and the internal spatial equilibrium

The internal spatial equilibrium balances agglomeration against dispersion. Agglomeration operates through density externalities with exponential spatial decay: productivity in block $j$ depends on the travel-time-weighted sum of workplace employment density around it, $A_{j} = a_{j}Y_{j}^{\lambda}$ with $Y_{j} = \sum_{s} e^{-\delta\tau_{js}}(H_{Ms}/K_{s})$, and amenities in block $i$ depend symmetrically on residence density, $B_{i} = b_{i}\Omega_{i}^{\eta}$ with $\Omega_{i} = \sum_{r} e^{-\rho\tau_{ir}}(H_{Rr}/K_{r})$ ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Structural estimates from Berlin give a productivity elasticity of about $\lambda \approx 0.07$ and an amenity elasticity of about $\eta \approx 0.15$, both decaying to near zero within roughly ten minutes of travel time; Tsivanidis' estimates for Bogotá are larger on the production side ($\mu_{A} \approx 0.25$) and add an amenity externality that depends on the college share of residents ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). Against these stand commuting costs — which, because workers self-select into bilateral commutes, equalize average utility conditional on a commute across all pairs even when some commutes are very long — and an inelastic supply of land, which converts density into higher floor prices ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

The interaction of these forces generates the city's gradients. Production externalities concentrate employment where productivity is high; commuting costs make residents trade off cheaper land against access to employment; and localized residential externalities make residence density itself an amenity. The equilibrium is reflected in the dispersion of land prices across blocks, which the model reproduces and which serves as an over-identification check: the implied ratio of floor space to land area is strongly and approximately log-linearly related to separate data on the density of development, with a coefficient close to one ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Land-price dispersion also connects internal structure to the system of cities, whose size distribution and commuting-technology efficiency jointly shape the spatial allocation of population ([[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

## The Berlin Wall natural experiment

The quantitative urban model was built to be identified off one of the cleanest natural experiments in urban economics: the division of Berlin after the Second World War and its reunification after the fall of the Iron Curtain. Division severed all local economic interactions between East and West Berlin — in the model, infinite commuting costs and infinite rates of decay of production and residential externalities across the Wall — while leaving the physical characteristics of West Berlin blocks unchanged, and reunification reversed the severing ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The reduced-form evidence is striking. In difference-in-difference specifications with 500-meter distance-grid cells from the pre-war central business district, West Berlin blocks closest to the pre-war CBD lost roughly 55 percent of their floor-space value between 1936 and 1986 (coefficient $-0.800$ on the first grid cell), then gained roughly 49 percent between 1986 and 2006 (coefficient $+0.398$), robust to controls for transport access, parks, water, war destruction, and land use, and with no evidence of pre-trends ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). The treatment effect is driven by proximity to the pre-war concentration of economic activity in East Berlin, not by proximity to the Wall itself: there is little effect along more remote sections of the Wall ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

The experiment separates externalities from fundamentals. A model with exogenous productivity and amenities, holding fundamentals fixed, predicts floor-price treatment effects far smaller than those observed — about $-0.41$ against the actual $-0.80$ for division, and close to zero against the actual $+0.40$ for reunification — whereas the model with the structurally estimated externalities reproduces the observed effects almost exactly ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The estimated model also answers counterfactuals no experiment provides: removing the automobile from Berlin's 2006 transport network — reverting to travel times based solely on public transport — would reduce city employment by about 14 percent and output by about 12 percent, cut average floor prices by about 20 percent, and make locations less specialized in workplace versus residence activity ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The Berlin Wall design is the gold standard for separating within-city externalities from locational fundamentals, because division exogenously severed density linkages while holding physical geography fixed, and because the recursive inversion of the model identifies fundamentals without taking a stand on the strength of externalities ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Cross-sectional regressions of outcomes on density cannot achieve this separation; they remain vulnerable to the fundamental-confound the natural experiment eliminates.

## Transit, density, and urban reallocation in a developing city

Tsivanidis carries the internal-structure toolkit to a developing city, building a quantitative urban model of Bogotá with multiple worker groups, car ownership, and transit modes to evaluate the city's bus rapid transit system, TransMilenio. The model is identified off the transit expansion, which shifts commuting costs and therefore commuter market access differentially across tracts. The first-order welfare gain from travel-time savings — the value-of-travel-time-savings (VTTS) measure standard in transit appraisal — is 1.25 percent, but the full general-equilibrium gain is 2.39 percent, so the VTTS captures only about 52 percent of the total; Proposition 2 shows via the envelope theorem that the VTTS is exactly the first-order welfare impact only when the equilibrium is efficient, with the gap due roughly three-quarters to amenity and productivity externalities and one-quarter to approximation error ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). Equilibrium reallocation effects, in short, matter for valuing urban infrastructure, not just time savings.

The distributional results are sobering. Because the amenity externality runs through the college share of residents, the reallocation raises welfare inequality between high- and low-skilled workers by 0.59 percent, though the test of whether high-skilled gain more than low-skilled has a p-value of only 0.14 ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). With an upward-sloping supply of migrants into the city, the aggregate welfare gain falls to 0.69 percent as population rises 11 percent and bids up floor-space values, while GDP rises 17.5 percent (6.5 percent per capita); TransMilenio accounts for between 3 and 17 percent of Bogotá's GDP growth over 2000–2016 and up to 40 percent of its population growth ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). Land-value capture can finance a substantial share of the system's costs, and allowing for congestion raises the estimated welfare gain modestly as commuters substitute from cars to the dedicated lanes ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The Bogotá distributional result — welfare inequality rising by 0.59% — should be treated with caution: the p-value of 0.14 on the high-versus-low-skilled difference falls well short of conventional significance, and the estimates are specific to one city, one transit system, and one model structure ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). The robust and portable finding is the VTTS accounting: first-order time-savings measures capture roughly half of general-equilibrium welfare gains, so cost-benefit analysis of urban transit that ignores reallocation and externalities is materially incomplete.

## Density, innovation, and city size

Internal structure is not only static allocation; density also shapes growth and city size. The spatial development model of Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg shows that a scale effect in innovation links density to growth: firms in denser locations face larger markets, innovate more, and push up local productivity, and because future returns to innovation are capitalized in land rents, the postwar concentration of US services helped trigger the mid-1990s take-off of service productivity growth and the associated rise in the level and dispersion of land rents ([[desmet-rossi-hansberg-2014-spatial-development]]). The dispersion of land prices is thus simultaneously an outcome of the internal spatial equilibrium and a marker of the dynamic concentration of innovative activity.

City-size optimality bridges internal structure to the system of cities. Wu and You calibrate a system-of-cities model with urban agglomeration externalities and land-based congestion to Chinese data and find that most large Chinese cities have surpassed their locally optimal sizes while remaining smaller than their nationally optimal sizes, because the most productive sites should receive more people even at the cost of exceeding their local optima — a local-versus-national tension: relaxing migration restrictions raises aggregate welfare but lowers the welfare of incumbents in the most desirable cities ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). The welfare effects of density at the city level also depend on the extensive margin of firm entry: Ma and Tang show that inflows of migrants raise welfare in Chinese destination cities — real income up 4.7 percent and welfare up 4.0 percent over 2000–2005 — because more residents support more firms and varieties, and that this result reverses if firm entry is shut down, in which case migration depresses wages and raises congestion disutility ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]).

## Open questions

The frontier of the internal-structure literature is defined as much by what the models leave out as by what they capture. Housing supply is typically treated as an exogenous density of development rather than a fully endogenous response of construction to prices, and the models abstract from congestion in transportation — travel times are fixed inputs rather than outcomes of commuting volumes — although the Bogotá extension begins to close this gap ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]). The potential for multiple equilibria is handled by an equilibrium-selection rule rather than resolved, which matters for the external validity of counterfactuals ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]). Finally, the parameters — commuting semi-elasticities, externality elasticities, and their decay rates — are estimated city by city, and how far they generalize across income levels, transport technologies, and land-use regulations remains open, with Berlin and Bogotá already differing by a factor of three on the production externality ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).
