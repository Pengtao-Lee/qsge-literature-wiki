The local employment elasticity measures the percentage change in local employment by workplace in response to a one-percent change in local labor demand, typically proxied by a productivity shock. This parameter is central to evaluating the employment effects of local tax incentives, infrastructure investments, and place-based policies. In a closed-economy framework without spatial linkages, this elasticity is a single constant determined by local factor supply conditions. However, once commuting and migration linkages between locations are recognized, the local employment elasticity becomes an endogenous variable that varies systematically across locations depending on their degree of commuting openness — the extent to which residents can work in nearby employment centers without relocating. The key mechanism is that commuting allows employment and residence to decouple: a location receiving many inward commuters can expand employment substantially without a commensurate increase in residents, and therefore without experiencing the full force of local land-price congestion. This openness logic conditions local employment responses not only across US counties but also within a single city, across skill groups, and — with migration access substituting for commuting — across Chinese cities, where it is channeled through the hukou and land-supply systems.

## The Conventional Approach: Single-Elasticity Local Labor Markets

A large empirical literature has estimated the effects of local labor demand shocks using variation from sectoral composition (Bartik shocks), productivity, international trade, natural resource abundance, and business cycle fluctuations ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The standard approach defines local labor markets as commuting zones (CZs) — spatial aggregates designed to minimize commuting flows across boundaries — and treats these zones as independent observations in reduced-form regressions, typically assuming the local employment elasticity is a single parameter that applies across treated locations, with little attention to linkages between spatial units in goods and factor markets ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]).

A central limitation is that no choice of boundaries for local labor markets can completely eliminate commuting spillovers. For a typical US county in 2006–2010, around 27 percent of residents work outside the county and 20 percent of workers live outside it; at the ninety-fifth percentile, these figures rise to 59 and 43 percent ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). Even at the CZ level, commuting beyond boundaries remains substantial — the median CZ has around 7 percent of residents working outside, rising above 20 percent at the ninety-fifth percentile. The share of residents working locally also fell from a median of 91 percent in 1960 to 69 percent in 2000, reflecting transport expansion and the growing spatial separation of workplace and residence ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). Counties therefore differ substantially in how shocks spill across boundaries, and a single-elasticity approach risks substantial bias.

## A Quantitative Spatial Framework with Commuting and Migration

Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) develop a quantitative general equilibrium model with spatial linkages in both goods markets (trade) and factor markets (commuting and migration), building on the broader class of quantitative spatial models surveyed in Redding and Rossi-Hansberg (2017). An arbitrary number of locations differ in productivity, amenities, and geographical relationship to one another. Workers have Cobb-Douglas preferences over final goods consumption and residential land, and draw idiosyncratic amenity shocks from a Fréchet distribution for each residence-workplace pair, which makes them heterogeneous in where they live and work when faced with the same prices and wages, giving rise to upward-sloping supply functions for residents and workers at each location.

Commuting costs enter utility as an iceberg disutility parameter $\kappa_{ni} \in [1, \infty)$, while goods trade is subject to iceberg trade costs $d_{ni}$. The resulting probability that a worker resides in location $n$ and works in location $i$ follows a gravity equation. A key expression is the conditional commuting probability — the probability that a worker commutes to workplace $i$ given residence $n$:

$$
\lambda_{ni|n}^{R} = \frac{B_{ni} (w_i / \kappa_{ni})^{\epsilon}}{\sum_{s \in N} B_{ns} (w_s / \kappa_{ns})^{\epsilon}},
$$

which implies a commuting gravity equation with an elasticity of commuting flows with respect to commuting costs of $-\epsilon$. The estimated commuting distance elasticity ($-4.43$) is substantially larger than the trade distance elasticity ($-1.29$), consistent with the intuition that moving people is considerably more costly than moving goods across geographic space ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The Fréchet shape parameter $\epsilon$, estimated at 3.30, determines the dispersion of idiosyncratic amenities and therefore the elasticity of labor supply to each workplace.

The model can be inverted to recover unique values of the unobserved location fundamentals — productivity and amenities — that exactly rationalize the observed data on wages, employment, commuting flows, and land area as an equilibrium of the model ([[allen-arkolakis-2014-trade-topography]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). This invertibility, which holds when dispersion forces dominate agglomeration forces, allows counterfactual analysis of changes in commuting costs, trade costs, or local productivity without observing all structural fundamentals. Population reallocation through migration and commuting is the labour-mobility mechanism that equalises expected utility across locations and turns access to high-wage locations into a welfare-relevant object ([[redding-2016-goods-trade-factor-mobility-welfare]]).

## Heterogeneous Local Employment Elasticities

Using the quantified model, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) compute 3,111 counterfactual exercises in which each US county receives a five percent productivity shock holding all else constant. The general equilibrium elasticities of local employment range from approximately 0.5 to 2.5 across counties, with a mean of around 1.52, while the elasticity of residents (employment by residence) ranges only from about 0.2 to 1.2. Since employment and residents can differ only through commuting, this contrast itself indicates that commuting links are the primary source of the heterogeneity.

*Across the full distribution of US counties, the local employment elasticity ranges from roughly 0.5 to 2.5, and this substantial heterogeneity is robust to the introduction of variable land supply elasticities, spatially correlated shocks reproducing the industrial composition of the US economy, and aggregation to commuting zones rather than counties ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]).*

The key determinant of this heterogeneity is the openness of the local labor market to commuting, measured by the residence own commuting share $\lambda_{ii|i}^{R}$ — the fraction of residents who work in the same county where they live. This single variable alone explains 89 percent of the cross-county variation in local employment elasticities, far exceeding the explanatory power of standard empirical controls such as county employment, wages, and land area, which together achieve an $R^2$ of only about 0.5 ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). Counties with more open commuting markets (lower own commuting shares) exhibit higher employment elasticities because they can draw additional workers from surrounding areas without requiring large increases in the local resident population and therefore without bidding up land prices as severely.

The heterogeneity is not an artifact of the specific model structure: heterogeneous local employment elasticities are a generic prediction of the entire class of models consistent with a gravity equation for commuting flows, and the finding is robust to alternative measures of commuting openness — the workplace own commuting share, their average, and their minimum.

## Within-City Evidence: Berlin and Bogotá

The openness-to-commuting logic is not confined to counties. Ahlfeldt, Redding, Sturm, and Wolf (2015) show the same mechanism operating inside a single city using the Berlin Wall as a natural experiment. In their quantitative model of internal city structure, workers draw idiosyncratic utility shocks for each residence–workplace pair from a Fréchet distribution, yielding a gravity equation for commuting flows with a semi-elasticity of about 0.07 per minute of travel time. Berlin's division corresponds to prohibitive commuting costs across the East–West boundary, severing access to the pre-war central business district in East Berlin; reunification removes them. Both reduced-form and structural results show that West Berlin blocks closest to the pre-war CBD lost the most in floor prices, workplace employment, and residence employment after division and recovered the most after reunification — driven by the change in commuting access, not proximity to the Wall itself. Structurally, the production-externality elasticity (productivity with respect to workplace-employment density) is approximately 0.07, and both production and residential externalities decay essentially to zero within about ten minutes of travel time ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]).

Tsivanidis (2026) pushes the same logic to the neighbourhood scale and across skill groups in Bogotá. Estimating the commuting elasticity separately by skill using the variation in travel times induced by TransMilenio, he finds that high-skilled workers have a lower commuting elasticity than low-skilled workers ($\theta_H = 2.44$ versus $\theta_L = 4$), so high-skilled commutes are less sensitive to travel time and hence more costly to sustain — a given reduction in commute costs is therefore more valuable to them. The aggregate sufficient-statistic approach that uses a single elasticity $\theta = 3.401$ delivers an average welfare gain of about 2.4 percent, but it cannot reveal who wins and loses: with skill-differentiated commuting elasticities and imperfect substitutability between skill groups, welfare inequality rises by 0.59 percent, the high-skilled gaining more because they are concentrated in the city's north and connected to high-skill-intensive industries in the centre ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).

> *The openness-to-commuting logic that drives heterogeneity across US counties also operates within a single city and across skill groups — the same mechanism at different spatial scales, from metropolitan areas to city blocks ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]).*

## Quasi-Experimental Evidence from Million Dollar Plants

To provide independent empirical evidence for the role of commuting, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) exploit quasi-experimental variation from competitions for large manufacturing plants (Million Dollar Plants, or MDPs), following the design of Greenstone, Hornbeck, and Moretti (2010). Firms publicly report the winning county and one or two runner-up counties that survived a lengthy selection process but narrowly lost; the identifying assumption is that runner-up counties provide a valid counterfactual for the winner, conditional on observables.

An event-study analysis of 82 MDP announcements from 1972 to 2003 shows no statistically significant differences in employment between winner and runner-up counties in the ten years before the announcement, then a gradual rise to approximately 4 percent higher employment in winner counties by ten years after ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). This timing supports the identifying assumption and is consistent with direct plant operations plus indirect multiplier effects on suppliers and ancillary services.

The central prediction — that employment responses should be larger in more open commuting markets — is tested by interacting the treatment effect with the residence own commuting share. The estimated interaction coefficient is negative and statistically significant: a county completely closed to commuting ($\lambda_{ii|i}^{R} = 1$) experiences virtually no employment increase from the MDP, while a fully open county would see an increase of approximately 25 percent ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The standardized interaction coefficient is close in absolute magnitude to the standardized main effect, underscoring the economic relevance of commuting openness, and the results are robust across alternative commuting measures, industry-year fixed effects, and census-region-year or state-year fixed effects. An event-study specification for the interaction confirms the absence of pre-trends.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The MDP runner-up design provides some of the cleanest quasi-experimental evidence for commuting-driven heterogeneity, as comparing winners and runners-up within the same plant competition holds constant both the scale and timing of the labor demand shock. The MDP sample is, however, limited to 82 cases, and the external validity of these large-plant shocks for smaller or more diffuse labor demand shifts remains open.

## Housing Supply and Commuting as Alternative Adjustment Channels

An important implication of the framework is that commuting and housing supply elasticity provide alternative channels through which locations can accommodate increases in labor demand. When the model is extended to incorporate the Saiz (2010) estimates of developed land supply elasticities — which range from 0.76 to 3.09 across US metropolitan areas — the heterogeneity in local employment elasticities remains largely unchanged; the variable land supply elasticity primarily affects the resident elasticity, shifting its distribution rightward and increasing its dispersion, while the employment elasticity distribution is affected much less ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The pattern is intuitive: commuting lets workers access high-productivity employment locations with inelastic housing supplies without residing there and paying high land prices. Consequently, improvements in commuting technologies provide an alternative policy lever to relaxing housing supply constraints — a possibility informally discussed in the housing supply literature but for which Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) provide the first quantitative empirical evidence.

## Commuting Cost Reductions and Welfare

The model also provides a platform for evaluating the aggregate welfare implications of changes in commuting costs. Using observed commuting flows between US counties from 1990 and 2010, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) apply a Head-Ries (2001) adjustment to back out the implied changes in the ease of commuting. Between these two decades, the median implied reduction in commuting costs was 12 percent, with a twenty-fifth percentile reduction of 4 percent and a seventy-fifth percentile reduction of 21 percent.

Counterfactual simulations using these empirically grounded changes show substantial welfare effects: reducing commuting costs by the median 1990–2010 decline increases welfare by approximately 3.3 percent — comparable to the gains from opening the US economy to international trade in canonical trade models — and by 6.9 percent at the seventy-fifth percentile ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). The gains arise because commuting lets workers work in high-productivity locations while living in high-amenity ones, concentrating employment in net commuter-importing locations (such as Manhattan) and residents in net commuter-exporting locations (such as parts of New Jersey). The spatial pattern of employment changes is strongly predicted by the initial employment-to-residents ratio, a variable difficult to proxy with land area, size, or housing-supply elasticities.

## Chinese City-Size Application

The elasticity logic is also load-bearing for the Chinese debate over whether governments should restrain urbanisation, where the relevant openness margin is migration access rather than commuting. Wu and You (2025) build a quantitative spatial model with agglomeration and congestion forces, calibrate it to 340 Chinese prefectural cities between 2000 and 2020, and reach a nuanced diagnosis: from the national perspective most of the largest Chinese cities are too small, but from the local perspective they are already too large. Because equilibrium city sizes deviate from both local and national optima, the hukou system's internal migration restrictions — modelled as a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives — trade off correcting for city-size deviations against the welfare cost of distorting mobility. Averaged over 2000–2020, the hukou policy imposes a GDP loss of 13.6 percent and a welfare loss of 4.7 percent, and restricting any single city's size through further migration barriers is generally welfare-reducing even though some cities are locally oversized ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). The policy lever that most directly determines how employment and city-size responses translate into welfare is the price elasticity of urban land supply: increasing it has the largest effect on aggregate welfare, but the scarcity of developable land in the most productive sites is the fundamental constraint behind the trade-off between promoting and restraining urbanisation ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

This Chinese application mirrors the MRRH result from the opposite direction. Whereas in the United States the local employment response operates through the commuting margin, in China the adjustment margin runs through migration and the firm-entry margin, since a larger city population supports more firms and varieties, and the resulting love-of-variety gains are a crucial part of the local impacts of migration ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). The same structural logic that makes commuting openness the summary statistic for US employment elasticities therefore reappears in China as migration and land-supply policy, tying the concept to the wider hukou and land-quota debate ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** In China the employment-elasticity logic is channelled through hukou and land-supply policy rather than through commuting alone, and the relevant "openness" margin is migration access. The Wu–You finding that large cities are locally oversized but nationally undersized is the empirical backbone of the land-supply and migration-policy debate, and it shows that whether restraining city size raises or lowers welfare depends on which margin — migration, land supply, or commuting — is the binding constraint.

## Empirical Signatures and Implications for Research

The framework yields several testable predictions distinguishing it from a closed-economy approach. First, commuting openness should correlate positively with the employment response to a labor demand shock, with the residence own commuting share accounting for the great majority of the cross-sectional variation. Second, the correlation with the resident response should be substantially weaker, since commuting lets employment adjust without commensurate changes in residents. Third, the commuting distance elasticity should exceed the trade distance elasticity — confirmed by $-4.43$ for commuting versus $-1.29$ for trade ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]). Fourth, standard empirical controls — employment, wages, land area, neighbor characteristics — are substantially less successful than commuting openness in explaining heterogeneity in treatment effects. This finding suggests that reduced-form studies of local labor demand shocks should routinely include measures of commuting openness in their specifications, as these variables can capture in a parsimonious way the otherwise unmodeled spatial spillovers through factor markets ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). The within-city and Chinese evidence extends these signatures to finer spatial scales and to migration access ([[ahlfeldt-redding-sturm-wolf-2015-economics-density]]; [[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The broader lesson for the local labor markets literature is that the spatial structure of commuting networks conditions the local impact of demand shocks in ways that standard empirical proxies — county size, density, industrial composition — do not capture. Researchers evaluating place-based policies or estimating local multipliers should either incorporate commuting openness directly or recognize that their estimated treatment effects are weighted averages over locations with potentially very different underlying elasticities. The quantitative spatial framework provides a template for analyzing these interactions in any spatial context where bilateral commuting flow data are available.

## Open Questions

Three open questions connect the concept to the wider corpus. First, skill heterogeneity: the Bogotá finding that high-skilled commuters have a lower commuting elasticity implies that aggregate elasticities conceal who bears the incidence of transport and place-based policies, and the analogous skill margin in Chinese migration — hukou holders versus migrants — conditions the distributional effects of urban policy ([[tsivanidis-2026-urban-transit-infrastructure]]; [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]). Second, endogenous commuting networks: the commuting openness that determines local employment elasticities is itself a product of transport infrastructure and urban form, so policy changes that alter the network also alter the very elasticities used to evaluate them ([[monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting]]; [[redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial]]). Third, the interaction of commuting openness with migration policy: the US evidence operates with free internal migration, whereas in China, where migration access is the binding margin, the elasticity logic must be re-expressed in terms of who can move and where land can be built ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]; [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]).
