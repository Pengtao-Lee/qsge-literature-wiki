Local Employment Elasticities and Commuting

Conceptsconceptactive

The local employment elasticity measures the percentage change in local employment by workplace in response to a one-percent change in local labor demand, typically proxied by a productivity shock. This parameter is central to evaluating the employment effects of local tax incentives, infrastructure investments, and place-based policies. In a closed-economy framework without spatial linkages, this elasticity is a single constant determined by local factor supply conditions. However, once commuting and migration linkages between locations are recognized, the local employment elasticity becomes an endogenous variable that varies systematically across locations depending on their degree of commuting openness — the extent to which residents can work in nearby employment centers without relocating. The key mechanism is that commuting allows employment and residence to decouple: a location receiving many inward commuters can expand employment substantially without a commensurate increase in residents, and therefore without experiencing the full force of local land-price congestion. This heterogeneity has direct implications for how reduced-form estimates of local employment multipliers should be interpreted and applied across different settings.

The Conventional Approach: Single-Elasticity Local Labor Markets

A large empirical literature has estimated the effects of local labor demand shocks using sources of variation including sectoral composition (Bartik shocks), productivity, international trade, natural resource abundance, and business cycle fluctuations (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). The standard approach defines local labor markets as commuting zones (CZs) — spatial aggregates designed to minimize commuting flows across boundaries — and treats these zones as independent observations in reduced-form regressions. Within this framework, the local employment elasticity is typically assumed to be a single parameter that applies across treated locations, with little attention paid to the linkages between spatial units in goods and factor markets (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting; redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial).

A central limitation of this approach is that no choice of boundaries for local labor markets can completely eliminate commuting spillovers. For a typical US county in 2006—2010, around 27 percent of residents work outside the county and 20 percent of workers live outside it; at the ninety-fifth percentile, these figures rise to 59 and 43 percent respectively (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). Even at the CZ level, substantial commuting beyond boundaries remains — the median CZ has around 7 percent of its residents working outside and the ninety-fifth percentile exceeds 20 percent. Furthermore, the share of residents working locally declined markedly over recent decades, from a median of 91 percent in 1960 to 69 percent in 2000, reflecting the expansion of transportation infrastructure and the growing spatial separation of workplace and residence (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). These patterns suggest that counties differ substantially in how labor market shocks spill across their boundaries, and that a single-elasticity approach risks substantial bias.

A Quantitative Spatial Framework with Commuting and Migration

Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) develop a quantitative general equilibrium model that incorporates spatial linkages in both goods markets (trade) and factor markets (commuting and migration), building on the broader class of quantitative spatial models surveyed in Redding and Rossi-Hansberg (2017). The framework allows for an arbitrary number of locations that differ in productivity, amenities, and their geographical relationship to one another. Workers have Cobb-Douglas preferences over final goods consumption and residential land use, and face idiosyncratic amenity shocks drawn from a Fréchet distribution for each residence-workplace pair. These idiosyncratic shocks imply that workers make heterogeneous choices about where to live and work when faced with the same prices and wages, giving rise to upward-sloping supply functions for both residents and workers at each location.

Heterogeneous Local Employment Elasticities

Using the quantified model, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) compute 3,111 counterfactual exercises in which each US county receives a five percent productivity shock while holding all other exogenous variables constant. The resulting general equilibrium elasticities of local employment display substantial heterogeneity, ranging from approximately 0.5 to 2.5 across counties, with a mean of around 1.52. In contrast, the elasticity of residents (as distinct from employment by workplace) ranges from approximately 0.2 to 1.2, with less dispersion. Since employment and residents can differ only through commuting, this contrast itself indicates that commuting links are the primary source of the heterogeneity in employment responses.

Across the full distribution of US counties, the local employment elasticity ranges from roughly 0.5 to 2.5, and this substantial heterogeneity is robust to the introduction of variable land supply elasticities, spatially correlated shocks reproducing the industrial composition of the US economy, and aggregation to commuting zones rather than counties (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting).

The key determinant of this heterogeneity is the openness of the local labor market to commuting, measured by the residence own commuting share λii∣iR\lambda_{ii|i}^{R}λii∣iR​ — the fraction of residents who work in the same county where they live. This single variable alone explains 89 percent of the cross-county variation in local employment elasticities, far exceeding the explanatory power of standard empirical controls such as county employment, wages, and land area, which together achieve an R2R^2R2 of only about 0.5 (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). Counties with more open commuting markets (lower own commuting shares) exhibit higher employment elasticities because they can draw additional workers from surrounding areas without requiring large increases in the local resident population and therefore without bidding up land prices as severely.

The heterogeneity is not an artifact of the specific model structure. As shown in the online appendix of Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018), heterogeneous local employment elasticities are a generic prediction of the entire class of models consistent with a gravity equation for commuting flows. The finding is also robust to the use of alternative measures of commuting openness, including the workplace own commuting share, the average of the residence and workplace shares, and their minimum.

Quasi-Experimental Evidence from Million Dollar Plants

To provide independent empirical evidence for the role of commuting, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) exploit quasi-experimental variation from competitions for large manufacturing plants (Million Dollar Plants, or MDPs), following the design of Greenstone, Hornbeck, and Moretti (2010). In these competitions, firms publicly report the winning county and one or two runner-up counties that survived a lengthy selection process but narrowly lost. The identifying assumption is that runner-up counties — having demonstrated similar profitability for the plant — provide a valid counterfactual for the winner, conditional on observables.

An event-study analysis of 82 MDP announcements from 1972 to 2003 shows no statistically significant differences in employment between winner and runner-up counties in the ten years before the announcement. After the announcement, employment in winner counties rises relative to runner-up counties, reaching approximately 4 percent higher by ten years after the event (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). This timing pattern supports the identifying assumption and is consistent with gradual expansion through direct plant operations and indirect multiplier effects on suppliers and ancillary services.

The central prediction of the model — that employment responses should be larger in more open commuting markets — is tested by interacting the treatment effect with the residence own commuting share. In the baseline specification, the estimated interaction coefficient is negative and statistically significant: a county completely closed to commuting (λii∣iR=1\lambda_{ii|i}^{R} = 1λii∣iR​=1) experiences virtually no employment increase from the MDP, while a county with an own commuting share of zero would see an increase of approximately 25 percent (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). The standardized coefficient for the commuting interaction is close in absolute magnitude to the standardized coefficient for the main treatment effect, underscoring the economic relevance of commuting openness. These results are robust across alternative commuting measures, the inclusion of industry-year fixed effects, and census-region-year or state-year fixed effects. An event-study specification for the commuting interaction further confirms the absence of pre-trends.

Current assessment (2026-07): The MDP runner-up design provides some of the cleanest quasi-experimental evidence for commuting-driven heterogeneity in local employment responses, as the comparison of winners and runners-up within the same plant competition holds constant both the scale and the timing of the labor demand shock — features that are rarely available in reduced-form studies relying on cross-sectional or panel variation in commuting openness alone. However, the MDP sample is limited to 82 cases, and the external validity of these large-plant shocks for the effects of smaller or more diffuse labor demand shifts remains an open question.

Housing Supply and Commuting as Alternative Adjustment Channels

An important implication of the framework is that commuting and housing supply elasticity provide alternative channels through which locations can accommodate increases in labor demand. When the model is extended to incorporate the Saiz (2010) estimates of developed land supply elasticities — which range from 0.76 to 3.09 across US metropolitan areas — the heterogeneity in local employment elasticities remains largely unchanged. The variable land supply elasticity primarily affects the resident elasticity, shifting its distribution rightward and increasing its dispersion, while the employment elasticity distribution is affected much less (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). This pattern is intuitive: commuting allows workers to access high-productivity employment locations with inelastic housing supplies without actually residing there and paying the resulting high land prices. Consequently, improvements in commuting technologies provide an alternative policy lever to relaxing housing supply constraints for enabling workers to access productive locations — a possibility that had been informally discussed in the housing supply literature but for which Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) provide the first quantitative empirical evidence.

Commuting Cost Reductions and Welfare

The model also provides a platform for evaluating the aggregate welfare implications of changes in commuting costs. Using observed commuting flows between US counties from 1990 and 2010, Monte, Redding, and Rossi-Hansberg (2018) apply a Head-Ries (2001) adjustment to back out the implied changes in the ease of commuting. Between these two decades, the median implied reduction in commuting costs was 12 percent, with a twenty-fifth percentile reduction of 4 percent and a seventy-fifth percentile reduction of 21 percent.

Counterfactual simulations using these empirically grounded changes show substantial welfare effects. Reducing commuting costs by the median 1990—2010 decline increases welfare by approximately 3.3 percent — a magnitude comparable to the welfare gains from opening the US economy to international trade as estimated in canonical trade models (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). At the seventy-fifth percentile reduction, the welfare gain rises to 6.9 percent. These welfare gains arise because commuting enables workers to separate workplace from residence, allowing them to work in high-productivity locations while living in high-amenity locations. Reducing commuting costs increases the concentration of employment in net commuter-importing locations (such as Manhattan) and enhances the clustering of residents in net commuter-exporting locations (such as parts of New Jersey). The spatial pattern of employment changes is strongly predicted by the initial ratio of employment to residents — itself a variable that is difficult to proxy with standard controls like land area, size, or housing supply elasticities.

Empirical Signatures and Implications for Research

The framework yields several testable predictions that distinguish it from a closed-economy approach to local labor markets. First, there should be a positive and economically significant correlation between commuting openness and the employment response to a given labor demand shock, with the residence own commuting share accounting for the great majority of the cross-sectional variation. Second, the correlation between commuting openness and the resident response should be substantially weaker, since commuting allows employment to adjust without commensurate changes in the number of residents. Third, the commuting distance elasticity should exceed the trade distance elasticity, reflecting the higher cost of moving people relative to goods — a prediction confirmed by the estimates of −4.43-4.43−4.43 for commuting and −1.29-1.29−1.29 for trade (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting). Fourth, standard empirical controls routinely used in the local labor markets literature — employment, wages, land area, and neighbor characteristics — are substantially less successful than measures of commuting openness in explaining heterogeneity in treatment effects. This finding suggests that reduced-form studies of local labor demand shocks should routinely include measures of commuting openness in their specifications, as these variables can capture in a parsimonious way the otherwise unmodeled spatial spillovers through factor markets (monte-redding-rossi-hansberg-2018-commuting; redding-rossi-hansberg-2017-quantitative-spatial).

Current assessment (2026-07): The broader lesson for the local labor markets literature is that the spatial structure of commuting networks conditions the local impact of demand shocks in ways that standard empirical proxies — county size, density, industrial composition — do not capture. Researchers evaluating place-based policies or estimating local multipliers should either incorporate commuting openness directly or recognize that their estimated treatment effects are weighted averages over locations with potentially very different underlying elasticities. The quantitative spatial framework provides a template for analyzing these interactions in any spatial context where bilateral commuting flow data are available.
