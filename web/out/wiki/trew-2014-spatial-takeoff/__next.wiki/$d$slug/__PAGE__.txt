## Research Question

How did the spatial distribution of economic activity — in particular, the concentration of manufacturing productivity in the North and agricultural productivity around London — affect whether, when, and where an industrial takeoff occurred in England between 1710 and 1860? Can a dynamic spatial general equilibrium model, calibrated to parish-level occupational data, replicate both the aggregate facts (growth, structural transformation, relative prices) and the spatially heterogeneous patterns (deindustrialisation of the South, emergence of northern manufacturing hotspots)?

## Model / Experimental Design

The paper adapts the **Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg (forthcoming)** spatial development framework, extending it with:

- **Non-homothetic Stone–Geary preferences** (equation 2): $U = (c_A - \gamma)^\eta c_M^{1-\eta}$, with subsistence requirement $\gamma = 0.06$ and manufacturing share $\eta = 0.2$. As income grows, the marginal utility of agricultural consumption falls, driving structural transformation toward manufactures.

- **Two-sector endogenous innovation**: Firms in agriculture (A) and manufacturing (M) may invest in a probability $\phi$ of drawing an improved technology from a Pareto distribution with parameter $a_i$ ($a_A = 295$, $a_M = 70$). Innovation is subject to fixed costs $\psi_{1,i}$ ($\psi_{1,A}=0.5832$, $\psi_{1,M}=0.9340$) and marginal costs $\psi_{2,i}=0.002$. The optimal innovation probability is $\phi_i^*(\ell,t) = 1 - [\psi_{2,i}(a_i-1)/(p_i Z_i \hat{L}_i^\iota)]^{1/2}$ — a **scale effect**: larger firms invest more in innovation.

- **Energy cost channel**: Manufacturing innovation carries an additional location-specific energy cost $\Gamma_M \xi(\ell)$, reflecting coal transport costs based on Allen (2009) and Clark and Jacks (2007). Energy prices are highest in London (7x Newcastle) and decline monotonically toward northern coalfields.

- **Spatial structure**: A one-dimensional continuum of locations [0,1] (mapped to England's north-south axis via 500 discrete units). Technology diffuses across space with decay $\delta = 15$. Goods trade incurs iceberg cost $e^{-\kappa|\ell-r|}$ with $\kappa = 0.008$. Land is allocated to the highest-bidding sector.

**Calibration strategy:**

- Initial productivity distributions $Z_A(\ell,0)$ and $Z_M(\ell,0)$ are *inverted* from the c. 1710 male employment data of Shaw-Taylor et al. (2010a,b) using first-order conditions from the model (equations 19–20), with missing parish observations imputed via OLS regression on c. 1817 data ($R^2 = 0.82$ for total employment).
- A date-0 diffusion step smooths the London productivity peak before simulation proper begins.
- 500 spatial units; 150 time periods (1710–1860); 30 Monte Carlo runs per experiment.
- Preference parameters $\gamma, \eta$ chosen to match aggregate employment shares; innovation cost parameters set so agricultural innovation begins at t=1 and manufacturing at t=61 (targeting Crafts–Harley takeoff timing).

## Main Results

**Baseline equilibrium (Section 5):**

- **Agricultural innovation** begins at t=1 in the South, concentrated around London, consistent with Allen (2004). Higher agricultural output raises income, and non-homothetic preferences increase demand for manufactures.
- **Labour reallocation**: Manufacturing employment expands spatially into the North via land-use shift (visible after t=40). Initially, this *lowers* average manufacturing productivity because the new northern firms are small and do not yet innovate (Fig. 4.2.1).
- **Manufacturing takeoff** occurs at **t=61 at location $\ell=305$** (Lancashire) — a compromise between proximity to London's market, access to cheap northern coal, and initial manufacturing productivity. Once northern firms reach sufficient scale, the fixed cost of innovation is overcome.
- **Aggregate growth takeoff** matches the Crafts–Harley (1992) chronology: muted 18th-century growth (~0.3–0.5%), acceleration to ~2% after 1830 (Fig. 5).

**Fit to data (Section 5.1):**

- Aggregate employment shares: model tracks the slow decline in primary (43.4%→25.2%) and modest rise in secondary (38.7%→46.8%) shares across 1710–1861.
- Relative prices: manufacturing relative price first rises (as agricultural productivity advances), then falls sharply after manufacturing takeoff.
- Real wages and land rents: model tracks the takeoff path in Clark (2002) data.
- **Spatial match**: The northern manufacturing hotspot at $\ell=305$ fits the 1817 and 1861 Shaw-Taylor data well in both location and magnitude (Figs. 7, 8). The model matches textile employment (the leading sector) particularly closely. The disappearance of London as a secondary producer is captured. Primary employment's spatial concentration is somewhat overpredicted.

**Geographical counterfactuals (Section 6, Fig. 9):**

- **Flat distribution (Panel B):** All manufacturing productivity peaks removed, labour spread evenly. Takeoff occurs *immediately and everywhere* — nearly all northern firms reach innovation scale, and all benefit from low energy costs. London's manufacturing labour was, paradoxically, a *drag* on takeoff by raising the scale threshold elsewhere.
- **London peak only (Panel C):** Productivity peak in London retained, northern peaks flattened. **No manufacturing takeoff occurs within 150 periods.** The London agglomeration dominates, but its high energy costs prevent innovation; without northern productivity peaks, no location reaches innovation scale.
- **Smoothed distribution (Panel D):** As in Panel C — no takeoff. Northern productivity peaks, though small, are essential.

**Policy experiments (Section 8, Table 4):**

- 5% reduction in agricultural fixed cost ($0.95 \cdot \psi_{A,1}$): takeoff advances to t=53.6 (3.4 years earlier), location unchanged at $\ell=305$.
- 5% reduction in manufacturing fixed cost ($0.95 \cdot \psi_{M,1}$): takeoff advances to t=43.3 (13.7 years earlier), location shifts north to $\ell=324$–325 (closer to Newcastle coal).
- 1% subsidy to manufacturing land rent: takeoff **delayed** to t=89.3 (32.3 years later), with high variance (s.d. 22.6). Subsidised manufacturing spreads out spatially, reducing firm scale and delaying innovation.
- 1% subsidy to agricultural land rent: no significant effect (t=57.8).
- 56% reduction in London coal prices needed to relocate takeoff to London — infrastructure policy less cost-effective than direct innovation support.

**International trade (Section 7):**

- Exogenous net manufacturing exports through London bring takeoff forward ~25 years by raising the relative price of manufactures and accelerating labour shift.
- Location of trade entry (London vs. Liverpool) has negligible effect on takeoff timing (~1 year difference).

## Mechanisms Identified

> **Paper claim** — The spatial concentration of productivity determines whether firms in any location reach the scale necessary to amortise the fixed cost of innovation; the energy cost of manufacturing innovation (proportional to coal transport costs) then determines *where* among those locations takeoff first occurs.

1. **Scale effect in innovation** (equation 14): $\phi_i^*(\ell,t) = 1 - [\psi_{2,i}(a_i-1)/(p_i Z_i \hat{L}_i^\iota)]^{1/2}$. Innovation probability increases with firm size (labour deployed). This is the core mechanism linking spatial concentration to takeoff timing.

2. **Non-homothetic demand channel**: Stone–Geary preferences with subsistence $\gamma > 0$ generate structural transformation endogenously: rising agricultural productivity increases income, reduces marginal utility of food, and raises demand for manufactures — which pulls labour and land into manufacturing.

3. **Agricultural-innovation-led precursor**: Early agricultural innovation (t=1 onward, concentrated in the South) releases labour and raises income, setting the stage for manufacturing takeoff but not sufficient to trigger it alone — echoing Matsuyama (1992) and Gollin et al. (2002).

4. **Energy-cost geography**: Manufacturing innovation costs vary spatially with coal transport costs ($\xi(\ell)$, proportional to firm output). This locates the first manufacturing innovation at $\ell=305$ — near Lancashire coal — rather than in high-productivity but energy-expensive London.

5. **Agglomeration vs. dispersion trade-off**: Transport costs ($\kappa=0.008$) concentrate activity; congestion from diminishing returns to labour ($\mu < 1$) disperses it. The balance determines firm scale and thus innovation capacity.

## Methods and Measures

Links: [[wiki/methods/_index]], [[wiki/measures/_index]]

- **Continuum-of-space general equilibrium model**: Adapted from Desmet and Rossi-Hansberg (forthcoming) with non-homothetic preferences and sector-specific innovation costs. One-dimensional interval [0,1] with 500 discrete spatial units, solved using the Rossi-Hansberg (2005) excess-supply approach.
- **Productivity inversion from employment data**: First-order conditions (equations 19–20) used to back out initial $Z_A(\ell,0)$ and $Z_M(\ell,0)$ from observed parish-level employment, conditional on model-consistent prices and wages solved jointly.
- **Parish-level occupational data**: Shaw-Taylor et al. (2010a,b) c. 1710 data (1,062 of ~11,000 parishes observed). Missing observations imputed via OLS regression on c. 1817 data with geographical covariates, aggregated to 624 registration districts.
- **Counterfactual productivity distributions**: Four transformations of initial $Z_M$ (Fig. 9) — flattened, London-only peak, smoothed — holding aggregate mean constant to isolate spatial-distribution effects.
- **Monte Carlo methodology**: 30 simulations per parameter configuration to account for stochastic innovation draws (Pareto-distributed $\hat{z}$).

## Concepts Engaged

- **Spatial takeoff**: The transition from low-growth agriculture to high-growth industry is not spatially uniform — it originates at a specific location (Lancashire, $\ell=305$) where local conditions (firm scale + low energy costs) first overcome innovation thresholds.
- **Structural transformation**: Non-homothetic preferences drive labour reallocation from agriculture to manufacturing. The model shows that this reallocation can initially *lower* average manufacturing productivity as new, small firms enter before reaching innovation scale.
- **Endogenous innovation with fixed costs**: Innovation is not automatic — it requires firms to reach a minimum scale. This ties the timing of aggregate takeoff to the spatial distribution of activity.
- **Economic geography of the Industrial Revolution**: The North–South divide in England — agricultural South, industrial North — is an equilibrium outcome determined by the interaction of initial productivity, energy costs, and transport costs.
- **Coal and energy costs**: The spatial variation in coal transport costs (7:1 London-to-Newcastle price ratio) is a first-order determinant of where manufacturing innovation first becomes profitable.

## Connection to Debates

> **Paper claim** — The paper provides quantitative evidence that the *spatial distribution* of economic activity is not merely a consequence of industrialisation but a *determinant* of its timing: the presence of geographically concentrated manufacturing productivity in the North of England was a necessary condition for takeoff, and policies that reduced spatial concentration (land subsidies) could significantly delay industrialisation.

The paper speaks to:

- **Why England? (Allen 2009)** — The paper formalises Allen's wage-and-energy story in a general equilibrium spatial framework, showing that high wages alone are insufficient; the *location* of cheap energy relative to productivity concentrations determines whether an endogenous innovation response occurs.
- **Crafts–Harley growth revisionism** — The model reproduces the muted 18th-century growth followed by acceleration after 1830, providing a micro-founded spatial mechanism (slow scale accumulation in northern firms) consistent with the revisionist macro facts.
- **Role of agriculture in industrialisation (Matsuyama 1992; Gollin et al. 2002)** — Agricultural innovation is confirmed as a precursor, but the paper shows it is *not* sufficient: structural transformation in employment shares can proceed far in advance of a growth takeoff, matching the English data.
- **Spatial policy and development** — The counterfactual policy experiments (Section 8) suggest that governments have limited ability to *accelerate* takeoff (subsidies help modestly) but significant ability to *delay* it (land-use subsidies weaken agglomeration).

Links: [[wiki/debates/_index]]

## Theoretical / Empirical Significance

This paper is among the first to bring a **quantitative spatial general equilibrium model with endogenous innovation** to bear on the First Industrial Revolution, using newly available **parish-level occupational data**. It demonstrates that:

1. The spatial distribution of productivity is a first-order determinant of *whether* an industrial takeoff occurs at all (Section 6 counterfactuals), not just of *where*.
2. A model with non-homothetic preferences and scale-dependent innovation can simultaneously match aggregate growth, employment shares, relative prices, wages, land rents, and the spatial location of industrial activity — a much richer set of moments than previous exercises.
3. The paper establishes the baseline framework (Trew 2014) that Trew (2020) later extends with endogenous infrastructure. The 2014 framework embeds the energy-cost channel that drives the 2020 paper's location results, and the counterfactual method used here (geographical re-distributions, policy parameter shifts) is inherited and extended in the later work.
4. The finding that policies reducing spatial concentration can *delay* takeoff is a theoretically grounded caution for development policy in low-income countries today.

## Notes and Caveats

- **No capital**: Growth results solely from technological progress, not capital accumulation. Mokyr (2004) attributes 70–80% of growth to TFP, justifying this simplification for a first pass, but capital-skill complementarity and investment dynamics are absent.
- **No population growth or demographic transition**: Labour supply is fixed, so the model cannot speak to unified growth theory (Galor 2005) channels. Wrigley et al. (1997) find no significant geographical fertility/mortality differences in England, supporting the simplification for spatial analysis.
- **No tertiary sector**: The paper abstracts from services entirely. Shaw-Taylor et al. (2010a) find the tertiary sector was the most dynamic in the 19th century — this is a major limitation, partially addressed in Trew (2020).
- **Single manufacturing sector**: The model has one secondary sector. Textile employment matches well (Fig. 8), but other manufacturing sub-sectors may have different spatial and energy-cost profiles.
- **Complete long-run specialisation**: The model predicts complete North–South specialisation (South in agriculture, North in manufacturing), which is not observed historically — Southern industrial employment increased later in the 19th century.
- **One-dimensional geography**: The north-south interval is a strong simplification; east-west variation, the shape of coastlines (affecting coastal shipping), and multi-dimensional transport networks are abstracted.
- **Static infrastructure**: Transport costs are exogenous and time-invariant. Trew (2020) endogenises infrastructure and finds that the spatial distribution of improvements matters as much as their level.
- **No optimal policy**: Policy experiments are positive counterfactuals (parameter shifts) with no welfare analysis or consideration of fiscal costs.
- **Data limitations**: Only ~10% of parishes observed in c. 1710; imputation relies on the stability of spatial patterns across 100+ years (c. 1710 → 1817 regression).
