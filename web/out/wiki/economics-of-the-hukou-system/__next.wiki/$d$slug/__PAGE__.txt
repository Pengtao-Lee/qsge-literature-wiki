5:T3179,The hukou (household registration) system is China's institutional mechanism for controlling internal migration and determining access to local public services. Established in the 1950s, it assigns each citizen a registration status (agricultural or non-agricultural) in a specific location; changing one's hukou location or category requires government approval, which is difficult to obtain, especially in large cities. In economic terms, the hukou system functions as a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives: migrants without local hukou face higher prices for publicly-provided services such as education and healthcare, restricted access to social security, and limited employment rights. Unlike sunk moving costs, hukou costs are recurring flow costs that persist as long as the migrant lacks local hukou. In quantitative spatial models, these costs are parameterized as multiplicative disutility or fiscal wedges that reduce the effective wage migrants receive, creating a gap between the marginal product of labor and the private return to migration. The system is the single most important spatial distortion in the Chinese economy and a central friction in every major QSE study of China.

## Institutional background (1950s–2020s)

The hukou system was formally instituted in 1958 to control population mobility and maintain social stability. Before the economic reforms began in 1978, individuals were prohibited from living or working outside their hukou county. This prohibition was relaxed in the 1980s, but workers without local hukou still had to apply for a temporary residence permit, a difficult and risky process. A nationwide administrative reform in 2003 greatly streamlined the process for obtaining permits, and several coastal provinces eliminated the requirement altogether [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. By 2000, the share of Chinese living outside their birth county had risen to 15%, up from only 3% in 1990; by 2010, this share had grown to 18% [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

Despite these relaxations, the system has not been abolished. Today, each Chinese city operates its own hukou policy under national guidelines, with city-specific quotas and point-based systems. The most desirable cities—Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen—maintain the most restrictive policies, while small and medium cities have largely liberalized their hukou requirements. The result is a highly differentiated system: hukou policy is not a single national barrier but a spectrum of city-specific restrictions that vary with a city's economic appeal.

## The hukou as a fiscal wedge

The economic essence of the hukou system is that it creates a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives. Migrants without local hukou face higher user fees for publicly-provided private goods—education, healthcare, and social benefits—while paying the same taxes as natives. This wedge enters the individual's location choice as a reduction in the effective real wage available outside one's hukou city.

Wu and You (2025) estimate this wedge using a revealed-preference approach in the spirit of Rosen (1979) and Roback (1982): individuals' location choices reveal how they trade off local hukou status against income differences. The estimated average fiscal wedge was 50.7% of destination wage income in 2000, declining to 32.5% in 2005, 15.0% in 2010, and 15.0% in 2015. These magnitudes indicate that the costs of lacking local hukou were initially very high and declined quickly but remained substantial. Chen, Shi, and Tang (2019) provide corroborating evidence from housing markets, estimating the market valuation of urban hukou in Jinan—a tier-2 city—at approximately 90,000–126,000 RMB ($13,314–$18,639, or 1.5–2.1 times GDP per capita) in 2017 [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]].

## Modeling hukou in QSE frameworks

The hukou system enters QSE models through three distinct approaches, each capturing different features of the friction.

**Tombe-Zhu approach.** Tombe and Zhu (2019) model migration costs as recurring flow costs (rather than sunk costs), motivated by the hukou system's ongoing nature: a migrant without local hukou faces higher costs every period. In their Eaton-Kortum framework with 30 provinces and two sectors (agriculture and non-agriculture), workers choose locations based on real wages, migration costs, land-ownership rebates, and idiosyncratic Fréchet-distributed preferences. Migration costs include both symmetric geographic costs (distance) and an additional loss of land income when leaving one's home region—a feature motivated by China's collective land ownership, where migrants forfeit access to collectively-owned land [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]].

**Wu-You approach.** Wu and You (2025) develop a richer dynamic framework with 340 prefectures, two skill groups, and forward-looking migration decisions. The hukou system enters through two channels: (1) skill-specific hukou lottery probabilities (θ), capturing the chance that a migrant obtains local hukou, and (2) a fiscal wedge (λ), capturing the price discount that natives enjoy for publicly-provided private goods. The hukou-granting probability is estimated directly from census microdata: in tier-1 cities in 2010, only 3.3% of migrants with primary schooling received a local hukou, compared to 37.3% of college graduates, and the probability is monotonically increasing in educational attainment across all city tiers [[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]. The fiscal wedge is then estimated from a structural revealed-preference equation. This approach captures the fact that hukou status is a state variable in workers' dynamic optimization: obtaining local hukou changes a worker's entire future stream of migration options.

**Ma-Tang approach.** Ma and Tang (2020) model migration barriers as origin-destination-specific disutility parameters (δ) in a Melitz-style framework with endogenous firm entry. They estimate these parameters using Simulated Method of Moments (SMM) on bilateral migration flow moments from 279 prefectures. The destination-specific barriers are identified from the difference between predicted and observed migration flows into each city, controlling for geographic costs. Their estimates reveal that Shanghai's barriers are 24% above the national average, followed by Guangzhou (13%) and Beijing (8%) [[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]].

> ***Cross-pattern convergence**: Despite using different modeling frameworks—Eaton-Kortum (Tombe-Zhu), dynamic discrete choice with fiscal wedges (Wu-You), and Melitz with SMM (Ma-Tang)—all three approaches converge on the finding that hukou costs are large and economically significant, though their mapping to specific parameter values differs across methods.*

## Estimated magnitudes of hukou costs

The estimated magnitude of hukou costs varies across frameworks but consistently points to very large barriers. Tombe and Zhu (2019) find that in 2000, the average cost of rural-to-urban migration within a province was equivalent to shrinking real income by a factor of nearly 3, while between-province moves were an order of magnitude more costly (a factor of ~25). By 2005, migration costs had declined to 82% of their 2000 level, with between-province costs falling to 61% of their initial level—a drop driven by the 2003 administrative reforms and province-specific hukou liberalizations.

Wu and You (2025) translate the aggregate consequences into macro-level losses: the hukou system as a whole created a GDP loss of 13.6% and a welfare loss of 4.7% averaged over 2000–2020. The aggregate welfare loss is smaller than the GDP loss because rising housing prices in destination cities partially offset wage gains. In a robustness check varying key parameters, the 20-year-average GDP effect ranges from 8.6% to 19.1% and the welfare effect from 2.1% to 7.9%. Ma and Tang (2020) find that the additional barriers in the four largest cities (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen) result in a 0.7% to 3.9% loss in national welfare.

## Hukou reform and its effects

The hukou system has undergone significant but incomplete reform. The 2003 administrative reform that streamlined temporary residence permits reduced between-province migration costs by nearly 40% and was the single largest policy-driven reduction in measured migration costs [[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]. Between 2000 and 2005, within-province rural-to-urban migrants increased by roughly 15% and between-province migrants by 82%, largely due to these cost reductions.

The post-2014 period saw a new wave of reform: the central government pushed for hukou liberalization in small and medium cities (urban population under 3 million) while maintaining or tightening restrictions in the largest cities. Tier-1 cities introduced point-based systems that favor college graduates, skilled workers, and investors. The result is a "two-track" system: hukou has become nearly irrelevant for moving to small cities but remains a binding constraint for moving to Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. The Wu-You estimates of the fiscal wedge confirm this: the wedge declined from 50.7% to 15.0% of destination wage between 2000 and 2015, reflecting substantial liberalization, but the remaining wedge is concentrated in the largest cities.

## Hukou vs. other migration barriers

The hukou system differs from standard migration barriers in several economically important ways. First, hukou costs are policy-determined rather than geography-determined: while geographic distance and cultural differences affect migration everywhere, hukou costs are created and enforced by government regulation. Second, hukou costs are recurring flow costs, not one-time moving expenses: a migrant must pay the hukou premium every period, whereas typical migration barriers (information frictions, moving expenses, cultural adjustment) are largely sunk. Third, hukou interacts with land institutions: because rural land is collectively owned and lacks a rental market, migrants who leave their hukou location lose access to land-based income, which Tombe and Zhu (2019) estimate adds a factor of ~1.9 to the overall migration cost. This institutional interaction means that hukou reform alone may be insufficient—land reform that severs the link between registration and land rights is a natural complement.

## Frontier: QSE policy translation for China

The evolution of hukou policy in the 2020s raises new questions for QSE modeling. Most Chinese cities have now relaxed hukou restrictions entirely, leaving only the very largest cities—Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and a few others—with binding constraints. The key policy question is therefore not whether to abolish the system but how to manage the transition in the remaining restricted cities.

Partial reform creates complex dynamics that QSE models are only beginning to capture. For example, liberalizing hukou for college graduates only (as Beijing and Shanghai currently do) changes the skill composition of migration, with implications for local public finances, housing markets, and aggregate productivity. The digitalization of public services—e-health consultations, online education platforms, and digital social security portability—may reduce the hukou wedge by enabling migrants to access services remotely without changing their registration status ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]). To the extent that these digital substitutes lower the fiscal wedge, they reduce both the distortionary effect of the hukou system and the welfare gain from fully abolishing it.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Post-2020 reforms have substantially reduced measured hukou costs, but the remaining barriers in the largest cities continue to generate economically significant distortions. The QSE literature provides robust evidence that eliminating these residual barriers would raise aggregate welfare substantially, though the distributional consequences require complementary policies.
