The welfare effect of migration on destination-city residents is the central quantitative question in China's urbanization debate. When workers flow into a city, they simultaneously depress nominal wages through increased labor supply, bid up housing costs and crowd public services, but also expand local market size, induce entry of new firms, increase product variety, and lower the price of goods. The net local welfare effect is theoretically ambiguous and depends critically on the market structure of the goods-producing sector. Under an Eaton-Kortum framework with perfect competition and no firm entry margin — as in the quantitative analysis of Tombe and Zhu (2019) — migration inflows reduce local real wages because the downward wage pressure is not offset by an increase in varieties. Under a Melitz framework with monopolistic competition and endogenous firm entry — as in Ma and Tang (2020) — the inflow of workers induces firm entry, expands product variety, and lowers prices, yielding net local welfare gains for destination cities despite congestion. This modeling contingency has direct policy implications: if migration benefits destination cities, restrictive hukou policies impose a double loss on both migrants and natives; if it harms destination cities, local welfare may justify maintaining restrictions even at the cost of national efficiency.

## The Four Channels of Local Welfare Impact

Migration affects welfare in the receiving city through four distinct channels whose net balance determines whether inflows help or harm locals. The **wage channel** operates through standard labor-supply mechanics: an inflow of workers shifts the local labor supply curve outward, reducing the equilibrium nominal wage for a given labor demand curve. The magnitude of this effect depends on the elasticity of labor demand, which in turn depends on the production technology and the degree of openness to trade. The **congestion channel** captures the disutility and higher housing costs that accompany population density. In the Ma and Tang (2020) framework, congestion enters utility as an additive disutility term $C(L_j) = \rho (L_j / \bar{L})^\phi$, where $\phi$ governs how rapidly congestion disutility scales with city population. In the Tombe and Zhu (2019) framework, land functions as a fixed factor in production and housing, creating a similar congestion mechanism through rising land rents. The **variety channel** is the key offsetting force: a larger market supports more firms and product varieties. Under a CES utility structure with love of variety, an expansion of the set of available goods $\Omega_j$ directly lowers the ideal price index and raises real income. The **price channel** operates through scale effects on production: a larger labor force reduces marginal production costs, which, under imperfect competition, partially passes through to lower prices.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** *The four-channel decomposition is common across the QSE migration literature, but the weight assigned to each channel — particularly the variety channel — depends on modeling assumptions about market structure. Frameworks that suppress the variety channel ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]) find migration harmful locally; those that activate it ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]) find it beneficial.*

## Modeling Approach A: Eaton-Kortum with No Firm Entry

The Tombe and Zhu (2019) model assumes perfectly competitive goods markets with constant returns to scale, as in Eaton and Kortum (2002). In this structure, each location-sector produces a continuum of varieties under Fréchet-distributed productivity, and the equilibrium number of varieties in each location is fixed by the technological parameters — there is no endogenous firm entry or exit. Migration inflows affect local welfare through two channels: they depress nominal wages (wage channel) and increase the rental cost of land, which serves as a fixed factor in production and housing (congestion channel). There is no variety or price channel to offset these forces, because the set of available goods is technologically determined rather than market-size-dependent.

The model's predictions are unambiguous: destination provinces that receive migrants experience declines in real income per worker. The aggregate gains from migration — roughly 4.8 percent higher labor productivity and 11.1 percent higher welfare nationally — come entirely from the reallocation of workers from low-productivity agriculture to higher-productivity non-agriculture, not from any improvement in the productivity or variety of the destination economy. Within-province agriculture-to-non-agriculture reallocation accounts for 2.0 percent of the productivity gain, while between-province reallocation adds 2.8 percent ([[tombe-zhu-2019-trade-migration-china]]). The coastal destination provinces — Shanghai, Tianjin, Beijing, and Guangdong — that experience the largest population inflows simultaneously see declining real incomes, a pattern that would rationalize restrictive hukou policies on local welfare grounds.

## Modeling Approach B: Melitz with Endogenous Firm Entry

Ma and Tang (2020) build on the Melitz (2003) framework with heterogeneous firms, fixed entry costs, and endogenous firm entry and exit, estimated on 279 Chinese prefecture-level cities. The model also incorporates a detailed transportation network — road, rail, and waterway — estimated using Allen and Arkolakis's (2014) fast-marching method. In this structure, migration inflows generate two countervailing forces that are absent from the Eaton-Kortum framework. First, the inflow of workers increases local market size, raising expected profits for potential entrants and inducing more firms to enter. This expands the set of varieties available to local consumers and, because firms charge a constant markup over marginal cost, lowers the ideal price index proportionally to the elasticity of substitution $\varepsilon = 6$. Second, the inflow dampens nominal wages by expanding labor supply, which further reduces production costs and amplifies the price decline. These variety and price effects must be weighed against the congestion disutility that scales with population.

The quantitative results confirm that the variety channel dominates. Between 2000 and 2005, the 40 Chinese cities that received net population inflows — primarily large coastal cities and provincial capitals — experienced an average 4.7 percent increase in real income and 4.0 percent increase in welfare. Congestion offsets roughly 15 percent of the real income gain when expressed in welfare terms. When the model shuts down the firm entry margin by fixing the number of firms at their 2000 levels, the local impacts of migration turn negative — 44 of the 55 receiving cities experience lower real wages — thereby replicating the Eaton-Kortum prediction within the same framework ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). This sharp reversal confirms that the extensive margin of firm entry is the decisive mechanism.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *The empirical evidence supports the Melitz mechanism for Chinese cities. Ma and Tang (2020) find a positive and statistically significant relationship between city-level inflow rates and the growth rate of the number of firms using a model-based instrumental variables strategy. The IV estimates imply that a one-percentage-point increase in the inflow rate raises the growth rate of firms by approximately 1 percent, consistent with the structural mechanism.*

## Quantitative Comparison of the Two Frameworks

A direct comparison of the two frameworks within the same empirical context reveals that the modeling choice determines the sign of migration's local welfare effect but has relatively modest implications for aggregate welfare. In the Eaton-Kortum framework of Tombe and Zhu (2019), aggregate welfare rises by 11.1 percent from migration cost reductions, while in the Melitz framework of Ma and Tang (2020), the aggregate welfare gain from observed migration flows is 11.0 percent — very close in magnitude. The source of the gains, however, differs fundamentally. In the Eaton-Kortum model, the entire gain comes from labor reallocation across sectors and provinces; in the Melitz model, a substantial portion comes from the expansion of product variety in destination cities.

The difference in local predictions has first-order implications for policy. If the Eaton-Kortum model is the correct description, then individual provinces have a local incentive to restrict in-migration, and national welfare must be traded off against local interests. If the Melitz model is correct, destination cities gain from migration, and restrictive hukou policies are unambiguously harmful — they reduce both national efficiency and local welfare. Ma and Tang (2020) provide a direct test by noting that when they shut down the entry margin, the local impacts turn negative, suggesting that the data (which show positive correlations between inflow rates and per-capita GDP growth) favor the Melitz mechanism.

## Role of the Transportation Network in Shaping Local Impacts

The geographic location of cities on China's transportation network substantially moderates the welfare effects of migration. Ma and Tang (2020) are the first to bring the comprehensive Chinese transportation network — roads, railways, and waterways digitized from high-resolution atlas data — into a structural migration model, using the fast-marching method of Allen and Arkolakis (2014). The estimated geographic costs matrix reveals large variation in trade costs conditional on physical distance: city pairs 1,000 km apart can have trade costs ranging from 1.15 to 1.37 depending on network connectivity.

The transportation network creates a striking pattern in the distribution of migration's benefits. The top beneficiaries of internal migration are not the "superstar" cities themselves — Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen — but their neighboring cities, which enjoy productivity spillovers through inter-city trade while being spared the surge in congestion disutility that accompanies rapid population growth in the primary destination ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). Tianjin (near Beijing), Suzhou and Hangzhou (near Shanghai), and Foshan (near Guangzhou) all rank among the top welfare gainers from the observed migration flows, despite receiving relatively modest net inflows themselves. Strategically positioned inland cities such as Wuhan (at the intersection of the Yangtze River and the Beijing-Guangzhou railway) and Nanjing also benefit disproportionately through improved market access.

## City-Specific Migration Barriers and Their Effects

The largest Chinese cities maintain substantially higher migration barriers than the national average, which Ma and Tang (2020) estimate from the gap between model-implied and observed population inflows. Shanghai has migration barriers 24 percent higher than the national average, Guangzhou 13 percent higher, and Beijing 8 percent higher. These barriers reduce the national welfare by 0.7 to 3.9 percent, depending on the city. The barriers are identified from the fact that the observed population inflows into these cities are too small given their wage advantages, controlling for the estimated geographic and institutional migration costs.

Removing these city-specific barriers produces divergent local and national welfare effects. For Beijing and Guangzhou, complete removal of the additional barriers raises local welfare by 9.7 percent and 18.6 percent respectively, as the increase in real income dominates the surge in congestion. For Shanghai, however, complete removal of barriers — which would bring an estimated 6 million additional migrants — reduces local welfare by 8.3 percent because congestion costs overwhelm the real income gains ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). Shanghai is the exception that reveals the potential conflict between local and national welfare: the central government would prefer Shanghai to be substantially larger (population maximizing national welfare is roughly 22 million versus 17 million in 2005), but local residents would experience lower welfare at that population level.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** *Shanghai's status as the exception where local and national welfare conflict has important political economy implications. It suggests that a uniform national hukou liberalization policy may be locally optimal in most but not all large cities, and that Shanghai-specific complementary policies — such as housing supply expansion or congestion pricing — may be needed to align local and national interests before full barrier removal ([[wu-you-2025-urbanization]]).*

## Interaction with International Trade Liberalization

Internal migration and international trade liberalization interact to amplify aggregate welfare gains. Ma and Tang (2020) simulate a 10-percent reduction in international trade barriers and find that migration amplifies the gains from trade by roughly 55 percent. The mechanism operates through local labor markets: trade liberalization increases labor demand in coastal cities, pushing up wages. Without migration, rising wages throttle firm growth and limit the expansion of export-oriented production. With migration, the inflow of workers from the interior dampens the coastal wage increase, enabling further firm expansion and larger gains from trade. Under migration, the aggregate real income increase from the trade liberalization is 12.7 percent, compared to 8.2 percent without migration.

This amplification effect is specific to international trade. Internal trade liberalization and internal migration are substitutes rather than complements, because both serve to narrow spatial income differences — internal trade benefits smaller interior cities more than coastal ones, reducing the incentive to migrate ([[ma-tang-2020-geography-trade]]). The amplification also depends on firm entry: when both migration and firm entry are shut down, the gains from trade liberalization fall to 5.5 percent, a magnitude comparable to the standard gains from trade in models without spatial labor mobility.

## Frontier: QSE Policy Translation for China

The contrasting welfare predictions of the Eaton-Kortum and Melitz frameworks have direct implications for hukou reform policy design. If the Ma-Tang (Melitz) framework correctly describes Chinese cities — and the empirical evidence on firm entry supports this — then the policy conclusion is unambiguous: further relaxation of migration restrictions benefits both national efficiency and local welfare in almost all destination cities. The one exception (Shanghai) suggests that complementary housing and infrastructure policies, rather than continued migration restrictions, are the appropriate response to congestion.

The Ma-Tang framework has been extended by Li, Ma, and Tang (2024) to incorporate firm-level frictions (output and labor wedges), showing that migration alleviates the aggregate welfare impact of micro-level misallocation by allowing workers to move from heavily to lightly distorted regions. This interaction between migration and within-region distortions introduces a new policy consideration: reducing firm-level frictions (through SOE reform, financial development, or competition policy) and liberalizing migration are substitutes for raising aggregate welfare but have opposite effects on spatial inequality, with migration liberalization increasing it and friction reduction decreasing it.

Extensions incorporating dynamic migration with forward-looking firms, as in the dynamic QSE literature surveyed by Zhao, Chen, and Fu (2022), could further refine the welfare predictions by allowing firms to anticipate future population inflows when making entry decisions. The rapid expansion of digital infrastructure and the emergence of data as a non-rival production factor ([[lin-liu-zhou-2026-data-market]]) may also alter the local welfare calculus by reducing the fixed costs of firm entry and expanding the variety channel in smaller cities. These developments suggest that the welfare effects of migration in Chinese cities are not static but evolve with the technological and institutional environment.
