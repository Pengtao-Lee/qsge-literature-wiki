## A B S T R A C T

In a system of cities model that incorporates urban externalities, equilibrium city sizes deviate from optimal city sizes, which suggests a role for government intervention. Using a general equilibrium framework that incorporates agglomeration benefits and costs and that is calibrated to data from China between 2000 and 2020, we show that it is generally welfare-reducing to control city sizes through internal migration restrictions since the frictions they introduce outweigh the benefits of correcting for city size deviations. However, we also find that most large Chinese cities have already surpassed the optimal sizes from their local perspective, so without controlling city sizes, the average welfare of city residents would decrease. This suggests that a combination of policies is needed to both improve aggregate welfare and alleviate the distributional consequences for a rapidly urbanizing country.

## 1. Introduction

Developing countries are urbanizing rapidly. There are an estimated 2.5 billion people predicted to move into urban areas worldwide by 2050. In 2011, almost 70% of countries had policies in place to slow down the inflow of migrants into large cities (United Nations, 2015, 2017). In theory, due to both positive and negative externalities generated by the geographic concentration of a population, and because individuals usually ignore such externalities in their location choices, equilibrium city sizes can be either larger or smaller than optimal city sizes.<sup>1</sup> Therefore, the welfare effect of controlling city sizes is ambiguous. However, despite the prevalence of government interventions and the theoretical ambiguity in the effect, there has been little evidence so far on the welfare effect of restricting city sizes.

There are multiple challenges to answering this question, First, while policies to restrain urbanization are common, they are usually difficult to measure. Second, to estimate the effects of controlling city sizes on local welfare requires knowledge of the agglomeration force and the congestion force. Third, to accurately measure the aggregate productivity and welfare effects of controlling city sizes, we need a general equilibrium framework, as well as bilateral migration flow data at a reasonably fine geographic level. Such data are necessary for us to infer the counterfactual migration flows – hence the city sizes – and to take into account cross-region heterogeneity in unobservable labor productivity (Bryan and Morten, 2019). However, such data are rare in developing countries.

In this paper, we provide the first answer to the question of whether governments should control city sizes. We estimate a quantitative spatial model that is calibrated to data from China between 2000 and 2020, and we show that it is generally inefficient to control city sizes through internal migration restrictions. China is a natural context for conducting this study. From 1990 to 2020, over half a billion people moved from rural to urban areas in China. The sheer scale of this migration makes it an interesting and important context to study its effects. Moreover, crucially, China established its unique hukou system in the 1950s to contro internal migration better, and this system is the largest government intervention in internal migration in human history. Under the hukou system, every Chinese citizen is also a ‘‘citizen’’ of a locality. Local citizenship confers certain public benefits, such as better access to local public schools, health care, social security, and social benefits, for which noncitizens of that area are not eligible. Local governments can determine whom they wish to grant hukou status, as well as the public benefits associated with being a loca hukou holder. This system thus gives local governments in China a high degree of freedom to control city sizes. Last but not least, China has available data to quantitatively evaluate its welfare effect. We use restricted-use census microdata for the years 2000, 2005. 2010. and 2015. which allow us to track the migration flows between 340 prefectures over a critical period of urbanization

Our model features individuals with different skills and citizenships (i.e., their hukou city) with forward-looking relocation decisions, as in Caliendo et al. (2019) (CDP hereafter) and Caliendo et al. (2021)(COPS hereafter). There is a fixed number of sites that we call cities. In a given city, there is both an agglomeration force (productive externalities) and a congestion force. We model the agglomeration force as a productive externality that becomes stronger as the city’s population size becomes larger and the skill level of the population rises. We introduce an inelastic supply of land – where the elasticity decreases as the city exhausts the developable land within its jurisdiction – as the source of congestion. Land is used as an input in the production of housing, the latter of which enters final consumption.

The presence of agglomeration externalities and congestion forces yields an inverted-U-shaped relationship between average local welfare and population for each city. Cities differ in fundamental productivity and amenity. This site heterogeneity assumption, combined with the inverted-U-shaped curve for each city, yields important welfare implications (Albouy et al., 2019; Duranton and Puga, 2023). Individuals care about their own welfare they would derive in each city and ignore the externalities that they impose on incumbent residents in making their migration choices. A central planner, who takes into account the externalities and maximizes the total welfare of all residents, allocates individuals across sites to equate the net marginal returns to city size. Therefore, the equilibrium city sizes deviate from the socially(nationally) optimal sizes. The central planner would not let each city reach its own locally optimal size; this is because some sites are fundamentally more productive/amenable than other sites, so to maximize socia welfare, it would be optimal to place more people in the more productive/amenable sites, even though doing so implies that cities with better fundamentals surpass their locally optimal sizes. Furthermore, we also need to consider the fact that cities are able to control their sizes, as the Chinese cities do through the hukou policy. Suppose that cities control their sizes in a way that maximizes local average welfare, then such a policy can move the equilibrium sizes plausibly closer to their locally optimal sizes but not necessarily to the nationally optimal sizes.<sup>2</sup>

We introduce a local publicly-provided private good and model the hukou policy as a fiscal wedge (Chari et al., 2007-05; Sieg et al., 2023) between migrants and natives, which is treated as exogenous.<sup>3</sup> While the hukou policy changes equilibrium city sizes, it also introduces frictions on labor mobility. Therefore, the net effect of the hukou system combines the effect of (potentially) correcting for urban externalities and the effect of distorting mobility.

We estimate the agglomeration force by estimating the causal effect of city size on individuals’ wages. The approach we use is similar to Combes et al. (2015) and Chauvin et al. (2017), but we define city size in a way consistent with our data.<sup>4</sup> The way we model the congestion force translates the estimation task into a task of estimating the city-specific housing supply elasticity and land supply elasticity, for which we use the shift-share instruments (Card, 2001; Saiz, 2010; Diamond, 2016). To estimate the fiscal wedges created by the hukou system, we use a revealed-preference approach in the spirit of Rosen (1979) and Roback (1982). The key idea is that individuals’ location choices reveal the desirability of each location. We infer the value of a hukou by estimatin how individuals trade off local hukou status against income differences across localities.

Using this calibrated spatial equilibrium model, we estimate the effects of counterfactually changing the hukou system from 2000 onward. We derive three key results from this work. First, averaged across the years 2000–2020, the overall hukou policy in China created a GDP loss of 13.6% and a welfare loss of 4.7%. Not surprisingly, the increases in GDP and welfare would have come from the movement of populations from less productive cities to more productive ones, as well as from better matches between individuals’ idiosyncratic productivities and cities. A perhaps more surprising result is that, even though in theory, some locations must be overpopulated in equilibrium, it is generally welfare-reducing to restrict the population size of any city by imposing interna migration restrictions. We derive this result by examining the effect of counterfactually changing each ???????????????????? city’s hukou policy on ???????????????? welfare, and we find that the positive effects of greater labor mobility would almost always dominate the negative effects of allowing oversized cities to grow even bigger.

Second, we examine the welfare changes of the residents living in the most desirable cities. We look at three categories of residents: those who resided in the city in the previous period (called ‘‘natives’’ hereafter); migrants; and a ‘‘representative’’ resident whose welfare is what would be obtained by evenly redistributing the publicly-provided private goods and local land rents between migrants and natives. Supposing that internal migration restrictions were relaxed, we find that the average welfare of natives and that of the representative residents in the most desirable cities would have decreased while that of migrants would have increased. The main channel for the distributional effects between natives and migrants is crowding in local publicly-provided private goods. The decline in the welfare of the representative resident is another important result, which suggests the sizes of these desirable cities are larger than their locally optimal sizes. This result stems from urban land in the production of housing, which is inelastically supplied in the short run.

Taken together, the above results imply that governments should not attempt to control the degree of urbanization by setting migration barriers for the purpose of improving national welfare, but there may be a reason to control urbanization for the distributional effects caused by rapid urbanization. To achieve the goals of improving national welfare and reducing distributional effects, a combination of policies is needed. In the final part of this study, we consider several policies to achieve the dual goals. We show that increasing the price elasticity of urban land supply has the largest effect. However, increasing the urban land supply elasticity could exhaust developable land, especially in the most productive sites. Therefore, the scarcity of land acts as a fundamenta constraint in causing the trade-off between promoting or restraining urbanization.

This paper relates to a growing literature that uses quantitative models to estimate the impact of lowering migration barriers in China (Au and Henderson, 2006b; Bosker et al., 2012; Fan, 2019; Zi, 2021; Henderson et al., 2022; Ngai et al., 2019; Sieg et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024; Tombe and Zhu, 2019; Hsu and Ma, 2021; Hao et al., 2020; Gai et al., 2021; Ma and Tang, 2020; Cai et al., 2022; Imbert et al., 2023; Huang and Zhang, 2023). A common feature of these papers is that they all evaluate the effect of lowering migration barriers on some economic outcome(s). For example, Tombe and Zhu (2019) study how reductions in internal trade costs and migration costs contribute to the ?????????????????? labor productivity growth from 2000 to 2005 in China. Fan (2019) and Zi (2021) study the role of migration frictions in shaping the impact of trade liberalization. The key difference between our paper and this literature is the model setup and the research question. Our framework introduces agglomeration benefits and costs, and as a result, raises the question of whether governments should control urbanization, which links migration barriers to the canonical optima city size models. This is an unanswered question so far.<sup>5</sup> Secondly, in addition to aggregate effects, we also focus on the distributiona effects of changing migration policies. Thirdly, our paper carefully models the hukou system and estimates its parameters for each city. We develop a dynamic framework that builds on CDP and COPS and introduce a Markov process with hukou lottery draws to closely reflect how this system works. We then use a revealed-preference approach to quantify the intensive margin of the hukou restrictions, which is absent in existing literature. We believe our calibrated model provides an estimate of the effect of the hukou system that is closest to the true value.

This paper also adds to the literature on optimal city sizes in the presence of externalities (Mills and De Ferranti, 1971; Flatters et al., 1974; Henderson, 1974b; Arnott, 1979-01; Arnott and Stiglitz, 1979; Au and Henderson, 2006a; Henderson and Venables, 2009; Albouy et al., 2019; Fajgelbaum and Gaubert, 2020; Helsley and Strange, 2014; Duranton and Puga, 2023). A closely-related paper is Au and Henderson (2006a), who find that a large fraction of cities in China were undersized in 1997. Our work differs from theirs in two main aspects: First, while Au and Henderson (2006a) only consider the local optimality, we use a general equilibrium framework to evaluate both the local effect and the general equilibrium effect of controlling migration policies, which indirectly informs us of the local and national optimality of the actual city sizes. Second, we arrive at a different result: we find that, during the period 2000–2010, most of the largest Chinese cities were too small from the national perspective but too large from the loca perspective. One important reason for our differing finding from that of Au and Henderson (2006a) regarding the local optimality is that we consider local housing costs and the inelastic land supply when evaluating welfare.

The fact that among China’s largest cities, their nationally optimal sizes are greater than the actual city sizes, which, in turn, are greater than their locally optimal sizes, suggests a fundamental conflict between local and national interests. This result is close to that of Hsieh and Moretti (2019), who find that high-productivity cities in the U.S. have adopted stringent housing supply constraints, which limit the number of workers who can work there and lower aggregate productivity. Our work highlights the role of labor constraints imposed by high-productivity cities. In this regard, our paper sheds light on why large productivity gaps exist and persist between the rural/agricultural and urban/nonagricultural sectors across countries (Gollin et al., 2014; Lagakos, 2020; Gai et al., 2021)—that is, these gaps exist largely because the residents from high-productivity areas have strong incentives to create barriers to prevent internal migrants.

More broadly, our work is related to recent research on the general equilibrium effect of migration policies or removing migration barriers in various contexts, including but not limited to Caliendo et al. (2021) for the enlargement of the European Union, Bryan and Morten (2019) for Indonesia, Liu (2020), Monras (2020),Albert and Monras (2022), Albert et al. (2021) and Piyapromdee (2021) for immigration policies in the U.S., and Lagakos et al. (2023) for Bangladesh. This paper complements this literature by providing a rigorous empirical study of one of the most influential spatial policies in the world—the hukou system in China. Our theoretical framework also allows for the congestion and agglomeration forces in a more general formulation.

## 2. Institutional background

In the 1950s, the Chinese government formally instituted the hukou (household registration) system to control internal migration. Since then, each Chinese citizen has been assigned a hukou (city passport), typically in her county of birth. Before 1978, individuals were prohibited from living or working outside their hukou county. The mobility prohibition was relaxed in the 1980s but, before 1990, inter-county migration was still quite rare. In Fig. 1(a), we see that only 3% of Chinese lived outside their birth county in 1990.<sup>6</sup> As the industrialization proceeded and the private economy boomed in the 1990s, there was an increasing demand for labor in cities. Partly in response to this demand, and partly because it became increasingly difficult for the government to prohibit internal migration, migration control policies were greatly relaxed in this period.<sup>7</sup> The census data show that there was a dramatic change in the internal migration rate in the period 1990-2000: by 2000, the share of people who lived outside their birth county had increased to 15%. By 2010, this share had grown to 18%. These figures suggest that an additional 200 million people moved internally in China in 2000 compared to 1990.

The sudden relaxation of migration restrictions, in combination with regional income disparities – formed over the long history of the hukou system – caused significant variation in the migrant populations across cities. Fig. 1 plots the relationship between the migrant (born-elsewhere) share (in 1990 and 2000) and the GDP per capita (in 1990) for the 340 prefectures in China. We fix GDP per capita at the 1990 level to alleviate the concern about sorting. We see that the 1990 fitting line is nearly flat and very close to 0, suggesting that almost all the Chinese cities had a very small migrant population. In contrast, the upward-sloping fitting line for the year 2000 suggests that when people could travel much more freely in 2000, they tended to move to cities with a higher initial GDP per capita. The most populous cities saw extremely high migrant shares: all four of the tier-1 cities – Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen – had more than 40% of their populations born elsewhere.<sup>8</sup> Such high migrant shares for some cities suggest that the impacts of migrants on natives could be enormous. Therefore, giving cities some local control to mitigate these impacts is of critical importance.

In practice, Chinese cities were given considerable discretion in controlling their local populations and skill mixes after the domestic travel ban was removed around 2000. While hukou status no longer prevented people from traveling, it has continued to be associated with access to local public services. Migrants – people without a local hukou – are discriminated against in the sense that they only receive partial access to local public services, such as public schools, medical services, social security, and socia benefits. Such discrimination is economically important to households. Chen et al. (2019) estimate the market valuation of urban hukou in Jinan – a tier-2 city – to be approximately 90,000-126,000 RMB (\$13,314-\$18,639, or 1.5-2.1 times of GDP per capita) in 2017.

## 3.2. Fiscal policies

There are two sources of fiscal transfers between migrants and natives. The first source is the differential prices (user fees) of the publicly-provided education between migrants and natives, $P _ { g t } ^ { o d }$ , while both groups of people face the same tax rate. This is equivalent to the assumption that there is a fiscal wedge between migrants and natives, as in Sieg et al. (2023). We set

$$
P _ {g t} ^ {o d} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} P _ {g t} ^ {d}, & \mathrm{if} o \neq d \\ \lambda_ {t} ^ {d} P _ {g t} ^ {d}, & \mathrm{if} o = d \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

where $0 < \lambda _ { t } ^ { d } < 1$ reflects the price discount enjoyed by natives. This is a key parameter that we need to estimate.<sup>16</sup>

The rivalry nature of accessing education implies that the city-level consumption of the publicly-provided education is the aggregation of individual consumption, which is described in equation A2 in Online Appendix B.

Local governments finance publicly-provided education through residents’ (local hukou holders and migrants) nonprogressive income taxes (equivalent to commodity taxes), fiscal transfers from the central government, and collected user fees. The government budget constraint implies that the fiscal revenues equal the costs of production:

$$
G _ {g t} ^ {d} P _ {m t} ^ {d} = \sum_ {o} \sum_ {s} \left[ \chi^ {d} \tau (1 + R _ {s t} ^ {o d}) w _ {s t} ^ {d} \pi_ {s t} ^ {o d} \bar {L} _ {s t} ^ {o} e ^ {- \nu_ {s} \bar {\gamma}} \Gamma (1 - \nu_ {s}) (\pi_ {s t} ^ {o d}) ^ {- \nu_ {s}} + G _ {g t} ^ {d} P _ {s t} ^ {o d} \right] + \rho^ {d} T _ {t}\tag{7}
$$

$$
T _ {t} = \sum_ {o} \sum_ {d} \sum_ {s} (1 - \chi^ {d}) \tau (1 + R _ {s t} ^ {o d}) w _ {s t} ^ {d} \pi_ {s t} ^ {o d} \bar {L} _ {s t} ^ {o} e ^ {- \nu_ {s} \bar {\gamma}} \Gamma (1 - \nu_ {s}) (\pi_ {s t} ^ {o d}) ^ {- \nu_ {s}}\tag{8}
$$

where $P _ { m t } ^ { d }$ is the production costs or market price of one unit of educational service, so the left-hand side $G _ { g t } ^ { d } P _ { m t } ^ { d }$ represents the total costs of publicly-provided education. $\chi ^ { d }$ is the rate at which the local government in city ?? retains its local tax revenues, $G _ { g t } ^ { d } P _ { s t } ^ { o d }$ is the collected user fees, $T _ { t }$ is the total tax revenues that the central government collects at time $t ,$ and $\rho ^ { d }$ is the share of $T _ { t }$ that is transferred to city $^ { d , }$ where $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { d } \rho ^ { d } = 1 } \end{array}$ . The parameters $\tau , ~ \chi ^ { d }$ , and $\rho ^ { d }$ are all treated as exogenous. In a counterfactual exercise in Section 5.3, we consider alternative fiscal transfer rules that endogenize $\chi ^ { d }$ and $\rho ^ { d }$ .

The second source of ‘‘fiscal’’ transfers between migrants and natives is through the distribution of land income. The term $R _ { s t } ^ { o d }$ in Eq. (5) denotes the returns from the local fixed factor (land), which is specified as a fraction of an individual’s wage income, where the fraction is origin–destination-specific,

$$
R _ {s t} ^ {o d} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} R _ {t} ^ {d}, o = d \\ \mu_ {s} R _ {t} ^ {d}, o \neq d \end{array} \right.\tag{9}
$$

Here, $\mu _ { s } < 1$ means that migrants enjoy a lower share of the total local land income compared to natives, which captures the fact that the homeownership rate is lower among migrants. This share is skill-specific, reflecting that migrants' homeownership rate differs across skills. With this model specification, rising housing prices could potentially compensate natives as they are more likely to be home owners (Fang and Huang, 2022).

## 3.3. Production

Tradable Good. Each city represents a competitive labor market. In each city, perfectly competitive firms produce two goods: a freely tradable composite good, and a non-tradable good (housing). The tradable good serves as the numeraire. It is used in final consumption as well as used as an intermediate input in the production of housing. Housing is modeled as a flow of services, and it enters into final consumption.

Production of the tradable goods requires two types of labor. The low-skill labor and the high-skill labor are imperfect substitutes in production, in the sense:

$$
q _ {t} ^ {d} = \bar {A} _ {t} ^ {d} (L _ {H t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {H}} (L _ {L t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {L}} [ \xi^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} (l _ {H, t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} + (1 - \xi) ^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} (l _ {L, t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} ] ^ {\frac {\sigma}{\sigma - 1}}
$$

where $q _ { t } ^ { d }$ is the firm-level output of the tradable good, $L _ { s t } ^ { d } , s \in \{ H , L \}$ is the resident population of skill ?? in city $d , l _ { s , t } ^ { d }$ is the firmlevel labor input, the parameter ?? corresponds to the elasticity of substitution between the two labor inputs in the firms’ production technology, and the parameter ?? determines the weight of high-skill labor in production.

The city-specific productivity level, $\bar { A } _ { t } ^ { d } ( L _ { H t } ^ { d } ) ^ { \phi _ { H } } ( L _ { L t } ^ { d } ) ^ { \phi _ { L } }$ , has two components: The first component is a city-specific fundamenta productivity level, $\bar { A } _ { t } ^ { d }$ . In addition, there is an endogenous productivity component that increases with the city’s population and the share of high-skilled workers (reflected by $\phi _ { H } ~ > ~ \phi _ { L } )$ , which captures the idea that there are benefits from having firms and workers be located next to each other (agglomeration effects). We assume that high-skilled workers contribute more to the agglomeration effects since there is suggestive evidence of this from the U.S. (Diamond, 2016), and also because it is consistent with the casual observation that there is fierce competition among Chinese cities to attract talent by offering subsidies.<sup>17</sup> Parameter $\phi _ { s }$ is the elasticity of the agglomeration effects to the skill-specific city population. The term $( L _ { H t } ^ { d } ) ^ { \phi _ { H } } ( L _ { L t } ^ { d } ) ^ { \phi _ { L } }$ is external to firms production decisions. It is worth mentioning that, while our definition of $L _ { s t } ^ { d }$ is the city resident population within the administrative boundary, to calculate the agglomeration effects here, we use the (population-weighted) average urban agglomeration size within the administrative boundary to measure $L _ { t } ^ { d }$ (see Online Appendix D and Figure A2 for details). We do so because the Chinese prefectures do not correspond exactly to commuting zones.

Since the production function features constant returns to scale at the firm level, the city-level production function is given by

$$
Q _ {t} ^ {d} = \bar {A} _ {t} ^ {d} (L _ {H t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {H}} (L _ {L t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {L}} [ \xi^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} (\tilde {L} _ {H t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} + (1 - \xi) ^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} (\tilde {L} _ {L t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} ] ^ {\frac {\sigma}{\sigma - 1}}\tag{10}
$$

where $Q _ { t } ^ { d }$ is the city-level output, $\tilde { L } _ { H t } ^ { d }$ and $\tilde { L } _ { L t } ^ { d }$ represent the city-level effective high-skill labor and low-skill labor, respectively, which are defined by

$$
\tilde {L} _ {s t} ^ {d} = \sum_ {o} \pi_ {s t} ^ {o d} \bar {L} _ {s t} ^ {o} e ^ {- \nu_ {s} \bar {\gamma}} \Gamma (1 - \nu_ {s}) (\pi_ {s t} ^ {o d}) ^ {- \nu_ {s}}.\tag{11}
$$

Given the production technology, solving the representative firm’s profit-maximization problem and aggregating to the city-leve labor demand function yield

$$
w _ {s t} ^ {d} = [ \bar {A} _ {t} ^ {d} (L _ {H t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {H}} (L _ {L t} ^ {d}) ^ {\phi_ {L}} ] ^ {\frac {\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} (Q _ {t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} \xi_ {s} ^ {\frac {1}{\sigma}} (\tilde {L} _ {s t} ^ {d}) ^ {- \frac {1}{\sigma}}\tag{12}
$$

Housing. Next, we turn to describe the production of housing. Houses are constructed by atomistic real estate developers in competitive markets. The firm-level housing production technology combines an intermediate good (materials) and an inelastically-supplied factor—?????????? land—as inputs according to the Cobb–Douglas technology:

$$
h = \bar {A} _ {h t} ^ {d} (f) ^ {\omega^ {d}} (m) ^ {1 - \omega^ {d}}
$$

where $\hat { A } _ { h t } ^ { d }$ is the fundamental productivity in the housing sector in city ??, ?? is the firm-level input of land, and ?? is the firm-leve input of materials, which come from the tradable good. $\omega ^ { d }$ governs the share of land in the production of housing, as well as the elasticity of the supply of housing, which varies across cities. Similarly, we can solve the representative firm’s profit-maximization problem, derive the firm’s optimal input decisions, and then derive the upward-sloping city-level housing supply function,

$$
H _ {t} ^ {d} = (\bar {A} _ {h t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {1}{\omega^ {d}}} \frac {1}{\omega^ {d}} (P _ {t} ^ {d}) ^ {\frac {1 - \omega^ {d}}{\omega^ {d}}} F _ {t} ^ {d},\tag{13}
$$

where $H _ { t } ^ { d }$ is the city-level supply of housing, $F _ { t } ^ { d }$ is the city-level supply of ?????????? land, which is controlled by local governments according to

$$
\frac {F _ {t} ^ {d}}{\bar {F} _ {m a x} ^ {d}} = 1 - \left(\frac {x ^ {d}}{P _ {t} ^ {d}}\right) ^ {\frac {1}{\zeta}}, 0 <   x ^ {d} <   P _ {t} ^ {d}, \zeta > 0.\tag{14}
$$

Here, $\hat { F } _ { m a x } ^ { d }$ is the maximum land area available for city development within the administrative boundary of city ??, and $\frac { F _ { t } ^ { d } } { \bar { F } _ { m a x } ^ { d } }$ is the share of developed (urban) land among all developable land in city ?? at time $t . ~ x ^ { d } ~ > ~ 0$ is a city-specific parameter, which can be interpreted as the minimum land conversion cost. The higher this value is, the more difficult it is to convert other land uses into urban land use, hence leading to a lower developed land share. We assume that each city has a continuum of sites, and the conversion costs of these sites for urban land use follow a Pareto distribution of $\begin{array} { r } { F ( c ) = 1 - \left( \frac { x ^ { d } } { c } \right) ^ { \frac { 1 } { \zeta } } , c > x ^ { d } } \end{array}$ . Local governments convert the sites for urban use as long as the local housing price index is greater than the conversion costs. This will deliver the city-leve land supply function (14) (Yu, 2022).

Eq. (14) has two important properties. First, under this functional form, the price elasticity of the urban land supply is positive. Second, this elasticity begins to be infinitely large when the developed land fraction is tiny, and it approaches zero when either the developed land area approaches the maximum of developable land in the area or the local housing price index approaches infinity.<sup>18</sup> Therefore, this implies that when land is abundant, the production technology of housing is nearly constant returns to scale, but when the land is exhausted, the production of housing becomes decreasing returns to scale, and housing prices will become very high when the city size grows large. This source of congestion, combined with the agglomeration force in the production of the tradable good, yields an inverted-U-shaped relationship between average local welfare and city size (See Online Appendix B for a discussion of its relation to the literature).

Finally, we assume that city governments own the land, rent it to local firms, collect all the rents, and then rebate the rents to every local resident (including natives and migrants) according to rule defined by Eqs. (9) and (15):<sup>19</sup>

$$
\omega^ {d} P _ {t} ^ {d} H _ {t} ^ {d} = \sum_ {o} \sum_ {s} R _ {s t} ^ {o d} w _ {s t} ^ {d} \pi_ {s t} ^ {o d} \bar {L} _ {s t} ^ {o} e ^ {- \nu_ {s} \bar {\gamma}} \Gamma (1 - \nu_ {s}) (\pi_ {s t} ^ {o d}) ^ {- \nu_ {s}}\tag{15}
$$

The left-hand side of Eq. (15) is the income from land, and the right-hand side suggests that this income is redistributed to every resident in proportion to each resident’s labor income. The term $R _ { s t } ^ { o d }$ implies this redistribution favors natives according to Eq. (9).

## 3.4. Equilibrium

The endogenous state in each time period is given by the distribution of the population across all cities, $\{ L _ { s t } ^ { d } \} , \{ \bar { L } _ { s t } ^ { d } \}$ . The time-varying fundamentals of the economy are the city-specific productivities in tradable goods and in housing, $\{ \bar { A } _ { t } ^ { d } \} , \{ \bar { A } _ { h t } ^ { d } \} ;$ the city-skill-specific amenity levels, $\{ \bar { B } _ { s t } ^ { d } \} ;$ the bilateral migration costs, $\{ d _ { s t } ^ { o d } \}$ ; and the group-specific population net growth rates, $\{ \eta _ { s t } ^ { d } \}$ }. The time-invariant fundamentals are the fiscal transfer rules, $\{ \tau \} , \ \{ \chi ^ { \ddot { d } } \} , \ \{ \rho ^ { d } \} ;$ ; the city-specific maximum developable land areas, $\{ \hat { F } _ { m a x } ^ { d } \} ;$ ; and the fixed costs of migration, $\{ f _ { s } ^ { o d } \}$ . ## 4. Data and estimation

We are interested in the economic variables under the counterfactual migration policies. An important departure from previous papers is that we choose not to model migration policies as changing the iceberg-form migration costs, i.e., $\{ d _ { s t } ^ { o d } \}$ (e.g., COPS, 2021). Instead, we consider that city governments can change the probabilities of granting hukou $\{ \theta _ { s t } ^ { d } \}$ } and can change the fiscal wedges $\{ \lambda _ { t } ^ { d } \}$ to control the population, which is essentially a form of resource transfer between natives and migrants.

We use the exact-hat algebra (Dekle et al. 2007) to estimate the effects of counterfactually changing internal migration policies in China.<sup>20</sup> We use superscript ′ to indicate the variables that are counterfactual, and use $\hat { x } = x ^ { \prime } / x$ to denote the changes relative to the actual values. We then express the equilibrium conditions in counterfactual changes in equations (A10)- (A23) in Online Appendix C.2. In Section 4.1, we describe the data used to construct each variable. In Section 4.2, we describe the calibration and estimation of the parameters. In Online Appendix C.3, we describe the algorithm that implements the hat algebra.

## 4.1. Data sources

We assemble a rich dataset of Chinese cities for this quantitative investigation. The data cover the demographics, economic statistics, migration patterns, geographic features, land use patterns, and hukou policies for 340 Chinese prefectural cities between 2000 and 2020, with 2000–2010 being the core period of the study. The 2020 data are used for testing the out-of-sample prediction of our model.

The first key data variable required by our methodology is bilateral migration flows $( \pi _ { s , t } ^ { o d } )$ . Our interest in cities requires that this information is on a reasonably fine geographic level. Additionally, the gravity structure of the model implies that the data must be geographically representative. The data set that we use is the restricted-use random samples of the Population Census microdata for the years 2000, 2005 (mini-census), 2010, and 2015 (mini-census). The sample sizes range from 1.2 million observations in 2000 (0.1%) to 4.6 million observations in 2010(0.33%). A critical feature of China’s census microdata is that it records the origins of migration (hukou place) at the sub-provincial (prefecture) level, which is rare for developing countries (Bryan and Morten, 2019). We construct prefecture-to-prefecture (340 × 340) migratory flows by skill group for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2015. For the prefectural (both hukou and resident) populations, we use the census aggregate data from the years 2000, 2010, and 2020. We use the aggregate data instead of aggregating the information from the census microdata to ensure the precision of the measurement.

Another key set of data variables is urban land area $( F _ { t } ^ { d } )$ as well as the determinants of the housing and land supply elasticities. We use the impervious surface areas constructed by Gong et al. (2020), which are available for all of China at a resolution of 30 meters for the years between 1978 and 2017, to measure the urban land areas during our study period.<sup>21</sup> We compute the impervious surface areas for each prefecture using ArcGIS for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2017. To measure the developable land area $( \hat { F } _ { m a x } ^ { d } )$ , we use the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (MODIS) data for the year 2001 (Wang et al. 2015),<sup>22</sup> and classify the land use types of ‘‘Forest’’, ‘‘Wetland’’, ‘‘Water’’, and ‘‘Snow/Ice’’ as undevelopable. In addition, as shown in Saiz (2010), the slope of the terrain is a key determinant of housing supply elasticity. Following a similar method as Saiz (2010), we use the Digita Elevation Model at a resolution of 90 meters<sup>23</sup> and ArcGIS to calculate the pixel-level slopes. We then use the pixel-level slopes to construct a measure of terrain ruggedness in the periphery of the urban core areas of each prefecture.<sup>24</sup> This measure then serves as the main determinant of housing supply elasticity.

In addition, we construct prefectural-level GDP and for the vears 2000, 2005. 2010. 2015, and 2020 and housing costs $( P _ { t } ^ { d } )$ for the years 2000 and 2010. We provide the construction details in Online Appendix D. We also obtain data on fiscal transfers from the 2007 Fiscal Statistical Compendium for All Prefectures and Counties (Quanguo Dishixian Caizheng TongjiZiliao). We use these data to infer the parameters $\chi ^ { d }$ and $\rho ^ { d }$ in the model. All the price data, including GDP, fiscal revenues, housing prices, are normalized to the 2000 level according to the CPI provided by the National Bureau of Statistics of China.

## 5. Results

In this section, we quantify the effects of changing the hukou system relative to the existing system. In all counterfactual scenarios, we assume that agents fully expect the future series of hukou policies. We also assume that the hukou population in each city in 2000 was held constant at the original level but that all other endogenous variables would have changed. First, in Sections 5.1 and 5.2, we consider the extreme scenario where the hukou system was removed entirely in the year 2000. In Section 5.1, we examine the aggregate effects of this counterfactual policy change, and in Section 5.2, we consider the distributional effects of this counterfactual policy change between migrants and natives, as well as discuss the mechanisms behind the distributional effects. Then, in Section 5.3, we examine the impacts of gradual changes in migration policy in individual cities on both the local and national welfare. We conduct this last exercise to test the theoretical possibility of improving the national welfare by restricting the sizes of certain cities. As we show below, it is generally welfare-reducing to restrict the size of any city. Moreover, there is an acute tension between the locally optimal city sizes and the nationally optimal sizes for China’s largest cities. Finally, we consider four policies that could potentially relieve this tension.

To isolate the effects of each channel, we hold each of these four components constant in turn and let the other three components change as the model predicts. We then calculate the resulting welfare changes, respectively.

Here, we see that the declines in welfare would be greater, which suggests that local hukou holders benefit from the uneven redistribution of the land rent income. Finally, the fifth pair of bins illustrates the effects of shutting down the wage channel. The declines here would also be greater than the benchmark case, suggesting that this channel would compensate the natives.

To summarize, our decomposition exercises show that changes in access to local publicly-provided private goods are the most critical channel shaping the distributional effects of removing migration restrictions between migrants and natives. The reason why this channel is particularly important is that the use of private goods is rival in nature. Attracting migrants necessitates offering more public resources to migrants.

## 5.3. Locally and nationally optimal city sizes

In Section 5.2, we documented that natives in the most desirable cities would suffer welfare losses if the existing migration restrictions were removed. However, this result does not imply the local optimality of the observed city sizes because ‘‘local welfare’ comprises the joint welfare of both migrants and natives. In theory, if the equilibrium city size is on the downward-sloping portion of the inverted-U-shaped curve between city population and welfare, a local government could restrict the city size and arrange transfers between migrants and natives to increase the average local welfare.<sup>31</sup> Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. A.1 in Appendix A, if a city passes its nationally optimal size (to the right of $B _ { 1 }$ for city 1), it might even generate national welfare gains from restricting its size. However, it should be noted that the imposition of migration restrictions has two effects: the first is that it affects city size, which has an ambiguous effect, depending on the direction of deviation from its nationally optimal size; and the second is that it introduces labor mobility frictions, which unambiguously reduces the national welfare. We empirically estimate the net effect.

In summary, our results reveal a fundamental tension in the allocation of a population within a country; that is, for cities with better fundamentals, it will be nationally beneficial to remove their migration barriers. However, most of these cities’ current sizes have surpassed their locally optimal levels, so relaxing their migration barriers would worsen their local welfare.

It is a question of great significance to resolve the conflict between the local and national interests in terms of desired population sizes, particularly for a rapidly urbanizing country. We consider four policy ideas that could potentially resolve the conflict of interest. The first policy is a fiscal transfer arrangement that conditions the transfers from the central government to loca governments on the number of migrants a local jurisdiction has received. The idea is that, given that the observed population sizes for the most productive cities were locally too large but nationally too small, this plan would incentivize the most productive cities to accommodate larger populations. The second policy idea is to increase the market supply of private goods such as education. As the decomposition exercise in Section 5.2 shows, the most important channel for the distributional effect is crowding in publicly provided private goods. so a natural solution would be to encourage the supply of substitutes, i.e., market-provided private goods The third policy idea is to increase the elasticity of urban land supply to housing prices, which would reduce the pressure of rising home prices from immigration. Lastly, the fourth policy idea is to apply the combination of all these three policies.<sup>33</sup>

## 6. Conclusions

In this paper, we sought to answer the question of whether governments should try to increase or decrease the degree of urbanization. Theories on optimal city sizes suggest a divergence between market equilibrium city size and optimal city size, so there is a role for government intervention. We focused our work on China, which prohibited internal migration for three decades (from the 1950s through the 1980s) using the hukou system, after which it started to gradually relax the migration restrictions to control the pace of urbanization. We used a quantitative spatial model to study the effect of this system, primarily focusing on the counterfactual scenario where China suddenly removed the hukou system in the year 2000. In this counterfactual scenario, we found both large aggregate GDP gains and substantial welfare gains. However, this would also lead to significant welfare losses for residents living in the largest cities of China. Therefore, the hukou system is a balance between aggregate efficiency and the distributional consequences between residents of China’s biggest cities and the rest of the country.

We also performed counterfactual analyses to study the effect of relaxing hukou policies in individual cities. We showed that while, theoretically, it is possible to improve the national welfare by controlling the sizes of overpopulated cities, it is generally not a good idea to do so by creating migration barriers since such barriers introduce additional frictions that outweigh the benefits of correcting for city size deviations. To achieve the dual goals of improving aggregate welfare and alleviating distributional consequences, especially for a rapidly urbanizing country, a combination of policies is needed. This study demonstrated that conditional inter-region transfers, increasing the elasticity of land supply, and encouraging the supply of substitutes for publicly-provided private goods are all feasible solutions.

Finally, a more fundamental solution to this problem – one that we have not yet discussed – is to create new cities or new city subcenters in order to attract people from a country’s most congested locations. In reality, China is in the process of implementing a massive plan to turn itself into a country of 19 ‘‘super-regions’’ (The Economist, 2018).<sup>36</sup> However, our theoretical framework could not evaluate the impact of such a policy because the number of cities, as well as the boundaries of the cities, are treated as exogenous. China’s plan is similar to the idea of city corporations discussed by Henderson (1974b) in the sense that the Chinese government is grasping the idea of agglomeration benefits and costs and is making plans to improve the efficiency of the system of cities. We believe this to be a promising subject for future research.

## Appendix A. The relationship between equilibrium, socially optimal, and locally optimal city sizes

We use Fig. A.1 to illustrate the relationship between the equilibrium city size under free migration, the socially optimal city size, and the locally optimal city size, as well as the welfare implications of imposing internal migration restrictions. Consider a country with two cities and homogeneous labor. Distance from the left vertical axis represents the population in city 1, while the distance from the right vertical axis represents the population in city 2. The national population is fixed and indicated by the length of the horizontal axis. For each city, there are two curves: the average (net) benefits curve (in solid). and the social marginal (net) benefits curve (in dash). The social marginal benefit is the net benefit that the marginal migrant derives plus the externalities (in utility units) that she imposes on incumbent residents. The social planner is to maximize the total welfare of all residents – or equivalently – the welfare of a representative resident because the national population is fixed. The benefits a resident receives are equal to the average, rather than the marginal benefit associated with that city. The average benefits curve has an inverted-U shape because there are both positive urban externalities and negative ones. Before crossing the locally optimal size, the positive externalities dominate negative ones, while after crossing the locally optimal size, the negative externalities come to dominate positive ones. Therefore, the social marginal benefit curve crosses the average benefits curve at the peak of the average benefits curve and falls below the average benefits curve after crossing the peak.

Now we consider the relationship between the locally optimal sizes, the socially optimal sizes, and the equilibrium city sizes under free migration. As pointed out by Albouy et al. (2019), the key factor driving the difference between the locally and socially optimal sizes is site heterogeneity. To reflect site heterogeneity, we let the average benefits curve of city 1 be above the average benefits curve of city 2 over the majority of the population range. City 1 can be interpreted as the more productive location of the country, while city 2 represents the rest of the country. In the presence of site heterogeneity. having each city reach its locally optimal sizes – indicated by $A _ { 1 }$ and $A _ { 2 } - \mathrm { i } s$ not a social optimum. To maximize the total benefits of all residents (city 1 and city 2 residents together), the social planner would continue to place more residents in city 1 – which is fundamentally more productive – until the social marginal benefits curve 1 crosses the social marginal benefits curve 2. The populations of the two cities under the social optimum are indicated by $B _ { 1 }$ and $B _ { 2 } ,$ , respectively. On the other hand, in the market equilibrium where there is no restriction on migration, the population would move between the two cities until their average benefits are equalized. In this specific example, since the average benefits curve of city 1 is much higher than that of city $^ { 2 , }$ such a process will continue until the entire population is attracted by city 1. This allocation of the population is indicated by $C _ { 1 }$ and $C _ { 2 }$ in the figure.
